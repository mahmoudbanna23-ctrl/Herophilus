MOVEMENT DISORDERS
• Movement disorders are
  defined as neurological
  syndromes in which there is
  either an excess of
  movement or a lack of
  voluntary and automatic
  movements unrelated to
  weakness or spasticity.
             CLASSIFICATION OF MOVEMENT DISORDERS

• Movement disorders are conventionally divided into 2 major categories:

•    1) Hyperkinetic movement disorders (also called dyskinesias) refers to excessive, often repetitive,
    involuntary movements that intrude into the normal flow of motor activity.

• This category includes     chorea, dystonia, myoclonus, stereotypies, tics, and tremor.

• 2) Hypokinetic movement disorders refers to

•    akinesia (lack of spontaneous movement) (e.g. in facial expression) or associated movement (e.g. arm
    swing during walking).

•   hypokinesia (reduced amplitude of movement) the movements are also smaller than desired, as in the
    micrographia of patients' handwriting,

• bradykinesia (slow movement), and rigidity.

• Parkinsonism is the primary hypokinetic movement disorder.
                         ANATOMY OF THE BASAL GANGLIA

  group of nuclear masses and fibers.
• A) Nuclear Masses
1.In the cerebral hemisphere:
• The corpora striata consist of a caudate nucleus
  and a reticular nucleus, the latter divided into the
  putamen and the globus pallidus.
2.In the diencephalon:
• The subthalamic nuclei
3.In the pons and medulla:
• The vestibular nucleus and the reticular nucleus
• B) Fibers
• 1. Short fibers:These connect
  the nuclear masses to each
  other and other parts of the
  brain.
• 2. Long fibers:These form the
  descending pathways (the
  rubrospinal, vestibulospinal,
  and reticulospinal tracts) that
  terminate around the lower
  motor neurons.

PARKINSON'S DISEASE


• Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a complex neurodegenerative disease with
  progressive loss of dopamine neurons.


• It is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder of the
  elderly, presenting primarily with symptoms of motor impairment.


• PD patients usually manifest a series of motor and non-motor
  symptoms.

   HOW TO TREAT THIS PATIENT?


• There are currently no disease-modifying
  treatments for PD


• Medical Management Is Predominantly
  Focused On Controlling The Motor Symptoms
  using drugs.
TREATMENT PREDOMINANTLY FOCUSES ON SYMPTOMATIC RELIEF WITH
DRUGS AIMING TO EITHER RESTORE THE LEVEL OF DOPAMINE IN THE
STRIATUM OR TO ACT ON STRIATAL POST-SYNAPTIC DOPAMINE RECEPTORS.




  Dopamine is incapable of crossing the blood–brain barrier (BBB), and it must be produced within the central nervous system (CNS) in order to act in the striatum In contrast,
  the dopamine precursor levodopa is able to cross the BBB and can be administered as a therapy

       FIRST LINE LEVODOPA TREATMENT
• levodopa, combined with a peripheral decarboxylase inhibitor, has been regarded as the gold standard for
  the treatment of PD. (most frequently prescribed combination drugs are carbidopa/levodopa (co-careldopa [trade names Sinemet,
  Pharmacopa, Atamet]) and benserazide/levodopa (co-beneldopa [trade name Madopar])

• the gold standard for the treatment of PD. Better motor improvement in short term, reduce freezing of
  gait
• . Long-term levodopa therapy frequently leads to disabling side effects.
• Levodopa-induced dyskinesias develop at an average rate of 10% per annum after commencing levodopa,
  although this figure is higher in younger onset patients , are predominantly due to duration of levodopa
  treatment The development of drug-induced dyskinesias in PD seems to be associated with intermittent
  stimulation of dopamine receptors.
• Motor fluctuations are most strongly related to disease duration and dose of levodopa exposure,
• Levodopa has a short half-life of 60 –90 min, and pulsatile levodopa supply to a denervated striatum seems
  to be an important aetiological factor.
           FIRST LINE DOPAMINE AGONIST TREATMENT
• There are six orally acting dopamine agonists available in the UK. Four are ergot derivatives: bromocriptine, pergolide, cabergoline and
  lisuride; and two non-ergot drugs: ropinirole and pramipexole.
• Less dyskinesia but more adverse effects (nausea , postural hypotension, somnolence
• These drugs all work by stimulation of the post-synaptic dopamine receptors. The dopamine agonists were initially licensed for use in
  conjunction with levodopa in patients with advanced PD.
• Their introduction as first-line agents came about as a result of their efficacy in improving motor symptoms in addition to their ability
  to delay the introduction of levodopa and the subsequent development of levodopa complications
• Commonly used non-ergot-derived dopamine agonists include ropinirole, pramipexole and rotigotine. An uncommon, but important,
  side effect most frequently reported to date with pramipexole is an increased risk of pathological gambling.


•   There have been few head-to-head comparative studies between the agonists. Cabergoline is an ergot derivative with a high affini ty for the D2 and D3 receptors. However, there have been
    increasing reports of non-inflammatory fibrotic degeneration of cardiac valves with the ergot agonists, specifically cabergoline and pergolide, and for this reason, they are no longer
    recommended as first-line treatments. Regular monitoring including ESR, chest X -ray and 6-monthly echocardiography are recommended for those continuing on ergot-derived agonists     .

                           TREMORS

• A tremor is an involuntary,
  rhythmic, oscillatory
  movement of a body part
  produced by alternating or
  synchronous contractions of
  antagonistic muscles.
• It is the most common form
  of involuntary movement.

                       ESSENTIAL TREMOR

• Essential tremor is more common in people aged 40 and over.
• Clinical Presentation
• Start gradually.
• It affects the hands and arms.
• It can also affect the head and voice.
• The tremor is typically postural (occurring with voluntary maintenance
  of a position against gravity) and kinetic (occurring during voluntary
  movement; it usually resolves when the body part relaxes).

CHOREA


• Choreic movements are abrupt,
  jerky, and quite irregular (in
  terms of form, location, and
  time interval between two
  successive movements).
           COMMON CAUSES OF CHOREA:

• A- Hereditary: Huntington's disease
• B- Acquired
• a) Sydenham's chorea: rheumatic chorea
• b) Drug-induced chorea: (Levodopa, lithium)
• c) Metabolic disorders:Wilson's disease
• d) Post-kernicterus
• e) Post-anoxia, hemorrhage (newborns)
• f) Vasculitis: collagenous systemic lupus erythematosus [SLE]
     RHEUMATIC CHOREA [SYDENHAM'S CHOREA]


• Sydenham's chorea is the most common cause of
  acquired chorea in young people.


• Affects children and adolescents between 5 and 15
  years of age, with females more often than males
  [ratio 2:1].


• A complication of a previous group A hemolytic
  streptococcal infection.
• The onset is acute or insidious.
• Usually self-limited; resolves within a
  few weeks or months (3-6 months)
• Chorea is a major manifestation of
  acute rheumatic fever.
• Chorea may be the first and only
  manifestation of rheumatic fever.
• However, some patients may have
  subtle evidence of carditis on
  echocardiography despite a normal
  clinical examination and ECG.
                            CLINICAL IMAGE

• Sudden and fairly irregular jerky movements
• They involve the extremities, face, and trunk muscles.
• May affect the tongue
• They occur at rest, are increased by emotional stress
  and voluntary activities, and disappear during sleep.
• Tone: Decreased with the manifestation of hypotonia.
• Boat-shaped or scaphoid hand: When the hand
  extends beyond the arms, there is flexion at the wrist
  and overextension at the metacarpophalangeal and
  interphalangeal joints with the spread of the fingers
• Pronator sign: As the patient elevates and supinates their arm, they deviate
  downward and laterally and become pronate.
• Emotional lability
• Variable reflexes, knee swing reflex
• Sensation intact
• Gait → frequent abnormal falls
• Evidence of cardiac involvement (30% of cases)

DIAGNOSTIC SIGNS

• 1) Onset in early middle age
• 2) Positive family history
• 3) Progressive choreic movements
• 4) Progressive dementia
• Diagnosis:
• a- Genetic testing
• b- MRI of the brain → cortex and caudate atrophy
• Treatment:
• Dopamine antagonist (e.g., haloperidol)
• Dopamine-depleting agent (tetrabenazine)

                                     DYSTONIA

• Dystonic movements are sustained contractions
  that are twisting in nature and usually increase
  with activity.
• Types
• A. Generalized: dystonia
• B. Segmental
• a) Neck: spastic torticollis
• b) Arms: writer's cramp
• c) Jaw: oromandibular dystonia
• d) Eye: blepharospasm
• e) Vocal cord: spastic dysphonia
                             TREATMENT

• 1) Medical: Anticholinergics - Benzodiazepine - Baclofen
• 2) Local injection of botulinum toxin into the affected muscles.
• 3) Deep brain stimulation: Uses implanted wires placed unilaterally or
  bilaterally in target areas such as the thalamus, subthalamic nucleus, or globus
  pallidus
                                            ATHETOSIS


• These are slow, twisting movements (mobile
  spasms). In the limbs, they are more prominent in
  the distal parts. One or both sides of the body may
  be involved.
• Muscle tone is increased, with a variable degree of
  rigidity.
• Causes
• a) Congenital: congenital diplegia
• b) Acquired
• a. Kernicterus
• b. Sequelae of an anoxic state(CO poisoning)
• c. After encephalitis
                                             TICS

• Tics are rapid, coordinated repetitive movements.
• Tics involve the same group of muscles, such as
  blinking, twisting the neck, shrugging the shoulders,
  or clearing the throat.
• Tics are the only involuntary movements that can
  be stopped by the patient for a short period of time.
• Gilles De La Tourette’s syndrome:
• These are severe tics characterized by chronic,
  lifelong multiple motor and vocal tics. They begin
  between the ages of 2 and 13 years, and are more
  common in males.
