                Dementia
• The term “dementia” is of Latin origin and
  means ‘devoid of mind’. It is used to describe
  a persistent state of serious cognitive,
  functional and emotional deterioration from a
  previously higher level of functioning old. It
  should not be confused with mental
  retardation which denotes deficient cognitive
  development before age of 18 years.
       Memory decline in dementia

▪ Initially affects learning of new information:
▪   Who they spoke to on the telephone earlier that day
▪   What one had for breakfast that day
▪ As disease progresses ,patient is unable to recall previously learned inform
  ation
▪ also:
          ▪   Names of famous people
          ▪   Local geogragh
▪ In advanced stages, patient may be unable to recognize even close relative
  s,and in final may not be able to say who they themselves are
    Decline in other cognitive abilities

▪    Initially ,unable to do relatively complicated task they could previously:
▪    Cooking different items simultaneously
▪    Financial transactions involving filling forms
▪    As illness progress ,they need assistance even for relatively simple tasks:
           ▪   Paying bills by cash
           ▪   Choosing items in the shop
▪    In advanced stages, unable to do any cognitive activity even with assistance.

            Alzheimer’s disease

• Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most common
  form of dementia, accounting for around two-
  thirds of cases. It causes a gradual decline in
  cognitive abilities, often beginning with
  memory loss "recent amnesia".
                   Diagnosis of AD

❑Gradual onset of symptoms
❑Clear consciousness
❑Irriversible
❑One or more acquired significant impairments
    (independence lost) in cognitive domains such as:
•   Memory (amnesia)
•   Language (aphasia)
•   Execution of purposeful movement (apraxia)
•   Recognition/familiarity (agnosia)
•   Visuospatial function (topographical disorientation)
•   Self-control/management (executive functions impairment)
• Other manifestations:
• Mathematics ( acalculia or dyscalculia)
• Emotional expression/comprehension (depres
  sion in early stage followed by apathy)
• Writing (agraphia)
• Behavior not in accord with social standard of
  patient
❑Other causes of dementia or memory loss have to
  be excluded.
❑Alzheimer’s disease Progresses Through Distinct
Stages:
• Mild stage (It can be preceded by a state of
  "mild cognitive impairment MCI"), moderate
  stage and severe phase.

                  Pathology of AD

Gross pathology: marked brain atrophy.
Histopathology: There are 3 consistent neuropathological   hallm
arks:
• Amyloid-rich senile plaques
• Neurofibrillary tangles
• Neuronal degeneration
These changes eventually lead to clinical symptoms, but
they begin years before the onset of symptoms.
Neurotransmiters: Acetylcholine (Ache) is an important neur
otransmitter in areas of the brain involved in memory
formation. Loss of Ache activity correlates with the severity
 of AD. Also glutamate involved in AD.

                    Delirium
• It is a neuropsychiatric syndrome also called acute
  confusional state or acute brain failure that is
  common among the medically ill and often is
  misdiagnosed as a psychiatric illness which can
  result in delay of adequate medical intervention.
  Significant mortality is associated with delirium so
  identifying it is crucial.
•
                      Delirium
• Nomenclature: Deliruim is also called
Acute confusional state, ICU psychosis, Sundowning ,
Encephalopathy, Acute brain failure, Syndrome of cerebral
insufficiency
                                  Delirium
•   Definition:
•   Delirium is defined as a transient, usually reversible, cerebral dysfunction that
    manifests clinically with abroad range of neuropsychiatric abnormalities.
•   The hallmarks of delirium are waxing and waning signs, symptoms, and sensorium.
    Alertness and vigilance in the patient fluctuate.
•
•   Delirium is life-threatening but can be prevented.
       Epidemiology of delirium:
• Delirium is present in 10% of hospitalized medical patients
  and a further 10-30% develop delirium after
  admission.Postoperative delirium occur in 15-53% of patients
  and 70-87% of those in intensive care.

                        Etiology:
It is usually multifactorial
❑ Systemic illness:
• Infections
• Electrolyte abnormalities
• Endocrine dysfunctions (hypo or hyper)
• Liver failure- hepatic encephalopathy
• Renal failure- uremic encephalopathy
• Pulmonary disease with hypoxemia
• Cardiovascular disease/events: CHF, arrhythmias, MI
• CNS pathology: tumors, strokes, seizures
• Deficiency states: Thiamine, nicotinic or folic acid, B12
❑ Medications: any psychoactive medication can cause delirium. This can
  be in the context of toxicity, withdrawal and side effects of these
  medications.
•   Anticholinergics as TCAs
•   Analgesics (morphine, codeine.)
•   Steroids
•   Antiparkinsonian (anticholinergic and dopaminergic)
•   Sedatives (benzodiazepines, barbiturates), alcohol.
•   Anticonvulsants
•   Antihistamines
•   Antiarrhythmics (digitalis)
•   Antihypertensive
•   Antidepressants
•   Antimicrobials (penicillin, cephalosporins, quinolones)
•   Sympathomimetics
•   Others (furosemide, digoxin, theophylline, cimetidine, captopril)
           Course and prognosis:

• The symptoms usually continue to progress/fluctuate until
  treatment of underlying cause.
• About 50% of patients can improve. Symptoms usually
  resolve within a week of correction/improvement of the
  underlying etiology HOWEVER symptoms may wax and
  wane. In some patients, it can take weeks for the symptoms
  to resolve.
• Some patients, particularly older patients, may never return
  to baseline "about 25% of cases".
• Death can occur in about 25% of patients.
               Clinical characteristics:

•   Develops acutely (hours to days)
•   Characterized by fluctuating level of consciousness during the day. Cognitive
    functions are usually worse in the evening (sundowning phenomenon)
•   Reduced ability to focus, maintain or shift attention.
•   Agitation (hyperactive type of delirium) or hypersomnolence (hypoactive type).
    Sometimes it can be mixed type (both hyperactive & hypoactive type).
•   Extreme emotional lability.
•   A change in cognition or development of perceptual disturbances that is not better
    accounted for a preexisting, existed or evolving dementia.
•   There is evidence from this history, physical examination or labs that the
    disturbance is caused by the physiological consequence of a medical condition.
                      Testing:

• Mini mental status exam (MMSE) is not sensitive
  in identifying delirium however repeated MMSEs
  can reveal waxing and waning course
• Most sensitive items are serial 7’s, orientation,
  recall memory
• Tests of attention include serial 7’s,spelling WORLD
  backwards, months of the year backward, counting
  down from 20.
