Approach to Back Pain
• Broad clinical term, not a specific disease.



• There are a variety of causes - the most common
  cause is mechanical back pain due to muscle,
  tendon, or ligamentous strain.




• In up to 85% of individuals, no specific cause can
  be isolated
Acute Low Back Pain (LBP)
• common presenting illness in the primary care
  setting.
• Definition
  • As pain below the costal margins and above
    the inferior gluteal folds with no serious
    underlying pathology.
  • Often referred to as mechanical low back pain.
  • Frequently recurs and resolves on its own,
    regardless of treatment
• Work-up should focus on ruling out more serious
  pathology by screening for red flags.
  • Presence of red flags suggests it is not simple
    mechanical back pain.
Red Flags - Screen with Acute
LBP
• -Less than 20 or older than 50, with back pain
  for the first time.
• - Trauma.
• - The pain is constant and getting worse.
• - Pain is worse at night or when supine.
• - Previous cancer history.
• - Steroid use, IV drug use, risk of UTI and/or
  immuno-suppressed.
• - Fever and/or weight loss.
• - Neurological signs such as weakness,
  numbness, saddle-anesthesia or bowel/bladder
MANAGEMENT OF ACUTE LBP: IN THE
ABSENCE OF RED FLAGS, TREATMENT
IS CONSERVATIVE.
Chronic back pain
• Definition : low back pain of unspecified
  pathology that persists longer than 3 months.
   • The evolution of chronic LBP is complex, with
     physiological, psychological, and psychosocial
     influences.
   • Depression is often associated with chronic
     back pain – malingering is uncommon.
• Work-up for chronic back pain (and acute LBP)
  should include a screen for yellow flags ( special
  scales and scores to assess social, psychological
  and occupational disabilities ) that indicate risk
  for chronic disability
Management of Chronic Pain
• In the absence of red flags, chronic back
  pain should be treated in a multi-modal
  but conservative fashion.
•     Early return to work is a priority. The
  patient’s ideas about pain and other
  yellow flags need to be addressed with
  appropriate sensitivity.
• An important treatment goal for both
  acute and chronic back pain is for the
  patient to be active as soon as possible.
Neck pain and / or arm pain
(brachialgia)
• They are very common clinical
  problems; 2 out of 3 people
  experience neck pain at some point in
  life
NEUROLOGICAL CAUSES OF
NECK PAIN AND
BRACHIALGIA
• Cervical spondylosis

• Cervical disc prolapse
Cervical spondylosis
• the most common cause
• degenerative disorder of the cervical spine which can compress the
  cord or nerve roots.
• Patients often awake in the morning with a painful stiff neck and
  diffuse non pulsatile headache that resolves in a few hours.
• The lesion is most commonly at C5/6 and C6/7, and focal signs are
  likely to be at these levels.
• Wasting and weakness of the small muscles of the hands,
  particularly weakness of abduction of the little finger, is also
  frequently seen.
• Restricted neck movement is always present with significant cervical
  spondylosis.
• Bladder dysfunction, or the finding of long-tract signs or symptoms,
  indicates spinal cord compression.
Cervical disc prolapse
• Definition:
  • when the soft nucleus pulposus herniates through tear in the
    annulus fibrosis.
• Epidemiology:
  • The most common affected levels are C5-C6 and C6-C7
  • Usually occurs in middle age group from 30-50 Ys
• Clinical picture:
• If prolapse is poster lateral, it will cause nerve root
  compression i.e. radiculopathy.
• If the prolapse is central it will cause cord compression
  i.e. myelopathy.
• In case of both nerve root and cord compression, the
  result will be radiculomyelopathy.
• Symptoms:
  • Neck pain and stiffness.
  • Pain radiating down to the arm and hand
    called brachialgia which is exacerbated by neck
    motion (extension) and relieved by arm
    abduction (arm abduction test).
  • Paresthesia and numbness along the affected
    dermatome.
  • Motor weakness in long standing compression.
  • Sphincteric dysfunction in cases of myelopathy.
• Neck signs:
  • Loss of cervical lordosis.
  • Local tenderness.
  • Limitation of spinal movement.
  • Spasm of paravertebral muscle.
• Signs of radiculopathy:
  • Motor weakness in the group of muscles
    supplied by the compressed nerve root.
  • Sensory changes or dermatomal affection along
    the compressed nerve root.
  • Decreased reflexes according to the root
    affected.
  • The distribution of pain in the upper limb varies
    according to the nerve root affected
• The most common nerve root
  affected is C6 nerve root in C5-C6
  disc herniation and C7 nerve root
  in C6-C7 disc herniation and to a
  lesser extent is C5 root in C4-C5
  disc herniation.
Signs of myelopathy:

• Spastic quadriparesis.
• Hyper reflexia below the level of the
  compression.
• Clumsiness and ataxic Gait disturbance.
• Sphincteric disturbance.
Investigations:

• Plain x-ray cervical spine( Loss of normal
  cervical lordosis, Narrowing of the disc
  space, Osteophytes, Cervical subluxation)
• MRI cervical spine: the best diagnostic
  technique.
• CT cervical spine: more useful for bony
  details, to detect osteophytes, to detect
  congenital cervical stenosis and to
  diagnose OPLL (ossified posterior
  longitudinal ligament).
Treatment:
• Conservative ( Bed rest, Avoidance of heavy
  lifting, Physical therapy, Analgesics, Muscle
  relaxant, Neck collar)
• Surgical
• Indications for surgery:
• Brachialgia not responding to medical
  treatment.
• Progressive neurological deficit due to root
  compression.
• Manifestations of cord compression i.e.
  myelopathy.

Lower back and/or lower
limb (sciatica) pain
• Lower back pain is one of the most
  common reasons for neurological and
  neurosurgical consultation. Lower limb
  pain is a common accompaniment to
  lower back pain but can occur
  independently.
NEUROLOGICAL CAUSES OF
LOW BACK PAIN AND
SCIATICA
• Lumbar disc prolapse
• Siatica
• Cauda equina
Lumbar disc prolapse
• Definition:
  • Lumbar disc prolapse occurs when the soft
    nucleus pulposus herniates through tear in the
    annulus fibrosus.
• Etiology
  • Lumbar spine degeneration
  • Multiple repetitive microtaraumata such as
    lifting heavy weights
  • Single major trauma such as falling or lifting
    heavy weight preceding the onset of
    symptoms.
Clinical presentations:

• Back syndrome: This includes back
  manifestations only
• Leg syndrome: This includes neurological &
  mechanical manifestations in one or both legs
  along one or more nerve roots(
  Monoradiculopathy or Polyradiculopathy)
• Classic back & leg syndrome
• Neurogenic claudication: This includes unilateral
  or bilateral monoradicular or polyradicular
  manifestations along the lower limbs on
  movement and relieved by rest.
• Cauda equina syndrome
• Back signs: Loss of lumbar lordosis, Local
  tenderness, Limitation of spinal movement,
  Scoliosis, Spasm of paravertebral muscle.
• Leg signs : “Signs of radiculopathy”
• Motor weakness in the group of muscles
  supplied by the compressed nerve root.
• Sensory changes along dermatomal affection of
  the compressed nerve root.
• Decreased reflexes according to the root
  affected.
• Positive Straight leg raising test (SLRT)
Sciatica
• Definition:
  • - Sciatica describes the pain referred down the
    course of the sciatic nerve from the back to the
    buttock, down the back of the leg to the foot.
• Causes :
    •-     The most common cause of sciatica arises from
     compromise of the L5 and S1 roots, this commonly
     occurs due to:
    •o     Lumbar disc prolapse
    •o     Diabetic ischemia of the nerve and roots
    •o     Neoplastic involvement
• Diagnosis: Sciatic neuropathy is a clinical diagnosis
sciatica
Cauda equina syndrome.
• It occurs due to large central lumbar disc
  herniation compressing the cauda equina and
  needs early surgical intervention as any delay
  leads to permanent neurological deficit.
Symptoms:
• Low back pain.
• Bilateral sciatica.
• Sphincteric disturbance of LMNL: urine retention
  with over flow incontinence, chronic
  constipation.
• Autonomic sexual dysfunction (impotence in
  males).
• Motor: bilateral LMN weakness of dorsal and plantar
  flexion of the foot (flail foot) due to weakness of ankle
  dorsiflexors and ankle planter flexors.
• Weakness of hip flexors and/or extensors and weakness
  of knee flexors and/or extensors may also occur.
• Sensory: hyposthesia bilaterally according to the spinal
  roots affected
• Saddle shaped hyposthesia due to affection of sacral
  roots.
• Absent ankle and knee reflexes bilaterally.
• SLRT: positive bilaterally
• The most common nerve root affected is S1 nerve root
  in L5-S1 disc herniation and L5 root in L4-L5 disc
  herniation and to a lesser extent is L4 root in L3-L4 disc
  herniation.
Investigations:

• Laboratory Investigations
• It is recommended that no laboratory
  investigations be ordered for patients with
  uncomplicated mechanical low back pain.
• If there is suspicion about a systemic cause
  of low back pain such as inflammatory
  arthritis, connective tissue disease, or
  infection, then CBC, ESR, and other
  markers may be appropriate.
• No imaging is recommended for acute
  mechanical low back pain in the absence of red
  flags.
  • Keep in mind that imaging can be used to help
    confirm a clinical diagnosis, but cannot confirm
    that a particular structure is the cause of a
    patient’s pain.
    • Patients with uncomplicated acute low back pain
      and no red flags, who are between 20 and 50 years
      old, do not require imaging.
• In chronic low back pain, it may be appropriate
  to take AP and lateral lumbosacral x-rays.
• If the pain radiates below the knees, an MRI
  may be indicated
Treatment
• Conservative treatment:
• It is done for patients who present with low
  back pain and sciatica without any
  neurological deficit.
• It is usually applied for 4-6 weeks
• It includes:
 • Bed rest for 2-4 days on hard board mattress,
   Activity modification and avoid lifting heavy
   weights, Analgesics and NSAIDs, Muscle
   relaxants, Reduction of body weight, External
   lumbar support, Physical therapy
