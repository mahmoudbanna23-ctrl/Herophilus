Case Example: Part 1



Clara's Cryptic Presentation
Clara is a 23-year-old postgraduate student. Over three
weeks, she shifted from deep sadness and isolation to intense,
unprovoked agitation.


The Crisis: She was found holding a kitchen knife, screaming
at unseen entities, stating she "wanted to end the static."
Case Example: Part 2



The Assessment Failure

The team focused on outward agitation and her history of SSRI use for minor depression. Because she was uncooperative, no
formal Suicide Risk Assessment was performed.



The Missing Piece: They missed her sense of worthlessness and the fact that she had given away her prized
possessions.

The Intervention: She was chemically restrained with Haloperidol and left unmonitored in a standard cubicle.

Suicide & Deliberate Self-Harm
  Terminology & Clinical Boundaries


    Suicide

    The intentional taking of one's own life in a culturally non-endorsed manner.




    Attempted Suicide

    An unsuccessful suicidal act characterized by a non-fatal outcome.




"Clinical Note: Any deliberate self-harm must be treated as a potential suicide attempt until proven otherwise; intent and lethality
do not always align."

Diagnostic Variations in Suicide Risk

MDD

Risk peaks during early recovery, when psychomotor energy returns before mood lifts.




Schizophrenia

Driven by command hallucinations or high-functioning individuals realizing long-term decline.




Substance Use

Intoxication weaponizes passive suicidal ideation into lethal action by stripping inhibitions.
Myths vs. Clinical Facts (Part 1)




Myth: "People who talk about suicide don't complete it."   Fact: Most give clear, direct verbal or behavioral warnings.




Myth: "People who attempt really want to die."             Fact: Most are ambivalent; they want an end to pain, not
                                                           necessarily life.
Myths vs. Clinical Facts (Part 2)



Myth: "Once they decide to die, nothing stops them."   Fact: Crises are time-limited. Immediate intervention saves
                                                       lives permanently.




Myth: "All suicidal individuals are mentally ill."     Fact: Extreme distress can occur in those without a formal
                                                       diagnosis.
Securing the Physical Environment




Search Protocols: Thoroughly search patient for alcohol, drugs, or hidden pills.

Remove Hazards: Belts, straps, shoelaces, glass, and sharp instruments.

Unit Control: Never leave medical trays within reach; no locking doors from the inside (especially bathrooms).

Catatonia
Understanding the Syndrome

                       Stupor: Motoric immobility and lack of
                       responsiveness.

                       Excitement: Purposeless, frantic motoric activity.

                       Negativism: Motiveless resistance to all instructions.

                       Echo Phenomena: Echolalia and Echopraxia.
Secondary vs. Primary Catatonia



Medical Catatonia

Direct physiological consequence of an underlying medical illness (laboratory-proven).




Exclusion Criteria

Diagnosis is excluded if symptoms are better explained by a primary mental disorder or occur exclusively during acute
delirium.

Systemic & Lethal Complications




Thromboembolic: Prolonged immobility leads directly to DVT and fatal PE.

Nutritional: Dehydration and electrolyte derangements due to mutism/negativism.

Pulmonary: Aspiration pneumonia and decubitus ulcers.

Malignant Catatonia: Hyperthermia and autonomic instability.

Benzodiazepines & ECT



Benzodiazepines (First-Line)

Rapid, temporary improvement in symptoms. Drastically improves communication and enables self-care.




Electroconvulsive Therapy (ECT)

An emergency mandate if catatonia is life-threatening (starvation) or has escalated to Malignant Catatonia.

Cooling: Cooling devices and antipyretics for hyperthermia.

Antidotes: Bromocriptine/Amantadine and Dantrolene Sodium.

NMS vs. Serotonin Syndrome



The NMS Proﬁle: Precipitated by dopamine antagonists. Onset is subacute (days/weeks). Distinguished by severe "lead-pipe"
muscle rigidity and bradykinesia. Autonomic features include labile blood pressure and profuse diaphoresis. Requires
Bromocriptine/Dantrolene.




The Serotonin Syndrome Proﬁle: Triggered by serotonergic system overstimulation (SSRIs/MAOIs). Onset is hyperacute
(minutes/24h). Characterized by hyperreﬂexia, clonus, and distinct tremors. Features gastrointestinal hyperactivity and
mydriasis. Requires Cyproheptadine.
