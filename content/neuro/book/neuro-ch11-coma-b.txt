C. Other important information to obtain immediately:
- Current medications, especially any diabetic, anticonvulsant, cardiac drugs, or warfarin Any
  history of adverse or allergic medicinal reactions.
- Any history of recent head trauma, febrile or other illness, or previous neurological symptoms
- Any use of recreational drugs.

PHYSICAL EXAMINATION

1. Vital signs
- Hypertension to an extreme degree
  - Acute cerebral hemorrhage or infarction
  - Hypertensive encephalopathy
  - Cocaine abuse
  - Eclampsia
- Hypotension
  - Hypovolemia
  - Cardiogenic shock
  - Septic shock
- Temperature
  - Fever: consider infection, heat stroke.
  - Hypothermia: cold exposure (especially with alcoholism), hypothyroidism, hypoglycemia,
    sepsis, or, infrequently, primary brain lesion.
- Pulses: Asymmetry: dissecting aneurysm.

2. General look
- Head: Inspect and palpate scalp for signs of trauma. Blood in external ear canal (Battle sign)
  or around eyes (raccoon sign) suggests skull fracture. Inspect ears and nose for blood and CSF.
- Cutaneous Bleeding
  - Around the eyes or mastoid area -> skull fractures
  - Diffuse hematomas -> systemic bleeding
  - Splinter nail bed or palmar/plantar hemorrhages -> Infective endocarditis
- Jaundice and ascites -> Hepatic coma Hepatosplenomegaly

LEVEL OF CONSCIOUSNESS

- It is more useful to describe the patient's spontaneous behavior and responses to stimuli than
  to use terms such as stupor or obtunded. Even a coma has a spectrum of possible responses.

CRANIAL NERVES

The pupils
- Parasympathetic lesions: e.g., oculomotor nerve compression in uncal herniation or aneurysm;
  pupil enlarges; ultimately, full dilation, no reaction to light.
- Sympathetic lesions: Horner syndrome (miosis, ptosis, anhydrosis).
  - Pinpoint but reactive pupils: pontine hemorrhage, opiate intoxication.
  - Bilateral Fixed, dilated pupils: confirm with bright light.
    Consider:
    a) Anticholinergic drugs (atropine, glutethimide, amitriptyline, antiparkinsonian agents)
    b) Hypothermia
    c) Severe barbiturate intoxication
       [italic red, centred] (b and c can cause reversible picture simulating brain death)
    d) Ongoing or recent seizure (pupil abnormality may be unilateral)
    e) Anoxic-ischemic event (fixed pupils imply poor prognosis).

- Eyes dysconjugate at rest: paresis of individual muscles, internuclear ophthalmoplegia.
- Conjugate lateral deviation of the eyes toward one side indicate a lesion in:
  - The contralateral brainstem.
  - Ipsilateral cerebral hemisphere.
- Persistent, rhythmical nystagmus -> a subtle finding of non-convulsive status epilepticus

[Rounded blue box occupying the lower two-thirds of the page]

Voluntary conjugate eye movement pathway

If you want to look toward the RIGHT direction:
1. The LEFT frontal eye field (FEF) (area 8) fires. The signal travels down and crosses the
   midline (decussates) as it descends toward the brainstem.
2. The signal arrives at the paramedian pontine reticular formation (PPRF), often called the
   "Horizontal Gaze Center," located in the pons.
3. The PPRF stimulates the Abducens Nucleus (CN VI) on the same side (in this case, the right
   side).
   - The Abducens nucleus is unique because it houses two distinct types of neurons:
     a) Lower Motor Neurons: These travel via the Abducens nerve directly to the Lateral Rectus
        muscle of the right eye. This pulls the right eye outward (abduction).
     b) Internuclear Neurons: These are the "secretaries" that keep the other eye in the loop.
4. To get the left eye to move right, the signal from the Abducens interneurons must travel to
   the Oculomotor Nucleus (CN III) on the opposite side. These neurons cross back over the
   midline and travel upward through a specialized white matter tract called the Medial
   Longitudinal Fasciculus (MLF).
5. The MLF delivers the signal to the Oculomotor Nucleus in the midbrain. From here, CN III
   activates the Medial Rectus muscle of the left eye. This pulls the left eye inward
   (adduction).
6. The Result: Both eyes move smoothly to the right in perfect synchrony.

Blinking
- Spontaneous blinking -> intact ARAS
- "Locked-in syndrome" -> only blinking and vertical eye movements.

RESPIRATORY PATTERN

- An observation of hypo or hyperventilation can be helpful in the diagnosis of a patient with
  coma, especially when combined with blood gas results.
- There are different respiratory patterns that might be helpful in localizing levels of
  involvement.
  1. Cheyne-Stokes respiration (CSR): periods of hyperventilation and apnea alternating in
     crescendo-decrescendo fashion. Bilateral cerebral disease, including impending
     trans-tentorial herniation, upper brainstem lesions, metabolic encephalopathy.
  2. Sustained hyperventilation: usually metabolic acidosis, pulmonary congestion, hepatic
     encephalopathy.
  3. Apneustic breathing: inspiratory pauses; pontine lesions (especially infarct); infrequently,
     metabolic coma or transtentorial herniation.
  4. Ataxic breathing (Biot breathing): variably irregular rate and amplitude; medullary damage.
  5. Stertorous breathing (i.e., inspiratory noise): airway obstruction.
  6. Acetone, alcohol, fetor hepaticus.

MOTOR EXAMINATION

- It is important to assess muscle tone, as well as spontaneous and elicited movements and
  reflexes.
- Asymmetrical limb movements or tone occur with structural brain lesions.
- Abnormal postures:
  o Decorticate posturing (FLEXED upper limb) -> Supratentorial lesions.
    [blue rounded box]
    - deCORticate = arms flexed toward CORE
    - loss of CORtical inhibition of rubrospinal tract (still intact)
  o Decerebrate posturing (EXTENDED upper limb) -> Infratentorial lesions.
    [blue rounded box]
    - decerebrate has more E & R
    - Extended upper limbs & transected Rubrospinal tract
- Bilateral myoclonic jerks suggest toxic-metabolic causes of coma.
- Stiff neck: (on passive flexion, not lateral rotation or tilting): meningitis, subarachnoid
  hemorrhage, foramen magnum herniation.
- Resistance in all directions: bone or joint disease, including fractures (exclude cervical
  fracture before checking for stiff neck).

INVESTIGATION

- CT or MRI: Perform promptly if coma unexplained; before lumbar puncture if examination
  suggests focal lesion.
- Lumber Puncture: [sic] Perform promptly if coma not explained by imaging. If imaging is not
  readily available and mass lesion not suspected on examination, perform spinal tap cautiously.
- EEG Can distinguish coma from psychic unresponsiveness and locked-in state. Always abnormal in
  true coma. May also reveal asymmetry or clinically unsuspected seizure activity.
- Emergency laboratory studies
  Blood levels of glucose, sodium, calcium, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine; arterial pH, PCO2,
  PO2; other electrolytes and liver function tests; blood and CSF cultures; blood or urine
  toxicology screening. Coagulation studies, other metabolic tests, depending on suspicion.

| Immediate tests | Referred tests |
| --- | --- |
| Glucose | Toxic Screen |
| Electrolytes | Liver Function Tests |
| CBC, BUN, Cr | Ammonia |
| Osmolality | Coagulation tests |
| ABG | TSH, cortisol |
| LP (if no mass lesion) | AED levels |

MANAGEMENT OF COMA IN SPECIFIC SITUATIONS

1) Comatose patient with fever or septic syndrome
   - Initial approach.
   - Urgent LP to exclude CNS infection (unless contraindicated).
   - Once LP is done, or if there will be a delay in LP for any reason, immediately start empiric
     treatment.

2) Comatose patient with focal findings on neurological examination (Structural lesions)
   - Initial approach.
   - Urgent brain CT without contrast to exclude hemorrhage.
     o Investigate and treat intracranial hemorrhage or other cause of brain edema or shift.

PROGNOSIS

- Non-traumatic
  Only about 15% of patients in nontraumatic coma make a satisfactory recovery.
  - Best ultimate outcome: Metabolic causes like hepatic encephalopathy
  - Intermediate prognosis: Hypoxia-ischemia (cardiac arrest if treated before 5 minutes)
    TIME IS CRITICAL [printed in red]
  - The worst prognosis: Diseases causing structural damage (cerebro-vascular disease including
    subarachnoid hemorrhage) or cardiac arrest for more than 5 minutes
- Drug-induced coma usually reversible
- Traumatic coma
  In relationship to their initial degree of neurological abnormality, traumatic coma patients do
  better than non-traumatic coma patients.
