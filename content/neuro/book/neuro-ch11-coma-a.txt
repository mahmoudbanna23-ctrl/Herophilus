CHAPTER 11

CLINICAL APPROACH TO "COMA" CASE

[Banner heading:] COMA

Consciousness

Awareness of self and environment; requires both arousal and mental content; requires
integrity of reticular activating system and cerebral cortex.

> Two components of conscious behavior:
  - Content: The sum of cognitive and affective function (cognitive and affective mental
    function) (function of both cerebral cortices).
  - Arousal: Appearance of wakefulness (function of ascending reticular activating system)

> The Content depends on arousal, but normal arousal does not guarantee normal content.
  - Arousal damage -> coma, semi coma, delirium.
  - Content damage -> dementia, delusions, confusion, inattention.

Spectrum of consciousness

- Alert, conscious: perfectly normal state of arousal.
- Lethargy: lies between alertness and stupor. difficulty maintaining alertness.
- Stupor: is a state of baseline unresponsiveness that requires repeated application of
  vigorous stimuli (verbal or motor) to achieve arousal.
- Semi-coma: a state of response to motor stimulus only (not verbal).
- Coma: is a state of complete unresponsiveness to arousal.

[Boxed panel:] You should differentiate between...!
- Sleep: It is the only normal form of altered consciousness.
- Delirium: acute disturbance of attention associated with disturbance in cognition with
  clouding of consciousness, perception, and orientation -> reduced ability to sustain
  attention to environmental stimuli.
- Encephalopathy is a term for any diffuse disease of the brain that alters brain function
  or structure.

Neuroanatomy of Arousal

- Ascending Reticular Activating System (ARAS) 'core of the brainstem' receives input from
  somatic afferents (acts as a gate).
- ARAS projects to thalamus and the limbic system -> both cerebral cortices.

ASCENDING RETICULAR ACTIVATING SYSTEM (ARAS)

[FIGURE: mid-sagittal drawing of the brain and brainstem with blue arrows sweeping from the
brainstem core up through the thalamus to the cortex. Text labels read:]
  Cerebral cortex
  Thalamus
  RAS projections to cerebral cortex
  Arousal Center
  Cerebellum
  Pons
  Reticular formation

  Visual impulses from eyes
  Auditory and vestibular impulses from ears and vestibular apparatus
  Reticulospinal tracts (to skeletal muscles)
  Somatic sensory impulses (from nociceptors, proprioceptors, and touch receptors)

Coma pathogenesis

- Coma implies dysfunction of: ARAS OR Both hemi-cortices.
- Anatomically:
  - Central brainstem structures (bilaterally) from caudal medulla to rostral midbrain.
  - Both hemispheres.

Classifications of Coma

A. Toxic metabolic coma (2/3 of cases)

  Metabolic Coma Differentiating Features:
  - Confusion and stupor commonly precede motor signs.
    (A confusional state or delirium occurs initially).
  - Motor signs are usually symmetrical.
  - Pupillary reactions are usually preserved.
  - Asterixis, myoclonus, tremor, and seizures are common.
  - Acid-base imbalance with hyper- or hypoventilation is frequent.
  - Tremulousness, myoclonic jerks, and asterixis are typical.

[Running header: coma]

> Causes:

1. Deprivation of oxygen, substrate, or metabolic cofactor (shock)
   - Hypoxia, diffuse ischemia (cardiac disease, decreased peripheral circulatory
     resistance, increased cerebrovascular resistance, widespread small-vessel occlusion)
     or any cause of hypotension.
   - Hypoglycemia
   - Thiamine deficiency (Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome)

2. Disease of organs other than brain
   - Liver (hepatic coma, sedative hypersensitivity)
   - Kidney (uremia)
   - Lung (CO2 narcosis)
   - Pancreas (diabetes, hypoglycemia, exocrine pancreatic encephalopathy)
   - Thyroid (myxedema, thyrotoxicosis)
   - Adrenal (Addison or Cushing disease, pheochromocytoma)
   - Other systemic disease (e.g., sepsis)

3. Exogenous poisons (drugs or toxins): e.g., sedatives and narcotics, Psychotropic drugs,
   Acid poisons (e.g., methyl alcohol, ethylene glycol), Others (e.g., anticonvulsants,
   heavy metals, cyanide)

4. Abnormalities of electrolytes: e.g., water and sodium (hypo- and hypernatremia),
   acidosis, alkalosis, magnesium (hyper- and hypomagnesemia), calcium (hyper- and
   hypocalcemia), phosphorus (hypophosphatemia).

B. Structural coma (1/3 of cases)

  > Supratentorial lesions cause coma by either widespread bilateral disease, increased
    intracranial pressure, or herniation.
  > Infratentorial lesions involve the RAS, usually with associated brainstem signs.

[FIGURE: small coloured drawing of the brain in sagittal view with the tentorium marked in
red. Title and labels read:]
  The Tentorium Cerebelli
  Supratentorial (cerebrum)
  Tentorium
  Infratentorial (cerebellum)

- Asymmetric motor or ocular reflex findings occur early.
- A unilateral dilated pupil unresponsive to light indicates uncal herniation until proven
  otherwise.

[Running header: coma]

1. Supratentorial Mass Lesions Differential Characteristics
   - Initiating signs usually of focal cerebral dysfunction (point to one anatomic area).
   - Motor signs are often asymmetrical.
   - Causes (Hematoma, Neoplasm, abscess, contusion, vascular accidents, diffuse axonal
     damage)

2. Infratentorial Mass Lesions Differential Characteristics
   - History of preceding brainstem dysfunction or sudden onset of coma.
   - Localizing brainstem signs precede or accompany onset of coma and always include
     oculovestibular abnormality.
   - Cranial nerve palsies are usually present.
   - "Bizarre" respiratory patterns are common, usually present at onset of coma.
   - Causes (Neoplasm, vascular accidents, trauma, cerebellar hemorrhage, central pontine
     myelinolysis).

3. Infection induced Coma Differentiating Features
   - History of infection
   - Fever
   - Nuchal rigidity (meningeal signs)
   - Kernigs, Brudzinski signs [sic — printed without apostrophe]
   - Skin Rash

C. Psychiatric causes: Catatonia (Mute, with marked decrease in motor activity)

Coma Differential Diagnosis

- Brain death.
- Persistent vegetative state.
- Locked-in syndrome.
- Thalamic lesions.
- Nonconvulsive status epilepticus.
- Psychiatric unresponsiveness.

[Banner heading:] BRAIN DEATH

- Irreversible loss of brain and brainstem function.
- The cause of coma is known and sufficient to cause brain death, such as cardiopulmonary
  arrest.
- No improvement occurs during observation and treatment.

  "Observation is at least 6 hours for adults, 12 hours to 2 days for children."

- Comatose patient with absence of all brainstem reflexes ->
  - Corneal reflex
  - Pupillary light reflex
  - Gag reflex
  - Oculocephalic reflex (Doll's sign)
  - Vestibulo-ocular reflex (Caloric test) -> Injection of water into the ear to see
    nystagmus sign.
  - No evidence of spontaneous respiration (Abnormal bedside apnea test: no observed
    breaths despite pCO2 > 60 mm, while on 100% oxygen)

[Running header: coma]

- Absence of any potentially reversible causes of marked CNS depression ->
  - Hypothermia (temperature 32°C [89.6°F] or less)
  - Hypotensive shock
  - Drug intoxication (particularly barbiturate overdose)
  - Severe metabolic disturbance

- Ancillary testing may help to confirm the clinical diagnosis ->
  - Flat-line EEG
  - Absent cerebral blood flow by Trans-cranial doppler or Radioisotope scan

[FIGURE: six pen-and-ink clinical drawings of brain-death bedside testing. Text labels read:]

    stimuli
  Feels for breath on cheek — No spontaneous respiration
  Pupils dilated, unresponsive to light
  Ice water in ear: eyes do not move
  "Doll's eyes": head turned sharply to side, eyes remain centered
  Corneal reflex lost

[Banner heading:] PERSISTENT VEGETATIVE STATE

  "Defect in the content not in the awareness"

- Appears intermittently awake.
- Breathes spontaneously, intact cardiac and gastric motility.
- Exhibits primitive reflexes or eye-roving behavior.
- Severe cerebral damage persists.
- NO meaningful communication or cortical responsiveness occurs.
- A chronic not acute event with impaired content of consciousness (cortex)

  "It may be due persistent hypoxia chronic not treated perfectly and it will make the
  patient pass through a chronic state of PVS." [sic — printed as written]

> Criteria for Diagnosis of Persistent Vegetative State
  - No awareness of self or environment.
  - No interaction with others.
  - No evidence of sustained, reproducible, purposeful, or voluntary behavioral responses
    to visual, auditory, tactile, or noxious stimuli.
  - No evidence of language comprehension or expression
  - Intermittent wakefulness manifested by the presence of sleep/wake cycles.
  - Bowel and bladder incontinence.
  - Preserved autonomic functions.
  - Variably preserved cranial nerve (pupillary, oculocephalic, corneal, vestibulo-ocular,
    and gag) and spinal reflexes.

[Banner heading:] LOCKED-IN SYNDROME

- The patient may appear in a persistent vegetative state
- Unable to move the limbs and face, or gaze laterally "de-efferented".
- Vertical gaze and eye blinking are PRESERVED.
- Communication and cortical functions are preserved only by blink and vertical gaze.
- The patient accurately blinks once of "yes" or twice for "no" into the examiner. [sic]

> Cause -> Extensive pontine infarction.

[Banner heading:] NON-CONVULSIVE STATUS EPILEPTICUS

- Continued or persistent generalized seizures may occur in the absence of obvious clinical
  convulsive activity.
- Subtle clinical manifestations include:
  - Rhythmical nystagmus
  - Twitching of an eyelid or part of the face or limb.
  - Obtain an emergent EEG recording and assess the response to IV benzodiazepine bolus.

[Banner heading:] PSYCHIATRIC UNRESPONSIVENESS

  Rare [not diagnosed except after exclusion of all organic causes]

> Diagnosis of exclusion->
  - No history of drug overdose.
  - Intact brainstem reflexes.
  - Spontaneous breathing.
  - No focal neurological deficit.
  - Normal metabolic profile.
  - Normal brain MRI with DW sequence.
  - Normal EEG, similar to the awake state.

[Running header: coma]

Golden rules

- All alterations in arousal constitute acute life-threatening emergencies. There is always
  an urgency about such medical problems need to determine the underlying disease process
  and the direction in which it is evolving and to protect the brain against more serious
  or irreversible damage. [sic]
- NO management of coma at home.
- ABC's are 1st Priority (Airway, Breathing, Circulation (ABC) - identify and address life
  threatening inadequacies).
- Must ensure oxygen reaches CNS and vital organs.
- Must address immediately life-threatening conditions (cardiac, metabolic, ... etc) before
  addressing CNS.

  Try to go in a more generalized rather than a neurological approach.

- Treat rapidly progressive metabolic disorders - hypoglycemia.
- Evaluate for intracranial hypertension and imminent herniation and treat Signs of
  increased ICP/Herniation

Initial approach for a comatose patient

- ABC; maintain airway, breathing and circulation.
- Urgently correct any hypothermia
- Establish stability of the cervical spine if trauma has occurred or strongly suspected.
- Rule out hypoglycemia with an immediate fingerstick glucose reading or empirical infusion
  of 50% dextrose with IV thiamine (at least 100 mg)
- Check basic blood work and urine drug screening; CBC, electrolytes, glucose, renal and
  liver functions, PT, PTT, INR, ABG, possible carbon monoxide (CO) level if CO poisoning
  suspected.

[FIGURE: three-column pictogram panel — an airway, a pair of lungs, a heart. Text labels:]

  Airway
    - Talking = good
    - Look: edema, blood, vomitus, foreign body
    - Listen: noisy = obstructed

  Breathing
    - Look: work of breathing, respiratory rate
    - Listen: breath sounds
    - Check: pulse oximetry

  Circulation
    - Look: mental status, color
    - Feel: peripheral pulses
    - Check: heart rate, cardiac rhythm, blood pressure

[Running header: coma]

> Management of the Comatose Patient Airway

  Evaluate ->
  - Is the airway patent.
  - Is there trauma or foreign body obstructing airway
  - Try chin lift to help open airway -- protect cervical spine
  - Place airway if indicated - nasal or oral airway
  - Intubation

> Management of the Comatose Patient Circulation

  - Is the patient in shock?
    - Check pulses, heart rate, blood pressure, perfusion.
    - Remember hypotension is a late sign of shock.
  - Start treatment for shock.
    - Do not restrict fluids in comatose patients with inadequate intravascular volume.
    - Cardiac output and cerebral perfusion are much more important than fluid restriction.
    - Use isotonic solutions and blood, as indicated.
    - Do not use hypotonic solutions to treat shock, particularly patients with coma or
      possible cerebral edema.
    - Identify life threatening hemorrhage anywhere and control it. (especially if trauma
      is the cause)

[Banner heading:] HISTORY

A. Sudden Onset
   - Intracranial hemorrhage (prodromal severe headache may accompany subarachnoid
     hemorrhage [SAH] from a ruptured intracranial aneurysm, or occur with cerebral
     hemorrhage)
   - Cardiopulmonary arrest (significant cerebral hypoperfusion)
   - Embolic: multiple cerebral infarcts.
   - Trauma: observed or unwitnessed head trauma

B. Acute Onset (confusion or delirium preceding coma)
   - Toxo-metabolic etiology
     - Organ dysfunction or infection
     - Electrolyte disorders
     - Medicinal or drug toxicity
   - Critical brainstem infarction
