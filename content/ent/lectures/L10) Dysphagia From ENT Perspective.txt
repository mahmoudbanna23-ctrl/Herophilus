Definitions
Dysphagia
• Dysphagia is the term used to describe difficulty with swallowing solids, liquids, or both.
  It implies impairment of one or more of the phases of swallowing. Dysphagia usually
  arises as a complication of another health condition. It can be divided into oropharyngeal
  (high) dysphagia and esophageal (low) dysphagia.
• It is not to be confused with Odynophagia (pain on swallowing) or Globus sensation (a
  sensation in the throat, commonly of a ‘lump’)

Aspiration
• Aspiration is the entry of food or liquid into the airway below the true vocal folds. It may
  be due to incompetent or inadequate airway protection and ill-timed, uncoordinated
  events before, during, or after the swallow has triggered. Silent aspiration is defined as
  foreign material entering the trachea or lungs without an outward sign of coughing or
  attempts at expulsion.

   Hypopharyngeal tumors
• The hypopharynx is:
   • The caudal part of the pharynx
     (laryngopharynx)
   • Extending from the hyoid bone to
     the lower margin of the cricoid
     cartilage.
   • Hypopharyngeal cancers make up 5–
     10% of head and neck cancers.
Hypopharyngeal tumors
Epidemiology
• Hypopharyngeal cancers occur in individuals over 50 years
  old.
• Subsites of cancer (in order of most frequent to least) are:
    • Piriform sinus (PS)
    • Posterior pharyngeal wall (PPW)
    • Post-cricoid area (PC)
• PS and PPW lesions are more common in males, whereas PC
  lesions are predominant in females.
Hypopharyngeal tumors
Etiology
• Tobacco.
• Alcohol.
• Age (mean age of presentation is 65 years).
• Human papillomavirus (HPV) is present in 10.9% of
  hypopharyngeal cancers
• Iron-deficiency dysphagia (associated with Plummer-
  Vinson syndrome) for PC tumours.
Hypopharyngeal tumors
Symptomatology
• Detected late, because the patients are often asymptomatic in the
  early stages.
• The earliest symptom may be mild non-specific throat discomfort.
  Globus sensation may be the only presenting complaint, with normal
  clinical findings.
• Dysphagia to solids first then may advance to fluids as well
• Hoarsness of voice in case of laryngeal involvement
• Otalgia (ear pain) unilateral.
• Half of patients present with a neck mass.
Hypopharyngeal tumors
Signs :
• An absent laryngeal click in Postcricoid Carcinoma: The
  tumor fills the space, preventing the cricoid cartilage from
  rubbing against the vertebral column.
• Endoscopic evaluation of the hypopharynx and larynx ,
  providing visualization of pyriform sinus, posterior
  pharyngeal, or postcricoid tumors:
   • ulcerated or erythematous mucosal lesions,
     hyperkeratosis, and vocal cord weakness. Noting any
     pooling of secretions or laryngeal asymmetry.
Hypopharyngeal tumors
Investigations:
• Imaging by :
    • Barium swallow : detect postcricoid tumors that are not apparent
      on the endoscopic examination, and detect filling defects in the
      pyriform sinuses due to mucosal lesions.
    • CT or MRI, depending on local protocols.
    • Chest CT to examine for synchronous tumors.
    • Pet CT for assessment for distant metastasis.
• Panendoscopy (LARYNGOSCOPY AND OESPHAGOSCOPY ) for direct
  visualization of the upper aerodigestive tract and biopsy of the
  primary lesion under general anesthesia.
Management
• The overall treatment goal is to achieve locoregional control
• While minimizing functional morbidity by preserving phonation,
  deglutition, and oronasal respiratory function.
• Treatment options include
    • Surgery,
    • Radiotherapy,
    • Chemotherapy, or
    • A combination of these

Motility Disorders (Achalasia)
• Is an esophageal motility disorder characterized by aperistalsis in the body
  of the esophagus and failure of relaxation of the lower esophageal
  sphincter caused by impairment of the myenteric plexus of Auerbach's
  inhibitory neurons in the distal esophagus.
• An elderly patient may present with a long history of intermittent
  dysphagia with occasional aspiration and regurgitation.
• Younger patients may experience retrosternal pain from dysmotility and
                                 spasm.
Investigations
• Esophageal manometry is the most
  sensitive test for the diagnosis of
  achalasia and remains the gold
  standard.
• Barium swallow tests may initially be
  normal until esophageal dilatation and
  elongation occur in the later stages,
  revealing the classical findings of
  contrast retention with a tapering
  distal gastro-esophageal segment into
  a closed sphincter, also described as a
  ‘bird’s beak’
• Gastro-esophagoscopy is also
  performed to exclude an underlying
  malignancy.                               A contrast swallow demonstrating
                                            achalasia.
Management
• Non surgical
   • Calcium channel blockers,
   • Botox injections into the lower esophageal sphincter and
   • Sphincter dilatation
• Surgical: myotomy :
   • Laparoscopic Heller myotomy (LHM) with partial fundoplication is
     the surgical procedure of choice
   • Peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM) is an effective minimally
     invasive alternative to laparoscopic Heller myotomy
Globus Pharyngeus or Hystericus
• Feeling of a lump in the throat (FOLIT) brought on or made worse by
  anxiety with no other organic cause, which makes it a diagnosis of
  exclusion.
• Normal Examination and scan findings.
• Most of cases in Adults.
• DO YOUR WORK UP to exclude serious causes of dysphagia.
• Management: reassure the patient that the sensation will diminish as the
  stress diminishes, however, reassurance is often not enough. a trial of
  anti-reflux treatment and lifestyle changes to treat reflux, with follow-up
  in 4 to 6 weeks, because 30 per cent of globus patients have reflux
