• Language is a code whereby ideas about the
  world are expressed through a conventional
  system of arbitrary signals of communication.
• (Bloom and Lahey, 1978)
• Phonology: It is the sound system of a language and a linguistic rules
  that govern the sound combinations.
• Syntax: It is the linguistic rules governing the order and combination
  of words to form sentences, and the relationships among the
  elements within a sentence.
• Semantics: It is the system that patterns the content of an utterance
  intent and meanings of words and sentences.
• Pragmatics: It is the sociolinguistic system that patterns the use of
  language in communication which may be expressed motorly, verbally
  or vocally.

• The brain's primary language
  centers, mainly located in the left
  hemisphere, are Broca's

Aphasia
• Aphasia is a language disorder affecting understanding of spoken
  language; the ability to express thoughts verbally, including difficulties
  with word retrieval (anomia), and sentence production; the ability to
  read and understand written words and sentences and the ability to
  write including spelling and structuring written sentences.

learning disability
• Is a neurological-based processing disorder that interferes
  with learning basic skills (reading, writing, math) and higher-
  level functions (organization, time management, memory).
• It affects how individuals receive, store, retrieve, and use
  information, often causing significant academic or functional
  impairment despite having average or above-average
  intelligence.
Dyslalia
• The inability to correctly produce proper speech phonetics of a
  language that is based on any disorders of the articulation organ
  anatomy, the absence of any neurological disorders, and the absence
  of any hearing disorders.
• The etiologies of dyslalia is multifactor, it can be caused by the
  persistence of mistakes while articulating, the incorrect imitation of
  articulating patterns, effect on the articulation of vision
  abnormalities, and environmental factors.

Dysarthria
Dysarthria is a motor speech disorder resulting from impaired neuromuscular
    control over speech mechanisms due to damage of central or peripheral
    nervous system.
The main parameters of speech are respiration, phonation, resonance, articulation
    and prosody. One or more of these parameters can be affected leading to
    reduced speech intelligibility and reduced communication effectiveness.
Types:
•   Flaccid dysarthria (LMNL)
•   Spastic dysarthria (Bilateral UMNL)
•   Hypokinkinetic (Parkinson disease)
•   Hyperkinetic (chorea, asthetosis, dystonia)
•   Ataxic (Cerebellar)
•   Mixed dysarthria
Dysarthria (causes)

• Infectious processes.

• Anoxic conditions.
• Metabolic disorders

• The GRBAS scale, introduced by Hirano in 1981, is a widely used,
  internationally recognized, auditory-perceptual tool for assessing
  voice quality and dysphonia.
• It rates voice disorders on five parameters—Grade, Roughness,
  Breathiness, Asthenia, and Strain—using a 4-point scale (0-3) to
  indicate severity
• G (Grade): The overall degree of hoarseness or voice abnormality.
• R (Roughness): Indicates irregular, raspy vibration of the vocal folds.
• B (Breathiness): Indicates the extent of air leakage through the glottis.
• A (Asthenia): Represents weakness, lack of power, or low intensity.
• S (Strain): Reflects hyperfunctional, tight, or high-pitched vocal effort

Videostroboscopy

• During phonation the vocal folds vibrate between 80 and 1000 Hertz
  (cycles per second).
• Talbot’s law states that the retina can only register 1 image per 0.2
  seconds or 5 images per second.
• The vibration of the free edge of the vocal fold will therefore appear
  as a blur if viewed with a simple white light source.
Videostroboscopy
• The perceived stroboscopic motion of the vocal folds is an optical
  illusion created by pulsating flashes of light at multiple points of
  several successive vibratory cycles.

• Enables visual slowing of the rapid, periodic motion of the vocal folds

• Advantage:
  • Indirect assessment of vocal tract physiology by analyzing the interaction of
    the acoustic components of voice and vocal fold vibration.
  • Non-invasive and easy.
  • Compare pre and post treatment.

• Disadvantage:
   • Not diagnostic for the etiology.
   • No standardization.
   • Difficulty in obtaining correlation and comparisons between various studies.

Electroglottography
Electroglottography
• Consists of two electrodes placed on either side of the thyroid
  cartilage at the level of the vocal cords.
• A high-frequency current (3 MHz) is applied between the electrodes.
• The change in conductance correlates to the change in contact area
  between the vocal folds during vibration.
Electromyography (EMG)

• Study electrical potentials generated in a skeletal muscle.
• Diagnostic tool to differentiate between:
   • Neuromuscular and mechanical lesions.
   • Neurogenic and myogenic lesions
   • RLN or SLN affection.
• Degree and extent of vocal fold paralysis.
• Monitoring the prognosis.
