Pharyngeal Suppurations & Deep Neck Infections
By Professor EMAD A. MAGDY
Intended learning Objectives (ILOs):
   •   Define quinsy & list its clinical presentation.
   •   Outline management of quinsy.
   •   Discuss other most common pharyngeal suppurations (intratonsillar, parapharyngeal,
       acute and chronic retropharyngeal abscesses).
   •   List complications of pharyngeal suppurations.
   •   Outline management of pharyngeal suppurations.
   •   Discuss Ludwig’s angina & its management.

Causes of Pharyngeal Suppurations include:
1. Intratonsillar abscess.
2. Peritonsillar abscess (Quinsy).
3. Parapharyngeal abscess.
4. Acute Retropharyngeal abscess.
5. Chronic Retropharyngeal abscess.


1. Intratonsillar Abscess:
Definition:  Pus collection inside the tonsil.
Incidence:   Rare - usually in the adult age.
Causes:      Obstruction of one of the tonsillar crypts due to recurrent
             infection or foreign body or accumulation of food inside
             the tonsil. It is a rare complication of acute tonsillitis.
Symptoms: Mild in the form of: mild fever, pain and dysphagia.
Signs:       Yellowish, well-localized, soft cystic swelling on the
             surface of the tonsil.
Complications: Similar to that of acute tonsillitis.
Treatment:
       - Incision under local anesthesia.
       - General antibiotics and antiseptic gargles.
       - Tonsillectomy (after cure).

2. Peritonsillar Abscess (Quinsy):
Definition:    A suppurative process in the peritonsillar space (between the
               capsule of the tonsil and the adjacent lateral pharyngeal wall).
Incidence:     - Most frequently in adult males.
               - It is usually unilateral.
Etiology:      It usually follows an attack of acute tonsillitis when infection travels
               to the peritonsillar space via the crypta magna, resulting in cellulitis and pus
               formation.
Causative organism: Usually streptococcus haemolyticus.
Clinical picture: (As acute tonsillitis but in a very severe form).
Symptoms:
       • High fever, malaise and headache.
       • Marked sore throat with referred otalgia.
       • Marked dysphagia to the degree that the patient is unable to swallow even his own
          saliva.
       • Difficulty in opening the mouth (Trismus).
       • Painful swelling under the jaw.
       • Bad odoured breath (Halitosis).
Signs:
       • High temperature (39-40°C) & rapid strong bounding pulse.
       • Examination may be difficult due to trismus (results
          from spasm of masseter and pterygoid muscles).
       • Coated tongue & accumulated saliva.
       • Swelling of the soft palate above & lateral to the
          inflamed tonsil. The adjacent tonsil is pushed
          downwards and medially. The uvula is edematous and
          pushed to the other side.
       • Presence of a pale yellowish area over the swelling
          indicates abscess maturation (a point where it can
          spontaneously rupture).
       • Torticollis towards the affected side (results from
          spasm of the sternocleidomastoid “SCM” muscle).
       • Enlarged tender jugulo-digastric lymph nodes.
Complications:
       • Sudden rupture & inhalation of pus can lead to chest complications.
       • Extension laterally leading to parapharyngeal abscess or downwards leading to
          laryngeal edema & stridor.
       • Internal jugular vein thrombophlebitis.
       • Pyemia and septicemia (very rare).
Treatment:
       • Presuppurative stage:
          - Bed rest.
          - Antibiotics & analgesics.
          - Soft nutritious diet.
       • Suppurative stage:
          - Incision and drainage: Intraoral under local anesthesia. For
          incision of quinsy, a special quinsy knife (or a scalpel
          shouldered with adhesive plaster) should be used. For drainage
          of pus from the abscess a Bayonet-shaped quinsy forceps is
          introduced closed then opened on withdrawal to enlarge the
          opening safely (Hilton’s maneuver). Incision should be made at
          one of the following points:
           o 0.5 cm lateral to meeting of two lines, a vertical from
                the attachment of anterior pillar to base of tongue and a
                horizontal along the base of uvula.
           o Midway between a line between base of uvula and last
                upper molar.
           o The most pointing point.
          - General antibiotics.
          -   Tonsillectomy: one month later after all acute manifestations subside.

3. Parapharyngeal Abscess:
Definition: Suppuration (pus collection) in the parapharyngeal space.

Surgical anatomy:
    • The parapharyngeal space is an inverted cone lying
       in the lateral neck. It has the following boundaries:
           o Superiorly: Skull base.
           o Inferiorly: Hyoid bone.
           o Medially: Superior Constrictor muscle of the
               pharynx.
           o Laterally: Superficial layer of deep cervical
               fascia overlying: mandible – deep lobe of
               parotid – pterygoid muscles.
    • The parapharyngeal space is divided into anterior &
       posterior compartments by the styloid process.
           o The prestyloid or anterior compartment:
               “also known as the muscular compartment”
               contains no vital structures. It is related to
               the tonsillar fossa medially & the pterygoid muscles laterally.
           o The poststyloid or posterior compartment: Is traversed by the carotid sheath
               (containing the carotid artery & IJV), cranial nerves IX through XII & the
               cervical sympathetic chain. This is also known as the neurovascular
               compartment.
Etiology:
    1. Spread of infection from tonsils or quinsy.
    2. Odontogenic infections (e.g. extraction of 3rd molar tooth).
    3. Middle ear infections with bony destruction of mastoid tip (Bezold’s abscess).

Clinical picture:
            • High fever, sore throat & odynophagia.
            • Tender lateral neck swelling with inferior
               extent to the hyoid bone.
            • The lateral pharyngeal wall & tonsil are
               usually displaced medially.
            • Trismus.
Complications: (rare but serious)
1. IJV thrombosis (Lemierre’s syndrome).
2. Rupture of carotid artery.
3. Involvement of CN’s IX-XII or sympathetic chain.
4. Spread of infection to mediastinum (mediastinitis).

Treatment:
      • General treatment as peritonsillar abscess.
      • Systemic antibiotics.
      • Incision & drainage (externally – along the anterior
         border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle under general
         anesthesia).
4. Acute Retropharyngeal Abscess:
Definition: An abscess in the retropharyngeal space between the posterior pharyngeal wall
and the prevertebral fascia.

Etiology:
It is due to suppuration of the retropharyngeal lymph glands of
Henle present on each side of the midline. These glands tend
to atrophy about the fifth year of age and so the condition
usually occurs in infants and young children. Abscess
formation is usually due to transmission of infection from
nearby       structures.     In     adults    (especially    in
immunocompromised patients), this form of abscess may
sometimes be 2ry to foreign body in the posterior pharyngeal
wall or trauma from endoscopic procedures.

Symptoms:
    • Fever, malaise, and pallor.
    • Dysphagia (difficulty in suckling and swallowing).
    • Stridor (difficult noisy breathing).
Signs:
    • High temperature, rapid pulse, pallor, and toxemia.
    • Nuchal rigidity with tilting of the head (torticollis) toward the uninvolved side.
    • Pharyngeal congestion with swelling in the posterior pharyngeal wall limited to one
       side of the midline (due to midline attachment of the buccopharyngeal fascia
       “covering the posterior pharyngeal wall” & the prevertebral fascia).
    • Enlarged tender cervical lymph nodes.


Investigations:
Lateral soft-tissue neck radiograph (plain X-ray) confirms the
diagnosis. Characteristic findings include:
    • Abnormal thickening of prevertebral soft tissue (>50% of
       vertebral body) “red arrow in diagram”.
    • Straightening or reversal of normal cervical spine curvature.
    • Air in prevertebral soft tissue “black arrow in diagram”.
Complications:
    1. Spontaneous rupture can cause sudden death from
       aspiration.
    2. Laryngeal edema & stridor.
    3. Spread of infection to mediastinum (mediastinitis).

Treatment:
   • Systemic antibiotics, analgesics, & soft diet.
   • Incision & drainage: Vertical incision – perorally - without
      anesthesia “especially in infants”- in a head-low position while using suction to avoid
      aspiration.
   • Tracheotomy (in case of airway compromise).
5. Chronic Retropharyngeal Abscess
Definition: A cold abscess behind the prevertebral fascia due to T.B. of the cervical vertebrae
(Pott’s disease). It forms a midline swelling in the posterior pharyngeal wall.

Incidence:
Occurs later in age (middle-aged adults).

Symptoms:
    • Generalized tuberculous toxemia (loss of weight).
    • Mild fever “usually at night” – excessive sweating.
    • Dysphagia – painful neck movements.
Signs:
    • Normal temperature and pulse.
    • Tenderness along vertebrae.
    • Enlarged painless cervical lymph nodes.
    • Midline cystic fluctuant swelling on the posterior
       pharyngeal wall.

Investigations:
Neck radiographs show manifestations of a retropharyngeal
abscess and may show caries of the cervical vertebrae or calcified
tuberculous LN's.

Treatment:
General:
   • Full anti-tuberculous drug therapy.
   • Rest and good nutritious diet “rich in proteins and
       calcium”.
Local:
   • Incision & drainage: is made externally (through the neck &
       never through the mouth) along the posterior border of the
       sternocleidomastoid muscle under general anesthesia.
   • Stabilization of the cervical vertebrae in cases of spinal
       caries.




Causes of Deep Neck Infections include:
1. Peritonsillar abscess (Quinsy).
2. Parapharyngeal abscess.
3. Acute Retropharyngeal abscess.
4. Chronic Retropharyngeal abscess.
5. Ludwig’s angina.
Ludwig’s Angina
Definition: This is a rapidly spreading, potentially fatal infection involving the submandibular
space.

It is characterized by:
Rapidly spreading cellulitis, with no tendency for abscess formation, involving both
submaxillary & sublingual compartments of the submandibular space, usually bilaterally.
Spread is by direct extension along fascial planes & not by lymphatics.




Etiology:
    1. Dental or periodontal infections (70%) [Especially 2nd & 3rd lower molar teeth].
    2. Penetrating injuries of the floor of mouth e.g. stab wounds, gunshot wounds, horse
       kick …etc.
    3. Mandibular fractures.

Clinical features:
    • Young patient with poor dentition.
    • Unilateral neck pain & swelling that soon becomes
       bilateral.
    • Increasing edema & brawny induration of the suprahyoid
       soft tissues & floor of mouth that results in thrusting of
       the tongue against the palate with resultant respiratory
       embarrassment.
    • Increasing fever, neck rigidity, trismus & odynophagia.
    • Many patients progress rapidly from onset of symptoms to
       respiratory obstruction in 12-24 hrs.

Treatment:
   • Intensive I.V. antibiotic therapy.
   • Early airway maintenance (by nasal endotracheal
      intubation or tracheotomy).
   • Rapid surgical intervention:
      A horizontal submental incision just above the hyoid bone
      under general anesthesia. The mylohyoid muscle should
      be incised vertically to enable bilateral drainage of both the submaxillary & sublingual
      spaces. A straw-colored exudate, rather than true abscess fluid is usually released.
