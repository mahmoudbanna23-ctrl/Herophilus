STRIDOR
• Difficult noisy breathing due to partial airway obstruction at or
  immediately below the larynx
• It should be distinguished from
        STERTOR : low pitched snoring or snuffly sound (always inspiratory)
      noisy breathing due to airway obstruction at nasal &/or pharyngeal level
        WHEEZING : whistling sound, mainly expiratory, arising from lower
       airway obstruction - asthma, COD, foreign body in trachea etc..

• Inspiratory – Biphasic - Expiratory

                 Laryngomalacia
           malacia = abnormal flaccidity

• The most common congenital anomaly of the larynx and
 most frequent cause of stridor in children.

• Accounts for 60% of laryngeal problem in infants

• Boys : Girls = 2:1

• More common in premature infants.
                           Laryngomalacia
                         Clinical presentation

1. Inspiratory stridor:
• Begins in the first few days or weeks of life.
• Intermittent, mild-moderate, harsh and crowing.
• Worse with crying, feeding, sleeping (on back), and flexion of the neck.
• Improve with extension of the neck, lying prone and elevation of the mandible
2. Normal cry.
3. Normal general health .

4. No aspiration.
                   Laryngomalacia
                      Diagnosis


1. Clinical picture.
2. Endoscopic examination of the larynx:
• Flexible endoscopy under local anesthesia.
• Rigid direct endoscopic examination under GA
  (during induction and recovery)
3. Radiological examination:
• No diagnostic radiological feature.

                    Laryngomalacia
                     Management

1. Assurance of the parents.
2. Conservative treatment
3. Surgical treatment: indicated in
• Severe stridor with failure to thrive.
• Weight loss, chest deformity (Pectus excavatum).
• Cyanotic attacks, Cor pulmonale.
• Hypoxemia and hypercapnia.
                 Laryngomalacia
                Surgical treatment


I) Laser Supraglottoplasty
• Division of the aryepiglottic fold.
• Partial amputation of the epiglottis.
• Removal of the redundant supra-arytenoid
  mucosa and lateral borders of epiglottis.
II) Tracheostomy.
                 Vocal Cord Paralysis

• Congenital VC paralysis accounts for 10% of
  congenital laryngeal lesion.
• Bilateral : Unilateral = 1:4
                            Vocal Cord Paralysis
                                  Etiology
I) Unilateral CVC paralysis: (Lt > Rt)
• Congenital cardiovascular anomalies e.g. VSD, Fallot’s teratology, PDA,…..etc.
• Surgery for T-E fistula, congenital heart disease, cervical esophagostomy.
II) Bilateral CVC paralysis:
• Central causes as meningomyelocele, Arnold-Chiari malformation, bulbar palsy.
• Birth trauma e.g.prolonged 2nd stage of labor, forceps delivery.
• Hereditary. (very rare)
                                    Vocal Cord Paralysis
                                    Clinical presentation
I) Unilaterala CVC paralysis:
• Weak breathy cry.
• Aspiration of pharyngeal secretions.
• Choking and cyanotic attacks during feeding (rare).
II) Bilateral CVC paralysis:
• Inspiratory stridor:
     • Immediately after birth.
     • Severe (usually needs tracheotomy)
     • Improve by sleeping and worse on activity.
                           Vocal Cord Paralysis
                               Treatment

I) Unilateral cord paralysis:
• No treatment (resolves spontaneously).
II) Bilateral cord paralysis:
• Tracheotomy and follow-up.
• Arytenoidectomy or cordotomy.
                        Subglottic Stenosis

• Def.: “narrowing of the SG lumen less than 4 mm in full-term
  newborn or less than 3 mm in premature infant”
            (normal diameter = 4.5 mm in full term newborn)
• 3rd common congenital laryngeal anomaly.
• The most serious congenital anomaly.
• Cartilaginous / Soft tissue stenosis.
                         Subglottic Stenosis
                           Clinical picture
1. Severe form               Stridor and cyanosis immediately after birth.
2. Mild to moderate form
• Persistent croup (stridor > 3 weeks)
• Difficulty in intubation during general anesthesia.
• Difficult decannulation after tracheostomy.

• Fibrous tissue band due to incomplete recanalization of the laryngeal lumen.
• Thin membranous/fibrous/cartilaginous
• Types:
    • Glottic (most common)
    • Interarytenoid (posterior glottic)
    • Subglottic + SG stenosis.
    • Supraglottic (very rare)
                             Laryngeal Web
                          Clinical presentation


• Abnormal cry or voice.
• Respiratory distress and stridor:
    • Thick large web.
    • SG stenosis (1:3)
• Normal feeding.

• Def.: Congenital vascular malformation arising from mesodermal resets.
• SG region in the larynx         narrowing of the airway.
• 50% associated with hemangioma of the head and neck.
• Female : male = 2:1.
                 Laryngeal Hemangioma
                        Diagnosis


• Inspiratory stridor at 6
 months of age.
• Hemangioma in other
 areas in H & N (50%).
• Endoscopy.
• Imaging.
                        Laryngeal Hemangioma
                              Treatment
1. Tracheotomy and follow-up for spontaneous regression (2-4 ys.).
2. Corticosteroid (systemic or intralesional)
3. Laser endoscopic surgery.
4. Interferon.
5. Cryosurgery.
6. External irradiation.
7. Surgical excision.

  •     Prophylactic (Neck burn, Radiotherapy of big laryng. Tumor,

      2.   Partial laryngectomy
      3.   Total laryngectomy
      4.   Laryngopharyngectomy
Technique
            Functions of Tracheotomy
1. By pass an upper airway obstruction.
2. Decrease dead space (70-100 ml) by 10-50 %
3. Decrease resistance to air flow
4. An access to the trachea for suction
5. A pathway to deliver medication and humidifications
6. Protection against aspiration
7. Allows swallowing without reflex apnea
8. Decrease power of the cough

  • Low tracheotomy ( 5th & 6 th rings)

Care for Tracheostomy

• A tracheostomy needs extra care because it is a much more
  direct route to the lungs and therefore the air moving into
  the lungs will not have the benefit of the warming,
  moistening and filtering effect of the nasal passages.
• It needs special care to prevent it’s blocking with secretions.
• The tapes will need changing daily or when they become
  dirty or wet.
• The tube will need to be changed regularly depending on the
  type.
