7.14 · CLINICAL SCENARIO

A 4-year-old boy has periorbital oedema that is worst on waking, ascites, 4+ proteinuria, albumin 14
g/L, normal blood pressure, normal complement and normal renal function. What is the correct
treatment?

 A. Renal biopsy first
 B. Oral prednisolone 60 mg/m²/day for 4 weeks, then 40 mg/m² on alternate days
 C. Furosemide alone
 D. Cyclophosphamide
 E. Antibiotics only

Answer: B.

All five typical features are present, so this is steroid-sensitive nephrotic syndrome (85–90% of cases).
Median time to protein-free urine is 11 days; biopsy only if there is no response after 4–6 weeks or if
features are atypical.




7.15 · SINGLE BEST ANSWER

A child with nephrotic syndrome develops abdominal pain. Urinary sodium is 8 mmol/L and the
haemoglobin is high. What does this indicate?

 A. Hypervolaemia — give more diuretic
 B. HYPOVOLAEMIA — give IV 0.9% saline or albumin
 C. Constipation
 D. Appendicitis
 E. Steroid side-effect

Answer: B.

The child looks waterlogged but is intravascularly depleted: urinary sodium <10 mmol/L and a raised
haematocrit are the clues. Untreated it risks thrombosis and shock; diuretics make it worse. Spontaneous
pneumococcal peritonitis is the other cause of abdominal pain here.
