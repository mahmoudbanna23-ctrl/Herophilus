6.13 · SINGLE BEST ANSWER

Which finding distinguishes aortic stenosis from pulmonary stenosis?

 A. An ejection click
 B. A carotid thrill
 C. An ejection systolic murmur
 D. A normal chest X-ray
 E. Post-stenotic dilatation

Answer: B.

Aortic stenosis: upper RIGHT sternal edge, radiates to the neck, carotid thrill ALWAYS, slow-rising
small-volume pulse, soft delayed A2. Pulmonary stenosis: upper LEFT sternal edge, no carotid thrill.
Intervention when the gradient exceeds 64 mmHg.




6.14 · CLINICAL SCENARIO

A previously well term baby collapses at 48 hours with grey mottled skin, absent femoral pulses and a
pH of 7.05. What is the single most important immediate treatment?

 A. Prostaglandin E infusion
 B. Nebulised adrenaline
 C. Oral propranolol
 D. Furosemide
 E. Adenosine

Answer: A.

Duct-dependent coarctation — normal on day 1, collapse when the duct closes. Resuscitate, give
prostaglandin at the earliest opportunity, cover for sepsis, and refer to a cardiac centre for early surgery.




6.15 · SINGLE BEST ANSWER

Which findings suggest ADULT-type coarctation of the aorta?

 A. Absent femoral pulses with shock in the neonate
 B. Right arm hypertension, radio-femoral delay, rib notching and a "3 sign"
 C. Fixed split second heart sound
 D. Continuous murmur below the left clavicle
 E. Egg-on-side heart

Answer: B.

This lesion is NOT duct dependent and worsens over years; the leg pulse is delayed because blood arrives
through chest wall collaterals. Treatment is stent insertion or surgery.
