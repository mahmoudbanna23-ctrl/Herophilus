Definition of Cerebral Palsy

Umbrella term for a permanent disorder of movement and/or posture and motor
function due to a non-progressive abnormality of the developing brain; often accompanied
by disturbances of cognition, communication, vision, perception, sensation, behaviour, seizure
disorder and secondary musculoskeletal problems.

Epidemiology & Aetiology

   •   MOST COMMON CAUSE of motor impairment / disability in children.

   •   The cause can be identified only in 50% of cases.

   •   Certain factors in the history can increase risk (incidence in those with one or more RISK
       FACTORS is higher than those without).

   •   Prematurity and Low birth weight are the two most important risk factors.

   •   Postnatal risk factors are more important in developing countries.

Distribution of Causes (Developed Countries)

   •   Antenatal: ~80%
   •   Perinatal (e.g., birth asphyxia): ~10%
   •   Postnatal: ~10%

Prevalence:

   •   Developed countries: 1.5–2.5 per 1000 live births
   •   Developing countries: 1.5–5.6 per 1000 live births

       Timing-Based Risk Factors

       Prenatal (Conception → Onset of Labor)

   •   Prematurity
   •   Low birth weight
   •   Intrauterine infections (TORCH)
   •   Placental insufficiency
   •   Maternal diseases (e.g., preeclampsia, diabetes)
   •   Cortical migration disorders
   •   Structural brain maldevelopment
   •   Thrombotic disorders
   •   In vitro fertilization
   •   Small for gestational age



       Perinatal (28 Weeks Gestation → 7th Day of Life)

   •   Hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE)
   •   Birth asphyxia
   •   Obstructed labor
   •   Premature rupture of membranes
   •   Neonatal sepsis or meningitis
   •   Intracranial hemorrhage
   •   Kernicterus



       Postnatal (Birth → First 2 Years)

   •   CNS infections (meningitis, encephalitis)
   •   Severe dehydration (± hypernatremia)
   •   Intracranial trauma (including child abuse)




CHANGING PICTURE OF CEREBRAL PALSY

   •   There are significant racial and SES disparities in the prevalence of CP; the
       prevalence of CP is 70% higher in low/middle income families than high income
       families.

   •   Most individuals with CP are now living well into adulthood.

   •   Adults with CP experience a premature ‘aging’, with chronic pain, extreme fatigue,
       osteoporosis, and osteoarthritis – resulting in an early loss of ambulation and
       independence.

Classifications

1. Etiological classification:

    •   No single cause for CP, it is multifactorial.

    •   The most common single factor is prematurity / Low birth weight.

    •   Timing-Based Risk Factors:

A – Pre-natal risk factors / causes (conception to onset of labour):

    1. prematurity

    2. intrauterine infections (TORCH).

    3. congenital structural CNS malformations and cortical migration disorders.

    4. eclampsia or pre-eclampsia.

    5. placental insufficiency.

    6. uterine bleeding.

    7. small for gestational age.

    8. uncontrolled gestational DM.

    9. maternal chronic diseases as renal failure.

    10. in vitro fertilization (IVF) babies are at increased risk for CP.

B - Perinatal risk factors / causes (28 Weeks Gestation to 7th Day of Life):

    1. hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy / birth asphyxia.

    2. respiratory anoxia e.g. respiratory distress.

    3. obstructed labour.

    4. premature rupture of membranes.

    5. kernicterus

    6. neonatal sepsis and meningitis

    7. intracranial haemorrhage.

C - Postnatal risk factors / causes (birth to first 2 years):

   1. CNS infection as meningitis or encephalitis.

   2. intracranial trauma e.g. child abuse.

   3. severe dehydration with or without hypernatremia.
2 & 3. Anatomical and Clinical Classifications:

Based on the site of brain involvement:

   •   Pyramidal: involvement of motor area (4) and paramotor area (6) leading to UMNL
       manifestations. e.g spastic CP.

   •   Extrapyramidal: involvement of basal ganglia. e.g. dyskinetic CP.

   •   Cerebellum and its connections: leading to ataxic CP.

     quadriplegia, diplegia) are no longer used. Instead, Surveillance of Cerebral Palsy in
     Europe (SCPE) Classification is the used on:
      Spastic CP (≈ 90%)
   • Increased tone
   • Hyperreflexia
   • Pathological reflexes
   • Babinski sign
      Subtypes:

       Dyskinetic CP (≈ 6%)
   •   Dystonia
   •   Chorea
   •   Athetosis
   •   Fluctuating tone
   •   Primitive reflex patterns

       Ataxic CP (≈ 4%)
   •   Loss of coordinated movement
   •   Abnormal force, rhythm, and accuracy

  5. Functional Classification (gross motor functional level): using Gross Motor Functional
     Classification System (GMFCS).

       Gross Motor Function Classification System:
       Level        Description

       I            Walks without limitations

       II           Walks with limitations

       III          Walks with assistive device

       IV           Limited self-mobility; may use powered mobility
      Level         Description

      V             Dependent; transported in manual wheelchair

  6. Clinical Types and Features:
     Unilateral CP (Hemiplegia)
   • Often due to perinatal stroke
   • One side affected (arm > leg)
   • Possible visual field defects
   • Risk of seizures and learning difficulties
   • Usually GMFCS I–II

      Bilateral Spastic CP — Diplegia
  •    Common in prematurity
  •    Legs more affected than arms
  •    Toe walking and scissoring gait
  •    Good cognition and communication in many cases
  •    Often GMFCS I–III

      Bilateral Spastic CP — Quadriplegia
  •    Extensive brain damage
  •    All limbs involved
  •    Associated impairments common:
            o Intellectual disability
            o Feeding problems
            o Speech difficulties
            o Vision and hearing deficits
            o Seizures
  •    High risk of hip dislocation and scoliosis
  •    Often GMFCS IV–V

      Dyskinetic CP
  •   Often due to perinatal asphyxia or kernicterus
  •   Involuntary movements
  •   Feeding difficulties common
  •   Cognition may be preserved
  •   Frequently GMFCS IV–V




Management

  •   Parents should be given details of the diagnosis as early as possible.

  •   Prognosis is difficult during infancy until the severity and pattern of evolving signs and
      the child’s developmental progress have become clearer over several months or years of
      life.
   •   Children with CP are likely to have a wide range of associated medical, psychological
       and social problems, making it essential to adopt a multidisciplinary approach to
       assessment and management.



Principles of Management

   •   For aetiology

   •   For Associated impairments / secondary problems



Investigations

   1. Neuroimaging
      Recommended if aetiology is not established. MRI, when available, is preferred to CT
      (higher yield of type and timing of insult).

   2. Metabolic or genetic testing
      not routinely needed Unless suspected different diagnosis (inherited neuro-metabolic
      or degenerative disease…loss of acquired milestones, organomegaly, dysmorphic
      features).

   3. Testing for Congenital Infections
      TORCH screening is needed if suspected congenital infection to prevent further sibling
      affection.

   4. Coagulation studies
      For unexplained cerebral infarction on imaging and hemiplegic CP, to diagnose a
      hereditary coagulation disorder.

   5. Associated conditions

          o      An EEG should not be done for the purpose of determining aetiology of CP but
                 for findings in history to suggest epileptic seizures.

          o      Because of the high incidence of associated conditions, children with CP should
                 be screened for:

                    ▪   Intellectual disability (IQ test)

                    ▪   Ophthalmologic and hearing impairments (visual and hearing exam)

                    ▪   Speech and language disorders (speech and language assessment)

                    ▪   Swallowing, Nutrition and growth (barium studies. Etc)

                    ▪   Secondary joint dislocation or fracture (skeletal X ray)



Treatment Strategy

   •   Problems of Severe Spasticity in CP

   •   Tasks of Spasticity for the Caregiver
   •   Current Treatment Options of Spasticity

Treatment approaches should be in an integrated rather sequential way.

           o   Exercise and physical therapy

           o   Chemo-denervation injections by Botulinum Toxin into spastic muscles

           o   Systemic oral anti-spasticity drugs

           o   Intra-thecal baclofen

           o   Orthopaedic (tendon lengthening) or neurosurgical methods (selective dorsal
               rhizotomy or deep brain stimulation)



Team Approach for Management

Local Neurodevelopment Team:

   •   Neurodevelopment paediatrician / paediatric neurologist

   •   Physiatrist

   •   Physiotherapist, occupational therapist

   •   Speech therapist, psychologist

   •   Audiologist, ophthalmologist

   •   Nutritionist, dietician

   •   Social worker, nurse, educational advisor

   •   Surgeons (orthopaedics – neurosurgery – GIT..etc)

Effective management requires coordination among these team members.



Paediatrician’s Role in the Management of Cerebral Palsy

   •   Medical Management:

           o   Treatment of spasticity by either systemic oral drugs (tizanidine, baclofen,
               diazepam) or by Motor point intra-muscular injections (Botulinum toxin type A)
               or by Intra-thecal baclofen (not available in Egypt).

           o   Medical treatment for associated problems (seizures, GORD, failure to thrive,
               sleep & behaviour problems).

           o   Coordination of care and follow up of developmental progress of the child.



Therapists’ Role: (Physio, O.T., Speech, Orthotics)

   •   As soon as possible
   •   Both at home and centre-based

   •   Specialist knowledge of child development & CP

   •   Close liaison between team members

   •   Work with Clearly defined goals and specific outcomes

   •   Focus on empowering child & family
