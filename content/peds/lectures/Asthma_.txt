                                Childhood Asthma

Definition

Asthma is a heterogeneous disease of chronic airway inflammation which is
defined by the history of recurrent symptoms of wheeze, cough, shortness of
breath and chest tightness, that vary in intensity and over time together with
variable expiratory airflow limitation which is reversible.

Airflow limitation may be persistent later in the course of the disease. Asthma is
usually associated with airway hyperresponsiveness and airway inflammation,
but they are not essential to make the diagnosis.

   - Asthma is heterogenous as it is a polygenic disease, with multiple clinical
     asthma phenotypes, and it is a variable disease.
   - Asthma is a variable disease. Variability is over time (day and night,
     seasons in the year), intensity and in airflow limitation.
   - Expiratory airflow: it is the airflow pattern as measured by spirometry and
     pulmonary functions.
   - associated with a trigger
   - In case of atopic asthma which is the most common clinical type, it may be
     associated with other allergies and patients may have family history of
     atopy.

Epidemiology
- Asthma is the most common chronic respiratory diseases worldwide
  • In Egypt:
              • School age: 6.5 – 20%
              • Adults: 4.4 – 6.7%
       – Worldwide: WHO report 2022: 9% of children and 11% of
          adolescents have asthma
– Asthma has a high impact on people’s lives, 1/3 to 1/2 of children, adolescents
  and adults with asthma have severe symptoms that regularly interfere with
  everyday life such as awaking from sleep, morning coughing, effort
  intolerance, and absence from school
- Asthma affects all community groups, both genders and all socioeconomic
  classes.

Pathophysiology
Asthma is a type TH2-mediated immune response which represents a skewing
from the normal TH1-mediated immune response.




Diagnosis of asthma:

A) Clinical Presentation:
Asthma symptoms:
   • Wheezing
   • Non-productive
   • Shortness of breath
   • Chest tightness
All these symptoms are typically:
   • variable over time and vary in intensity
   • often worse at night or on waking
   • often triggered by exercise, laughter, allergens, cold air
   • worsen after end-exercise (very distinctive)
   • often appear or worsen with viral infections

Asthma signs
   • May be normal between attacks.
   • Inspection:
        – Hyperinflation of the chest: in long-standing asthma
        – Harrison sulci: in cases where the onset of the disease was in early
           childhood and left without treatment
   • Palpation: decreased chest expansion
   • Percussion: hyper-resonant note
   • Auscultation:

                                        2
        – Decreased breath sounds bilaterally
        – Vesicular breathing with prolonged expiration
        – generalized polyphonic (musical) expiratory wheeze, expiratory or
          inspiratory or both, and if severe the child may suffer a silent chest
   • Evidence of atopy: nasal, skin, eye
   • Growth assessment: usually normal

Clinical asthma phenotypes:

Allergic asthma: This is the most common and easily recognized asthma
phenotype. It often starts in childhood and is associated with a past and/or family
history of allergic disease such as eczema, allergic rhinitis, or food or drug allergy.
It usually responds well to ICS treatment. The cellular profile of this type is
usually eosinophilic

Non-allergic asthma: asthma that is not associated with allergy. It usually has
less response to ICS. The cellular profile of the sputum of these patients may be
neutrophilic, eosinophilic or contain only a few inflammatory cells.

Cough variant asthma and cough predominant asthma: cough may be the only
symptom of asthma, and evidence of airflow limitation may be absent. They
respond well to ICS. Some patients later develop wheezing and bronchodilator
responsiveness.

Asthma with persistent airflow limitation: in longstanding poorly controlled
asthma, some patients develop persistent irreversible airflow limitation. This is
thought to be due to airway wall remodeling.

Asthma with obesity: a different pattern of AW inflammation may be present in
obese asthmatics.

Adult-onset (late-onset) asthma: presentation with asthma for the first time in
adulthood. It tends to be non-allergic, and often requires higher doses of ICS

Red flags of Asthma
  • Chronic wet productive cough
  • Finger clubbing
  • Growth faltering
  • Chronic diarrhea
Any of these flags suggest a condition characterized by chronic infection such as
cystic fibrosis, immunodeficiency, or PCD
   • Chest pain à pulmonary embolism

   • Cardiac murmur, FTTà CHD/heart failure

Differential Diagnosis: Causes of recurrent or persistent childhood wheeze
   • Viral episodic wheeze /Multiple trigger wheeze
   • Asthma
   • Recurrent anaphylaxis (e.g. in food allergy)
   • Chronic aspiration
   • Cystic fibrosis
   • Bronchopulmonary dysplasia
   • Tracheo-bronchomalacia
   • Foreign body aspiration

Diagnosis

Age < 6y
The diagnosis of asthma is based on history of typical symptoms, examination
and response to treatment. It is mainly clinical.
All the following criteria should be met:

   1- Recurrent acute wheezing episode(s):
      A history of at least two reported acute wheezing episodes in the past 12
      months
      OR
      At least one acute wheezing episode AND asthma-like symptoms between
      episodes: e.g., dry cough, coughing, symptoms worse during sleep, or after
      laughing, crying or activity.

• Acute wheezing episodes are defined as asthma-like symptoms such as
  wheezing on expiration, accessory muscle use, breathlessness, or difficult fast
  breathing that the caregiver reported had lasted for more than 24 hours or were
  confirmed as wheezing by a healthcare worker.

   2- No likely alternative cause for the respiratory symptoms (except for a
      concurrent viral respiratory infection)

   3- A timely clinical response to asthma treatment.


Age > 6y
Diagnosis of asthma relies on history of typical symptoms and sign of asthma
with confirmed variable expiratory airflow, with absence of symptoms or signs
suggesting alternative diagnoses.


                                       4
1. History of typical symptoms, examination and response to treatment
2. Confirmation of variable expiratory airflow by:
      a. Peak expiratory flow rate (PEFR) – measured with peak flow
         meters
            • The child should fully inhale before placing the mouthpiece
                between their teeth and sealing tightly with their lips à A
                sharp, fast exhalation à estimate of the maximum rate of
                exhalation
            • In poorly controlled asthma there is increased variability in
                PEFR:
            • diurnal variability (morning usually lower than evening)
            • day-to-day variability.
      b. Spirometry
            • A non-invasive measure of flow through the airways
            • Blowing out as hard and fast as possible for as long as
               possible.
            • Measures the volume expired in the first second (forced
               expiratory volume in 1 second – FEV1) and the total volume
               of expired air (forced vital capacity – FVC).
            • In asthma there is a pattern of obstructive spirometry
               i. Low FEV1 and FVC is normal à low FEV1/FVC ratio
                  <80%
              ii. In mild to moderate asthma, the child may have normal
                  spirometry when well.

      c. Increase in lung function after 4 weeks of ICS-containing treatment
      d. Positive bronchial provocation test

Choosing the correct inhaler

Pressurized metered dose inhaler (MDI)
   • Suitable for adolescents, need cooperation
   • Deep slow inspiration then breath holding
   • Increases drug deposition to the lungs and reduces oropharyngeal
      deposition à decreased systemic side effects
   • Portable, easy
   • Less useful in acute attack as it needs deep breath
   • Needs cooperation and good breath
   • limited drugs

Pressurized metered dose inhaler (MDI) and spacer
   • Suitable for all ages as it doesn’t need cooperation or effort
   • With face mask <3y, Mouthpiece ≥ 3y
   • Needs tidal breathing
   • Increases drug deposition to the lungs and reduces oropharyngeal
      deposition à decreased systemic side effects
   • Useful for acute asthma attacks when poor inspiratory effort may impair
      the use of inhalers direct to the mouth.
   • Large size, limited drugs
   • Spacers lose their efficacy with time and need replacement

Dry powder inhaler
  • Suitable for ≥6y, as it needs effort
  • Needs high flow
  • Increases drug deposition to the lungs
  • Easy to use
  • Portable
  • Not Useful in acute exacerbation as it needs effort
  • Needs effort
  • Gets clumps with time, so more deposition in the oropharynx

Nebulizer
  • Any age
  • Needs pressured air/O2 source, electricity
  • Can use O2 in the session
  • Easy to use, doesn’t need cooperation
  • Wide variety of medications
  • Useful in acute exacerbation, can supply O2
  • Least efficient
  • Largest oropharyngeal deposition
  • Very long time per session
  • Needs electricity and source of pressurized air

Add-on therapy
  • > 5 years: LABA
  • < 5 years: oral leukotriene receptor antagonist: montelukast: especially in
     combined asthma and AR
  • Antibiotics are of no value in the absence of a bacterial infection
  • cough medicines and decongestants are of no value
  • Antihistamines can be used in combined allergic rhinitis
Non-pharmacological measures
  • Avoiding smoking: inhaling second-hand cigarette smoke / e-cigarette
     vapour is the most devastating exposure to the airway
  • In children with allergy to house dust mite:
        – dust-mite-impermeable bedding may reduce exacerbations of
            asthma requiring hospitalization.
  • Allergen immunotherapy is effective for treating atopic asthma due to a
     single allergen

Steps of asthma treatment:




                                      8
   Definition of Complete control
   1. no daytime symptoms
   2. no night-time symptoms
   3. no limit on activities (including exercise)
   4. no need for reliever use
   5. normal lung function
   6. no exacerbations (need for hospitalization or oral steroids) in the previous
      6 months

   Risk factors for exacerbations
   • Poor symptom control
   • History of ≥1 exacerbation in the previous year
   • SABA-only treatment (without any ICS)
   • Over-use of SABA
   • Incorrect inhaler technique
   • Socioeconomic problems, poor adherence
   • Exposures: smoking
   • Low FEV1
   • Blood eosinophilia.

Acute asthma exacerbation

Definition: acute or sub-acute worsening in symptoms and lung function from
the patient’s usual status
   • In some cases, a patient may present for the first time during an
       exacerbation.
   • Criteria for admission to hospital
Criteria for admission to hospital
If, after high-dose inhaled bronchodilator therapy, they:
    1. have not responded adequately clinically, i.e. persisting breathlessness or
        tachypnoea
    2. are becoming exhausted
    3. still have a marked reduction in their predicted (or
    4. usual best) PFR or FEV1 (<50%)
    5. have a reduced SO2 <92% in air
