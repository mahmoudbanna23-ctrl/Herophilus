                HEMOSTASIS


 Definition
Hemostasis: derives from the Greek meaning “The
stoppage of blood flow”.
HEMOSTASIS


  Hemostasis can be divided into two stages:
          Primary and Secondary.
  Primary hemostasis includes the platelet and vascular
  response to vessel injury.

  Secondary hemostasis includes the coagulation factors
  response to such injury.

  Together, platelets, vessels, and coagulation factors
  combine to stop bleeding and allow for vessel repair
  through formation of a stable fibrin-platelet plug at
  the site of injury.

Immune thrombocytopenic
purpura
Definition:
 Autoimmune destruction
 of platelets by IgG
 autoantibodies causing
 reduced platelet count and
 increased megakaryocytes
 with no budding in the
 bone marrow
 Abnormal autoantibodies,
 usually (IgG) against one or more
 platelet membrane glycoproteins
 resulting in
 circulating autoantibody-coated
 platelets ,recognized by Fc
 receptor on splenic macrophages
 ,phagocytosed and destroyed .



The spleen is the key organ in the
pathophysiology of ITP ,
• the site of autoantibody production (white pulp)
• the site of phagocytosis (red pulp).
Etiology:-
• The cause of ITP remains unknown in most cases, but recent
  history of viral infection EBV, varicella virus, CMV, rubella
  virus, or hepatitis virus (A, B, or C).
• In children; mostly it’s an acute disease and resolves
  spontaneously within 1-6 weeks .


Epidemiology:-
  ❖Sex :- Males more than females
  ❖Age :- Any age Peak 1-6 years
Classification
• • Newly diagnosed ITP
• ≤3 months from diagnosis
• • Persistent ITP
• 3–12 months from diagnosis, including:
• Patients not reaching spontaneous remission
• Patients failing to maintain Complete Remission to therapy
• • Chronic ITP
• >12 months from diagnosis
Clinical presentation:-
➢ History of preceding viral infection 1-4 weeks before onset of
  thrombocytopenia.

➢ The classical presentation is sudden onset of petechiae and purpura
  in previously healthy child.

➢ Bleeding symptoms :
❖Epistaxis
❖Bleeding gums
❖Easily bruising
❖Very rarely intracranial hemorrhage (ICH): headache, nausea,
  vomiting, lethargy, irritability, decreased consciousness or
  neurological symptoms.
➢ Normal otherwise physical examination; presence of HSM or bone
  or joint pain suggests another diagnosis.
Laboratory investigations:-
✓ CBC :- isolated thrombocytopenia , normal platelet shape
✓ The peripheral smear may show large platelets in acute ITP
✓ Bone marrow aspirate and biopsy:-
   • Not recommended in children with typical ITP.
   • Done if no response to treatment for > 6 months ,
      associated with another cytopenia .
   • Normal cellularity of myeloid and erythroid cells with
      normal or increased number of megakaryocytes.
✓ Other lab inv should be done to exclude other causes of
  thrombocytopenia (eg, ANA to exclude SLE, Coombs test to
  exclude Evan’s syndrome).
Management
• First-line treatment options in newly diagnosed patients
• Wait and Watch (irrespective of platelet count)
• Corticosteroids (short course: prednisone)
• IVIg (single dose, alternative to corticosteroid)
• IV anti-D$ (for Rh+ non-splenectomized patients)
• Treatment options in non-responders to first-line, or in
  persistent/chronic ITP*
• TPO-RA (Romiplostim, Eltrombopag)
• Rituximab (in patients failing first-line treatment or
splenectomy)
• Splenectomy should be delayed by ≥12 months (may be given
when unresponsive to corticosteroids, IVIg, anti-D, and/or in
those who have a need for improved quality of life)

 Henoch-Schonlein purpura
Also known as IgA vasculitis is a disorder that causes the small blood vessels
in your skin, joints, intestines and kidneys to become inflamed and bleed.
he most striking feature of this form of vasculitis is a purplish rash, typically on
the lower legs and buttocks.
Symptoms

 The four main characteristics of Henoch-Schonlein purpura include:

• Rash (purpura) : Reddish-purple spots that look like bruises develop on
  the buttocks, legs and feet. The rash can also appear on the arms, face
  and trunk and may be worse in areas of pressure .

• Swollen, sore joint : People with Henoch-Schonlein purpura often have
  pain and swelling around the joints — mainly in the knees and ankles.
  Joint pain sometimes precedes the classical rash by one or two weeks.

• Digestive tract symptoms : Many children with Henoch-Schonlein
  purpura develop belly pain, nausea, vomiting and bloody stools. These
  symptoms sometimes occur before the rash appears.

• Kidney involvement : Henoch-Schonlein purpura can also affect the
  kidneys. In most cases, this shows up as protein or blood in the urine,
  which you may not even know is there unless you have a urine test done.
Histopathology
 Renal biopsy is the
 most definitive tool
 to diagnose both
 IgAV with nephritis
 and IgA
 nephropathy. Both
 are likely to show

Complications
The most serious complication of Henoch-Schonlein
purpura is kidney damage. This risk is greater in adults
than in children. Occasionally the damage is severe
enough that dialysis or a kidney transplant is needed.

Bowel obstruction In rare cases, Henoch-Schonlein
purpura can cause intussusception — a condition in
which a section of the bowel folds into itself like a
telescope, which prevents matter from moving through
the bowel.

                     Emicizumab

Emicizumab is a subcutaneous humanized bispecific IgG4
monoclonal antibody that mimics a key function of activated FVIII
(FVIIIa): bridging FIXa and the FX zymogen to accelerate
activation of the latter.
The antibody recognizes FIX/IXa with one Fab arm and FX/Xa
with the other.
Emicizumab is not recognized by FVIII-neutralizing allo inhibitory
antibodies, so it remains effective in their presence and is now
licensed for bleed prophylaxis in hemophilia A patients, both with
and without FVIII inhibitors.

VON-WILLEBRAND DISEASE
 The most common inherited bleeding disorder affecting
 0.1 - 1% of the population.
  Caused by a deficiency (either quantitative or functional)
 in von Willebrand Factor (vWF).
 Affects males and females
VON-WILLEBRAND DISEASE
 Characterised by easy bruising, bleeding from mucous
 membranes (particularly epistaxis, oral mucosa,
 menorrhagia) and post-op bleeding.
 Role of vWF:
   Mediates platelet adhesion to damaged endothelium
   Carrier protein for factor VIII
   Essential for proper clotting
Types of VWD
 Type 1: Partial quantitative deficiency (most common)
 Type 2: Qualitative defect (subtypes: 2A, 2B, 2M, 2N)
 Type 3: complete deficiency (most severe)
VWD type I
 VWD type I causes a mild to moderate quantitative
 deficiency of vWF (ie, about 20-50% of normal levels).
 type I is usually inherited in autosomal dominant fashion
 the most common type, accounting for 60–80% of all
 VWD patients.
 Typical symptoms include mucosal bleeding, such as
 epistaxis and menorrhagia, as well as easy bruising and
 potentially surgical bleeding.
 Diagnostic guidelines use a VWF level, as measured by
 the VWF antigen assay
VWD type II
 vWD type II is due to qualitative vWF abnormalities and is
 subdivided into types IIA, IIB, IIN, and IIM.
 vWD type IIA, the most common qualitative abnormality
 of vWF, is associated with selective loss of large and
 medium-sized multimers.
VWD type III
 Patients with vWD type III, a severe, quantitative
 deficiency associated with very little or no detectable
 plasma or platelet vWF, have a profound bleeding
 disorder.
 vWD type III appears to result from the inheritance of a
 mutant vWF gene from both parents.
Diagnosis
 Personal and family bleeding history
 Laboratory tests:
 vWF antigen
 Ristocetin cofactor activity (vWF:RCo)
 Factor VIII levels
 Multimer analysis (for type 2)
Management
 Antifibrinolytics (Tranexamic acid)Often helpful for
 mucosal bleeding (most common form of bleeding) -
 mouth, epistaxis, menorrhagia. May be given alone or as
 adjunct therapy to Desmopressin/factor concentrate
 Desmopressin (DDAVP) for Type 1 (stimulates vWF
 release)
 vWF/factor VIII concentrates for more severe types
 Hormonal therapy for menstrual bleeding in teens
Prognosis
 Most children lead normal lives with proper management
 Regular follow-up with hematologist
 Monitoring for complications like anemia
