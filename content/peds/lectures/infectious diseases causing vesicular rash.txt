                                                      Chickenpox
Chickenpox (Varicella) is a highly contagious viral infection historically common in
childhood, though it can affect individuals of any age. While usually a mild, self-limiting
disease in healthy children, it can lead to severe or life-threatening complications in
adults, neonates, and the immuno-compromised.

        National Centre for Infectious Diseases

Causative Agent
Chickenpox is caused by the Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV), also known as human
herpesvirus 3 (HHV-3). VZV is a DNA virus of the herpesvirus family. After the initial
infection is resolved, the virus remains latent in the sensory nerve ganglia for life and can
reactivate later to cause herpes zoster (shingles).
Department of Health, Victoria

Incubation and Infectivity Periods
    •      Incubation Period: Typically, 10 to 21 days after exposure, with an average of
           14–16 days.

    •      Infectivity Period: A person is contagious from 1 to 2 days before the rash
           appears until all the blisters have dried and become scabs. This typically takes
           about 5 to 7 days from the onset of the rash.

           Centers for Disease Control and Prevention | CDC


Clinical Picture
The disease progresses through a prodromal phase followed by a characteristic
eruptive phase.
   • Prodrome: Often more pronounced in adults, this phase lasts 1–2 days and
       includes fever, malaise, headache, loss of appetite, and muscle aches. In
           children, the rash is often the first visible sign.
  •   The Rash (Exanthem):

          o    Distribution: Typically starts on the face, scalp, or trunk before
               spreading centripetally to the limbs.

          o    Morphology: Progresses rapidly from small red spots (macules) to raised
               bumps (papules) and then to clear, fluid-filled blisters (vesicles)
               described as "dew drops on a rose petal".

          o    "Crops": Lesions appear in waves over 3–5 days, meaning an individual
               will simultaneously have spots at all developmental stages (macules,
               vesicles, and scabs).

          o    Mucous Membranes: Painful ulcers (enanthem) can also occur in the
               mouth, nose, and genital area.

               Centers for Disease Control and Prevention | CDC


Complications
  •   Bacterial Superinfection: The most common complication in children, often
      caused by Staphylococcus or Streptococcus due to scratching.

  •   Pneumonia: The most common serious complication in adults and pregnant
      women.

  •   Neurological: Encephalitis (brain inflammation) and acute cerebellar
      ataxia (loss of coordination).

  •   Pregnancy & Neonatal Risks: Infection in early pregnancy (before 20 weeks)
      can lead to fetal varicella syndrome (limb hypoplasia, scarring). Maternal
      infection around delivery can cause severe neonatal varicella.

  •   Reye Syndrome: A rare but fatal condition linked to using aspirin during the
      infection.

      Department of Health, Victoria +6


Treatment and Prevention
  •   Treatment: Primarily supportive of healthy children:

          o    Symptom Relief: Calamine lotion or antihistamines for itching;
               acetaminophen for fever. Aspirin must be avoided.
           o   Antivirals: Oral or intravenous acyclovir may be prescribed for high-risk
               patients (adults, pregnant women, immunocompromised) if started within
               24 hours of rash onset.

   •    Prevention:

           o   Vaccination: The Varicella vaccine is highly effective (approx. 90-98%)
               and usually given in two doses.

           o   Isolation: Keeping infected individuals at home until all lesions have
               scabbed over to prevent spread.

           o   Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP): Vaccination within 3–5 days of
               exposure can prevent or lessen the disease. High-risk individuals may
               receive Varicella-Zoster Immune Globulin (VARIZIG).

               WA Health


       herpes zoster
Zona, more commonly known as shingles or herpes zoster, is a painful viral infection
resulting from the reactivation of a dormant virus. Unlike chickenpox, which is the
primary infection, shingles occur later in life when immunity wanes.
Causative Agent
The disease is caused by the Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV), a member of the
herpesvirus family. After a person recovers from chickenpox, the virus remains latent in
the dorsal root ganglia (nerve tissues near the spinal cord). Reactivation can be
triggered by aging, stress, or a weakened immune system.
Medscape

Incubation and Infectivity Periods
   •    Incubation Period: Technically, shingles have no incubation period because it
        is a reactivation of a virus already present in the body. However, if a person is
        exposed to VZV for the first time (leading to chickenpox), the incubation period is
        typically 10 to 21 days.

   •    Infectivity Period: A person with shingles is infectious from the moment
        the rash appears until all blisters have dried and crusted over, which usually
        takes 7 to 10 days. Transmission occurs through direct contact with blister fluid; it
       can cause chickenpox in those who are non-immune, but it cannot directly cause
       shingles in another person.

Clinical Picture
The progression of shingles is typically divided into three distinct stages:
   1. Prodrome: Lasting 1 to 5 days before the rash, this phase involves localized
      burning, tingling, itching, or stabbing pain in a specific area of the skin. Some
       may also experience fever, headaches, or malaise.

   2. The Rash (Acute Eruptive Phase):

           1. Distribution: The rash is characteristically unilateral (one side of the
              body) and follows a dermatome (a band of skin supplied by a single
               spinal nerve). It most commonly appears on the torso or face.

           2. Morphology: It starts as red patches that quickly develop into clusters of
              fluid-filled vesicles. These blisters eventually burst, ulcerate, and scab
               over within 2 to 4 weeks.

   3. Chronic Phase: For some, pain persists long after the rash has healed.

       World Health Organization (WHO)


Complications
   •   Postherpetic Neuralgia (PHN): The most common complication, where severe
       nerve pain lasts for months or years after the rash clears.

   •   Herpes Zoster Ophthalmicus: Shingles involving the eye can cause
       permanent vision loss.

   •   Ramsay Hunt Syndrome: Reactivation in the facial nerves can lead to facial
       paralysis and hearing loss.

   •   Bacterial Superinfection: Open blisters can become infected with bacteria
       like Staph or Strep.

       Medscape


Treatment and Prevention
   •   Treatment: Antiviral medications—such as acyclovir, valacyclovir, or
       famciclovir—are most effective when started within 72 hours of rash onset.
    Pain management may include over-the-counter analgesics, nerve pain
    medications (gabapentin), or topical numbing patches.

•   Prevention: The CDC Shingles Vaccine Recommendation is two doses of
    the Shingrix vaccine for adults aged 50 and older. To prevent spreading the
    virus, patients should keep the rash covered and avoid contact with pregnant
    women or immunocompromised individuals until scabbing is complete.
                                Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV)
Herpetic gingivostomatitis is the most common clinical manifestation of a
primary Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV) virus, typically occurring in children between 6
months and 5 years of age. While often self-limiting, it presents as a debilitating oral
infection that can lead to severe dehydration and systemic distress.
The Royal Children's Hospital

Causative Agent
The primary cause is Herpes Simplex Virus Type 1 (HSV-1), a double-stranded DNA
virus with a specific tropism for oral epithelium. Although less common, HSV-2 can also
cause gingivostomatitis in adults, often transmitted through oro-genital contact.
National Institutes of Health

Incubation and Infectivity Periods
     •    Incubation Period: Typically ranges from 2 to 12 days, with an average of
          about 4 to 8 days following contact with an infected individual.

     •    Infectivity Period: Patients are most contagious when active vesicles are
          present. Children typically shed the virus for at least one week and sometimes
          for several weeks (median of 3 weeks). The virus can also be shed
          asymptomatically throughout life.

          World Health Organization (WHO)


Clinical Picture
The disease is characterized by a sudden onset and a progression through several
phases:
   • Prodrome: Lasts about 1 to 4 days before oral lesions appear. Symptoms
          include high-grade fever, malaise, irritability, headache, and decreased appetite.

     •    Oral Manifestations (The "Rash"):

               o    Vesicles & Ulcers: Numerous small (2–5 mm), fluid-filled vesicles appear
                    on the gingiva (gums), tongue, buccal mucosa, and palate. These
                    rapidly rupture to form painful, irregular ulcers with a yellowish-grey center
                    and a red halo.

               o    Gingivitis: The gums become fiery red, swollen, and friable, bleeding
                    easily even with slight contact.
           o    Associated Signs: Severe halitosis (bad breath), drooling, and painful
                cervical or submandibular lymphadenopathy are common.

                National Institutes of Health (NIH) |


Complications
  •   Dehydration: The most common complication due to refusal to drink because of
      extreme oral pain.

  •   Autoinoculation: The virus can spread to other body parts, leading to Herpetic
      Whitlow (fingers, often from thumb sucking) or Herpetic Keratitis (eyes), which
      can cause vision damage.

  •   Neurological: In rare cases, the virus can lead to life-threatening HSV
      encephalitis or meningitis.

  •   Secondary Infection: Bacterial superinfection of the ulcers may occur if oral
      hygiene is severely neglected.

      National Institutes of Health


Treatment and Prevention
  •   Treatment: Primarily supportive, focusing on pain management and hydration.

           o    Supportive Care: Acetaminophen or ibuprofen for fever/pain. Soft, cold,
                and non-acidic foods/fluids are recommended.

           o    Antivirals: Oral Acyclovir (15 mg/kg for children, 800 mg for adults) is
                effective if started within 72 hours of symptom onset to shorten the
                duration of fever and lesions.

           o    Topical Agents: Viscous lidocaine or diphenhydramine rinses may
                provide temporary local numbing for older children and adults.

  •   Prevention:

           o    Avoid Contact: Refrain from kissing or sharing utensils, toothbrushes, or
                toys with someone who has active cold sores or oral lesions.

           o    Hygiene: Frequent handwashing and disinfecting toys in childcare
                settings.
                 Hand, Foot, and Mouth Disease (HFMD)
Hand, Foot, and Mouth Disease (HFMD) is a highly contagious viral syndrome that
primarily affects infants and children under the age of five, though it can occur in adults.
While typically mild, it is known for causing characteristic "blister-like" eruptions on
specific areas of the body.
Causative Agent
HFMD is caused by viruses belonging to the Enterovirus genus. The most frequent
culprits include:
   •   Coxsackievirus A16: The most common cause of mild cases.

   •   Enterovirus A71 (EV-A71): Often linked to more severe disease and
       neurological complications.

   •   Coxsackievirus A6: Increasingly responsible for "atypical" HFMD involving more
       extensive rashes and nail loss.

Incubation and Infectivity Periods
   •   Incubation Period: The time from exposure to the first symptoms is usually 3 to
       6 days.

   •   Infectivity Period: Patients are most contagious during the first week of
       illness. However, the virus can remain in the respiratory tract for 1–3 weeks and
       can be shed in the stool for several weeks or months after symptoms
       disappear.

Clinical Picture
The disease follows a predictable clinical course:
   1. Prodrome: Lasting 24 to 48 hours, symptoms include a low-grade fever, sore
       throat, malaise, and loss of appetite.

   2. The Rash (Exanthem and Enanthem):

          1. Oral Lesions (Enanthem): Small red spots appear on the tongue, gums,
             and inside of the cheeks, which quickly turn into painful blisters or ulcers.
              This often makes swallowing difficult.

          2. Skin Rash (Exanthem): A characteristic rash develops on the palms of
             the hands and soles of the feet. These lesions are typically flat or raised
             red spots, sometimes with blisters (vesicles) that have a "greyish" center.
             Unlike many other rashes, these are usually not itchy but can be tender.

         3. Other Sites: The rash may also appear on the buttocks, knees, elbows, or
             genital area.

Complications
  •   Dehydration: The most frequent complication, as painful mouth sores
      discourage children from drinking fluids.

  •   Onychomadesis: Temporary loss of fingernails or toenails may occur several
      weeks after recovery, particularly with Coxsackievirus A6.

  •   Viral Meningitis or Encephalitis: Rare but serious inflammation of the brain or
      spinal cord, primarily seen with EV-A71.

  •   Acute Flaccid Paralysis: A very rare, polio-like weakness in the limbs.

Treatment and Prevention
  •   Treatment: There is no specific antiviral medication; management is supportive.

         o   Pain/Fever Relief: Use of acetaminophen or ibuprofen. Avoid aspirin in
             children.

         o   Hydration: Giving cold liquids, milkshakes, or popsicles to soothe the
             throat and maintain fluid intake.

         o   Topical Relief: Over-the-counter anesthetic mouth gels can help with oral
             pain.

  •   Prevention:

         o   Hand Hygiene: Strict handwashing protocols after changing diapers or
             using the restroom.

         o   Disinfection: Cleaning shared toys and surfaces with a chlorine-based
             bleach solution.

         o   Isolation: Keeping infected children out of school or daycare until the
             fever is gone and all blisters have dried.
                                         Herpangina
Herpangina is a common childhood illness characterized by a sudden onset of high fever
and painful oral ulcers. While it shares the same viral family as Hand, Foot, and Mouth
Disease (HFMD), its clinical manifestation is restricted to the posterior oropharynx.
Causative Agent
Herpangina is primarily caused by Group A Coxsackieviruses (serotypes 1–10, 16, and
22). Less common causes include Group B Coxsackieviruses, Echoviruses,
and Enterovirus 71. These viruses belong to the Picornaviridae family and are most
prevalent during summer and autumn months in temperate climates.
Incubation and Infectivity Periods
   •   Incubation Period: Typically ranges from 3 to 5 days.

   •   Infectivity Period: Patients are most contagious during the first week of
       symptoms. However, because the virus is shed in feces, it can be transmitted
       for several weeks after the illness has clinically resolved. Transmission occurs
       through the fecal-oral route, respiratory droplets, or contact with contaminated
       surfaces.

Clinical Picture
The presentation is usually acute and follows a rapid progression:
   • Prodrome: The illness begins abruptly with a high fever (often reaching 38.9°C
       to 40°C), sore throat, headache, and malaise. In infants and toddlers, irritability
       and vomiting are common.

   •   The Lesions (Enanthem): Unlike HFMD, herpangina does not typically feature
       a skin rash on the hands or feet.

          o   Appearance: Small (1–2 mm) vesicles appear, which rapidly enlarge and
              ulcerate to form shallow, grey-white lesions with a red halo.

          o   Location: Specifically located on the posterior oral cavity, including the
              soft palate, uvula, and tonsils. This distinguishes it from herpetic
              gingivostomatitis, which usually involves the front of the mouth and gums.

Complications
   •   Dehydration: The most frequent complication, as the severe pain (odynophagia)
       leads to the refusal of fluids.
  •   Febrile Seizures: High, rapid-onset fevers can trigger seizures in susceptible
      young children.

  •   Neurological Involvement: Very rare, but Enterovirus 71 strains can lead to
      aseptic meningitis or encephalitis.

Treatment and Prevention
  •   Treatment: Management is entirely supportive.

         o   Analgesics: Acetaminophen or ibuprofen to manage fever and pain.

         o   Hydration: Encouraging small, frequent sips of cold fluids. Acidic or salty
             foods should be avoided as they irritate the ulcers.

         o   Topical Therapy: Some clinicians suggest "magic mouthwash" (a mix of
             liquid antacid and diphenhydramine) for older children to coat the sores.

  •   Prevention:

         o   Handwashing: Rigorous hand hygiene, especially after diaper changes,
             is the most effective CDC prevention strategy.

         o   Exclusion: Children should stay home from school or daycare until the
             fever has subsided for 24 hours.
