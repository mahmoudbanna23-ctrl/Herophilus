Bronchiolitis is an acute viral respiratory infection involving the terminal and
respiratory bronchioli in infants, resulting in small airways obstruction. There is
difference between European and American Academy of Pediatric guideline
de�inition of acute bronchiolitis.

European guideline definition: a seasonal viral illness in infants <12 months of
age characterized by nasal discharge, cough, tachypnoea, retractions and bilateral
crackles.
American Academy of Pediatric guideline definition: bronchiolitis as a disorder in
infants <24 months of age that is most commonly caused by a viral lower respiratory tract
infection characterized by wheezing

These definitions reflect differences in how the disease is interpreted, and the North Rearly
wheezy bronchitis

Etiology of bronchiolitis
   1. RSV 62%
   2. Human bocavirus
   3. Rhinovirus
   4. Human metapneumovirus                       38%
   5. Influenzas A and B viruses
   6. Parainfluenza 1-3

Risk factors for severe bronchiolitis are age <3 months, male sex, low socioeconomic
conditions, maternal smoking and RSV infection. Other risk factors for severe bronchiolitis
are prematurity with BPD, and coexisting comorbidities, such as cardiovascular diseases,
immunodeficiency and chronic respiratory diseases. The only known protective factor is
maternal breastfeeding.

RSV viral structure
   •   Single-stranded, RNA genome; 10 genes encoding 11 proteins
   •   2 key surface glycoproteins allow RSV to infect host cells:
           •   G protein: attachment to mucosal epithelium of nose or eyes
           •   F protein: fusion with epithelial cell membranes
                   1. Entry into host cell
                   2. Fusion with adjacent cells (syncytia formation)
    •   Variation in the G glycoprotein yields:
        a) 2 major subtypes, Groups A and B
        b) Different strains within each group
                 I. 12 genotypes of RSV-A
                II. 20 genotypes of RSV-B

The global pediatric burden of RSV disease
    •   RSV is the leading cause of severe respiratory infections
    •   Global incidence RSV LRTI (lower respiratory tract infection)
                   1. 22% LRTI due to RSV
                   2. 33.8 million RSV worldwide
    •   RSV is a Leading cause of pediatric hospitalization (3.6 million)
                   1. < 6 months of age (39%)
                   2. 6 months to 5 years (61%)
    •   Important cause of pediatric mortality
                   1. < 6 months (46%)
                   2. 6 month – 5 years (54%)
    •   Mortality burden > 99% of mortality in low-income countries
    •   Risk factors
                   1. Mortality is 30 times more in HIV infection
                   2. Mortality id 13 times more in VLBW <1500 gm compared to > 2500 gm
                   3. Mortality is 4.3 times more in primary immune deﬁcient patients

RSV infections
          1. Upper respiratory tract infection
          2. Lower respiratory tract infections (5%)
          3. Severe RSV illness (bronchiolitis and pneumonia)

RSV Transmission
   •   RSV is transmitted:
          • By droplets, large particles, and fomites
          • Directly – Contact with infectious secretions of an infected individual
          • Indirectly – Contact with infectious secretions in the environment
   •   RSV can survive for 6–12 hours or more on hard, nonporous surfaces
   •   Nosocomial infection remains a major problem

Period of incubation and infectivity
   •   RSV incubation period is 2–8 days
   •   Viral shedding typically occurs for:
           • 1–2 days in adults
           • Average of 9 days in infants
           • Up to 4 weeks in critically ill infants

High risk for severe bronchiolitis

   1. Infants born prematurely (<35 weeks’ gestation)
   2. <3 months of age at presentation
   3. Decreased hydration and feeding (<50% of usual fluid intake in preceding 24 h)
   4. Hemodynamically significant cardiac disease
   5. Chronic lung disease
   6. BPD
   7. Neuromuscular disorder
   8. Down syndrome
   9. Immunodeficiency
   10. Environmental factors: exposure to tobacco smoke and or air pollution
   11. Social factors: distance from the hospital or difficulty to access to the hospital; poor
       social circumstances; unreliable parents or parents do not able to spot red flag
       symptoms

Pathogenesis of RSV bronchiolitis
   1. Following the incubation period, RSV replicates in and destroys ciliated epithelial
      cells that line the airways
   2. Resulting in either complete obstruction with resultant atelectasis or Bal valve
      effect with resultant alveolar overinflation
   3. Both resulted in V/Q mismatch with resultant hypoxemia


Diagnosis of Acute Bronchilitis

   • Bronchiolitis is diagnosed on clinical grounds alone
   • In infant < 12 months with exposure of child or adult with RVI will
     develop coryza and signs of LRI
     RD, Low SpO2, rales and rarely wheezing in severe cases

   • Incubation period 1-5 days followed by URT symptoms (coryza for 3 days)
     followed by LRTI symptoms. Fever can be present during the coryza period
     and absent when LRTI starts.
   • The risk of reduction of food intake, dehydration and severe respiratory
     symptoms usually peaks between 3-5 days after the onset of rhinitis
   • Chest radiographs and blood tests are required only if suggested by clinical
     indications
   • ABG analysis is recommended only in more severe cases.
   • Rapid virus detection could reduce antibiotic use and is important for
     cohorting


Indications of hospital admission
   1. Respiratory distress

   2. Apnea

   3. Tachypnea

   4. Oxygen requirement

   5. Poor feeding

   6. Dehydration

   7. Requirements for continuous clinical airway clearance

   8. underlying chronic disease,
   9. inappropriate social and family conditions



Indication of PICU admission
   1. Failure to maintain SpO2 >92% with oxygen therapy,
   2. Deteriorating respiratory status with exhaustion
   3. Recurrent apnea



Management of Acute Bronchiolitis
In mild case:
   •   Breastfeeding should be supported in a small frequent feed
   •   With Severe bronchiolitis NG feeding or IV hydration to prevent dehydration is not
       >80% of daily requirement
   •   Cleaning the upper airways with saline is an important step in the initial
       management of infants with bronchiolitis

In severe cases

The major discharge criteria are:
   1. SpO2 that remains stable at 94% in room air in the absence of respiratory distress
      for 4 h including during sleeping, and
   2. Adequate daily oral intake (>75% of usual intake) at a level to prevent dehydration,
   3. Followed by adequate parental care and family education about the potential
      duration of acute symptoms
