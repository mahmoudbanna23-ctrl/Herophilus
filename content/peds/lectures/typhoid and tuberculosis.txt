                                                 Typhoid fever

Typhoid fever (enteric fever) is a systemic, potentially life-threatening infection that
primarily affects children in developing regions.
World Health Organization (WHO)

Causative Organism
    •    Salmonella Typhi: The primary agent, a Gram-negative bacterium belonging to
         the Salmonella enterica species.

    •    Salmonella Paratyphi: Serotypes A, B, and C cause a similar but generally
         milder illness known as paratyphoid fever.

         Cleveland Clinic


Clinical Presentation (Pediatric Focus)
Presentation varies significantly by age, often starting 6–30 days after exposure.
Infectious Disease Advisor

    •    General Symptoms: Stepwise high fever (up to 40°C/104°F), headache,
         malaise, and loss of appetite.

    •    Infants & Toddlers (<5 years): Frequently present with nonspecific symptoms
         like diarrhea, vomiting, and febrile seizures.

    •    Older Children: More likely to experience abdominal pain, tenderness,
         and constipation.

    •    Physical Signs:

              o   Rose Spots: Transient pink macules on the chest/abdomen (seen in
                  <20% of cases).

              o   Organomegaly: Hepatosplenomegaly (enlarged liver and spleen) is
                  common.

              o   Relative Bradycardia: A pulse slower than expected for the degree of
                  fever.

                  National Institutes of Health (NIH) | (.gov)
Complications
Occur in 10–15% of hospitalized children, typically in the 2nd or 3rd week.
www.pidjournal.com

    •   Gastrointestinal: Intestinal hemorrhage or perforation (terminal ileum),
        paralytic ileus, and acalculous cholecystitis.

    •   Neurological: Typhoid encephalopathy (delirium, coma), meningitis, and ataxia.

    •   Other: Myocarditis, pneumonia, and hepatitis (transaminitis).

        Pediatric EM Morsels


Diagnostic Investigations
    •   Culture (Gold Standard):

             o    Blood Culture: Most sensitive in the 1st week; requires 5 mL of blood in
                  children.

             o    Bone Marrow Culture: Most sensitive (80–95%) and remains positive
                  even after starting antibiotics.

    •   Serology:

             o    Widal Test: Measures O and H antibodies; often unreliable due to cross-
                  reactivity and false positives.

             o    Rapid Tests: Typhidot (IgM/IgG) and TUBEX are faster but have variable
                  accuracy.

    •   Supportive Tests: CBC may show leukopenia (low WBC) or leukocytosis in
        young children; mild elevation of liver enzymes is common.

        National Institutes of Health (NIH) | (.gov)


Treatment
    •   Antibiotics: The mainstay of treatment. Common choices include:

             o    Azithromycin or Ceftriaxone for empiric treatment.

             o    Ciprofloxacin (fluoroquinolone) where susceptibility is confirmed.

             o    Carbapenems for extensively drug-resistant (XDR) strains.
  •   Supportive Care: Oral or IV rehydration and fever management.

  •   Adjuncts: High-dose dexamethasone may be used for severe encephalopathy
      or shock.

      Centers for Disease Control and Prevention | CDC (.gov)


Prevention
  •   Vaccination:

          o    Typhoid Conjugate Vaccine (TCV): Recommended by WHO for children
               from 6 months of age.

          o    Oral (Ty21a) and Injectable (ViPS) vaccines are available for older
               children.

  •   WASH Strategy: Ensuring Water safety, Adequate Sanitation, and Hygiene
      (e.g., handwashing, boiling water, peeling fruit).

      World Health Organization (WHO)
                                        Tuberculosis (TB)

Tuberculosis (TB) in children is a complex, often "paucibacillary" (low bacterial load)
disease that requires a high index of suspicion due to its varied and often non-specific
presentations.
1. Causative Organism
   •   Mycobacterium tuberculosis Complex: The primary agent is M.
       tuberculosis (human TB). Other members include M. bovis (often from
       unpasteurized dairy) and M. africanum .

   •   Characteristics: Acid-fast, slow-growing, aerobic bacilli that spread through
       airborne droplets.

       MDPI


2. Clinical Presentation in Pediatrics
Presentation varies significantly by age and immune status:
   • Infants (<2 years): Highest risk for rapid progression to severe, disseminated
      disease. Symptoms are often non-specific: poor feeding, failure to thrive,
       lethargy, or persistent "pneumonia" that fails to respond to standard antibiotics.

   •   Children (2–10 years): Often asymptomatic or "oligosymptomatic" (mild
       symptoms). May present with a chronic, non-productive cough (>2 weeks), low-
       grade fever, and weight loss.

   •   Adolescents (>10 years): Often show "adult-type" TB with productive cough,
       night sweats, and cavitary lesions on chest X-ray.

   •   Extrapulmonary TB (EPTB): More common in children than adults (up to 25%).

          o   Lymphadenitis (Scrofula): Most common EPTB; painless, firm, matted
              cervical lymph nodes.

          o   TB Meningitis: Medical emergency; presents with irritability, vomiting,
              headache, and neck stiffness.

          o   Miliary TB: Disseminated disease with "millet seed" spots on X-ray;
              presents with hepatosplenomegaly and respiratory distress.

              National Institutes of Health (NIH) |
3. Complications
  •   Pulmonary: Bronchial obstruction (from enlarged nodes), bronchiectasis, and
      permanent lung scarring.

  •   Neurological: Hydrocephalus, stroke, and permanent disability (deafness,
      blindness, or intellectual impairment) following meningitis.

  •   Skeletal: Pott’s Disease (spinal TB) leading to "gibbus" (humpback) deformity
      and possible paraplegia.

  •   Abdominal: Intestinal obstruction and tuberculous peritonitis.

      National Institutes of Health (NIH) |


4. Diagnostic Investigations
  •   Screening: Tuberculin Skin Test (TST/Mantoux) or Interferon-Gamma
      Release Assays (IGRA). IGRA is preferred in BCG-vaccinated children to avoid
      false positives.

  •   Radiology: Chest X-ray (CXR) looking for hilar lymphadenopathy (lateral view is
      essential) or miliary patterns.

  •   Microbiology:

           o    Gastric Aspirate: Used for children who cannot expectorate; collected
                over 3 consecutive mornings.

           o    Induced Sputum: Using hypertonic saline nebulization.

           o    Stool Testing: A newer WHO-recommended child-friendly specimen
                for GeneXpert Ultra.

  •   Molecular: GeneXpert MTB/RIF Ultra is the preferred initial test to detect TB
      DNA and rifampicin resistance.

      Centers for Disease Control and Prevention | CDC (.gov)
5. Treatment Regimens
Treatment is divided into an Intensive Phase (2 months) and a Continuation
Phase (4–10 months).
Condition                              Intensive Phase (2 months)   Continuation Phase   Total Duration


Non-severe Pulmonary TB                HRZ (E)                      HR                   4–6 Months


Severe/Cavitary Pulmonary              HRZE                         HR                   6 Months


TB Meningitis / Bone TB                HRZE + Ethionamide           HR                   12 Months

   •   Key: H (Isoniazid), R (Rifampicin), Z (Pyrazinamide), E (Ethambutol).

   •   Complications:

            o    Meningitis/Pericarditis: Add Corticosteroids (e.g., Prednisolone) for 4–
                 6 weeks to reduce inflammation and pressure.

            o    Hydrocephalus: May require a Ventriculoperitoneal (VP) Shunt.

                 National Institutes of Health (NIH) |


6. Prevention
   •   BCG Vaccine: Given at birth; prevents severe forms like miliary TB and
       meningitis but has variable efficacy against adult-type pulmonary TB.

   •   TB Preventive Treatment (TPT): For children exposed to active cases.
       Common regimens include 3 months of weekly Isoniazid + Rifapentine (3HP) or
       4 months of daily Rifampin (4R).

   •   Contact Tracing: Systematically screening all household contacts of an index
       case.

       National Institutes of Health
