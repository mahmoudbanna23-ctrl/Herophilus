The Forgotten Killer of Children




KEY FACTS
   •   Leading cause of death in children worldwide.
   •   Kills an estimated 1.4 million children under age of ﬁve every years (more than
       AIDS, TB, and malaria combined).
   •   Can be prevented by immunization, adequate nutrition and addressing
       environmental factor.
   •   Only 30% children with pneumonia receive the antibiotics they need.


DEFINTION
Inﬂammation of the lungs that is caused by infectious organisms or physical or
chemical irritants.( PNEUMONIA VS PNEUMONITIS).

Acute inﬂammatory consolidation of alveoli or inﬁltration of the interstitial tissue of
the lung with inﬂammatory cells, or combination of both.




CLASSIFICATION

Clinical Classiﬁcation: primary without underlying cause or secondary to
pulmonary disease as bronchiectasis or extrapulmonary diseases like aspiration in
cerebral palsy.

  •   Community Acquired Pneumonia (CAP ) which is infectious pneumonia in a
      person who has not recently been hospitalized ,mostly caused by
      Pneumococci, H.inﬂuenzae, viruses and atypical bacteria.
  •   Hospital-Acquired Pneumonia ( HAP ) or nosocomial pneumonia which Is

  •   Ventilator Associated Pneumonia ( VAP )is a subset of nosocomial
      pneumonia which occurs after at least 48 hours of intubation and mechanical
      ventilation .

Pathogenesis of pneumonia
 Micro-organisms gain access to respiratory tract through:
  • 1- Aspiration of oropharyngeal contents.
  • 2- Inhalation of aerosolized particles.
  • 3- Entry via blood stream.

Occurrence of the disease will depend on:
  • Inoculum size
  • Virulence of the organism
  • Host defenses.


Factors aCecting host defenses:

   •   Injury to muco-cilliary apparatus.
   •   Loss or suppression of cough reﬂex.
   •   Interference with phagocytic function of alveolar macrophages.
   •   Pulmonary congestion and edema.
   •   Accumulation of secretions.



Following entry of organism into the lower respiratory tract and alveolar spaces
outpouring of edema ﬂuid and accumulation of PNL the organism multiply in edema
ﬂuid and spread of infection from one alveolus to another.
Tissue response:

   •   The inﬂammatory response to Streptococcus pneumoniae or Haemophilus
       inﬂuenzae often produces lobar consolidation, but these infections rarely
       result in tissue necrosis .
   •   Staphylococci and many Gram-negative bacilli often produce necrosis, which
       can lead to cavitation and even frank abscess formation; a peribronchial
       distribution is characteristic, but lobar consolidation may occur.
   •   Viruses generally produce interstitial inﬂammation rather than air-space
       exudates. The infection is usually bilateral and causes di_use alveolar damage
       and interstitial edema. Similar tissue responses may be initiated by
       Mycoplasma, Chlamydia, and Legionella species.
   •   Mycobacteria and fungi typically evoke a slow granulomatous response.

Etiology of infective Pneumonia:

Child Age is a good predictor of the likely pathogen.
Neonate younger than 3 weeks:
Infection is through vertical transmission from the mother during birth.
Group B Streptococci and Gram negative enterococci and bacilli are the most
common bacterial pathogens.
Three weeks to 3 months:
Most often bacterial in origin, and Streptococcus pneumoniae is the most common
pathogen
Four months to ﬁve years:
Viruses are the most frequent cause of pneumonia in preschool-aged children.
 Streptococcus pneumoniae & H. inﬂuenzae are the most common bacterial
pathogens.
Five years to Adolescence:
Pneumococci ,Mycoplasma and Chlamydophila pneumoniae often are the etiologic
agents in children older than ﬁve years and in adolescence.
In General: Streptococcus pneumoniae is the most common bacterial cause of CAP
after the neonatal period. Less common bacterial etiologies include haemophilus
inﬂuenzae type B, Moraxella catarrhalis, and Staphylococcus aureus.
Clinical Manifestations:
General Signs:
Tachypnea, fever, dyspnea, grunting, cyanosis
Nasal ﬂare, chest indrawing and tachycardia
WHO age speciﬁc criteria for tachypnea
    • Infants aged < 2 months: RR > 60 breath/min.
    • Infants 2-12 months: RR > 50 breath/min.
    • Children >12months - 5years: RR > 40 breath/min.
    • Children > 5 years: RR > 30 breath/min.
Local Signs:
Lobar pneumonia:
- Stage of Congestion: Decrease AE, Vocal
  resonance, TVF, and presence of ﬁne and crackling rales.
    - Stage of hepatization: Decrease chest movement
  increase TVF, VR, decrease AE, bronchial breathing, no
  rales and dullness to percussion.
    - Stage of resolution:rales reappear,
  decrease in BB and decrease in dullness.
Bronchopneumonia:
Main feature is the presence of medium sized consonating crepetations , with the
general signs of pneumonia. if conﬂuent , signs of consolidation will be present.



Some features of speciﬁc oragnisms:
Staphylococcal pneumonia:
At the onset is indistinguishable from pneumococcal pneumonia.
Almost exclusively a disease of infants but can complicate inﬂuenza in older children.
 Staphylococcal often cause tissue necrosis and lead to cavitation, abscess formation
and pyopneumothorax in rapid sequence .
Viral pneumonia:
Preceded by URTI, gradual onset, dry cough,low grade or no fever, dyspnea and
retractions.Clinical examination is unrevealing due to absence of excudate and there
may be wheezing.
Mycoplasma Pneumonia:
Gradual onset, non-productive paroxysmal cough that turn productive.
Severity of symptoms > clinical ﬁndings.
Non-pulmonary symptoms ( vomiting, diarrhea, myalgia, arthritis, skin rash,
myocarditis, hemolytic anemia and meningeo-encephalitis).
Course is prolonged than viral pneumonia and it occurs in school aged children.

Diagnostic testing:
In the community
There is no indication for any test in a child with pneumonia in the community
 in Hospitals :
Mostly commonly done tests are chest X ray, CBC, blood culture , serum electrolytes ,
viral studies , C reactive Protein, Arterial blood gases and ESR.
    • Pulse oximetry:
    • Pulse oximetry should be performed in every child admitted to hospital with
        pneumonia .
    • Acute phase reactants:
    • Acute phase reactants do not distinguish between bacterial and viral infections
        in children and should not be measured routinely [A].
    • Urea and electrolytes:
    • Urea and electrolytes to assess electrolyte imbalance is undertaken if the
        patient is severely ill or shows evidence of dehydration


Chest X Ray
Can not reliably di_erentiate between bacterial and viral pneumonia.
Bacterial pneumonia: Consolidation with air bronchogram.
Viral pneumonia: Hyperexpansion, parahilar and peribronchial
inﬁltrate, atelectasis and hilar adenopathy.
   Follow up Chest-X Ray: Generally is not required with simple consolidation or in
   patients who recover well rapidly. Repeat Chest X-Ray is required only in kids with
   lobar collapse , atelectasis after 4-6 weeks. Kids who do not recover clinically need
   follow up by Ultrasonography or X ray to rule out parapneumonic e_usion.

TREATMENT:
Home treatment
  • Families of children who are well enough to be cared for at home need
      information on managing pyrexia, preventing dehydration, and identifying any
      deterioration.
  • The child cared for at home should be mild pneumonia and must be reviewed by
      a general practitioner if deteriorating, or if not improving after 48 hours on
      treatment .
Hospitalization
  • All infants from birth to 3 weeks.
  • Infants 3 weeks to 3 months of age with fever.
  • All children who appear toxic.
  • Children with signs of severe disease and saturation < 92%.
General management
  • Oxygen if saturation less than 92%.
  • Hydration and Fluid therapy.
  • Fever and pain.
  • Monitoring.

ANTIMICROBIAL THERAPY:

   •   Whether to treat with antibiotics.
   •   Choice of antibiotic.
   •   Route of administration.
   •   Switching from parenteral to oral antibiotics.
   •   Duration of antibiotic treatment.

ANTIMICROBIAL THERAPY:

   •   Early empiric therapy is important to decrease mortality. Once organism is
       identiﬁed , Spectrum has to be narrowed.
   •   Duration of treatment is 7-10 days or longer in certain cases.
   •   Think in Viruses, S.aureus, Anaerobes or multiresistant organisms if no
       response to initial antimicrobial.

Empiric Antimicrobial for presumed Viral pneumonia

   •   Viral pneumonia caused by inﬂuenza A may be treated with Rimantadine or
       Amantadine, while viral pneumonia caused by inﬂuenza A or B may be treated
       with Oseltamivir or Zanamivir. These treatments are beneﬁcial only if they are
       started within 48 hours of the onset of symptoms.
   •    There are no known e_ective treatments for viral pneumonias caused by the
       SARS coronavirus, adenovirus, hantanvirus, or parainﬂuenza virus.
Oral versus Intravenous Antibiotics:
   • Intravenous : Is needed to achieve high concentration of drug in blood and
       tissues to ensure eradication of the organism.
   • Oral: Can be used in mild cases.
   • Switch therapy: shorter hospital stay; cost savings in drugs, nursing time, and
       IV solutions; fewer hospital-related complications, such as phlebitis or falls;
       and greater patient satisfaction.

Prevention

   •  Cessation of smoking: is important not only because it helps to limit lung
      damage, but also because cigarette smoke interferes with many of the body's
      natural defenses against pneumonia.
Prevention

   •   In newborn: testing and treating pregnant women for Group B Streptococci and
       Chlamydia trachomatis. Also suctioning oropharynx to prevent meconium
       aspiration.

   •   Vaccination: Against Pneumococci and Haemophilus inﬂuenzae in children and
       against Inﬂuenza .

Global action plan for the prevention and control of pneumonia (GAPP)
   • Protect: promote breast feeding, hand washing and reduce indoor air pollution.
   • Prevent: with vaccination.
   • Treat: every sick child has access to the right kind of care

Complications of Pneumonia:

If a child remains pyrexial or unwell 48 hours after start of treatment , re-evaluation is
necessary with consideration given to possible complications such as empyema or
abscess.
