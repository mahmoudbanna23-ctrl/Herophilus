                                      Antibiotic resistance

Antibiotic resistance is a natural evolutionary phenomenon accelerated by human
activity, leading to "superbugs" that survive drugs designed to kill them. It is currently
one of the top 10 global public health threats, directly responsible for over 1.2 million
deaths annually.
World Health Organization (WHO)

Key Bacteria with Developed Resistance
Health organizations like the World Health Organization (WHO) and the CDC classify
resistant bacteria by threat level:
    •   Urgent Threats (Critical Priority):

             o   Carbapenem-Resistant Enterobacterales (CRE): Often called
                 "nightmare bacteria," these include E. coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae that
                 are resistant to nearly all available antibiotics.

             o   Carbapenem-Resistant Acinetobacter baumannii: A major cause of
                 hospital-acquired pneumonia and bloodstream infections.

             o   Drug-Resistant Neisseria gonorrhoeae: Now showing resistance to
                 cephalosporins, the last-line treatment for gonorrhea.

    •   Serious Threats:

             o   Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA): Resistant to
                 penicillin-like antibiotics; a common cause of skin and life-threatening
                 bloodstream infections.

             o   Vancomycin-Resistant Enterococcus (VRE): Frequently causes
                 infections in healthcare settings.

             o   Drug-Resistant Streptococcus pneumoniae: Leading cause of bacterial
                 pneumonia and meningitis.

             o   Multidrug-Resistant Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MDR-B): Requires
                 longer, more toxic, and more expensive treatments.

Primary Causes of Resistance
Resistance is driven by both biological and socio-economic factors:
   1. Overuse and Misuse:

          1. Inappropriate Prescribing: Using antibiotics for viral infections like colds
              or the flu where they are ineffective.

          2. Patient Non-compliance: Stopping a course of antibiotics early allows
              the strongest bacteria to survive and multiply.

   2. Agricultural Practices:

          1. Growth Promotion: Up to 80% of antibiotics in some regions are used in
             livestock to promote growth rather than treat disease, creating a reservoir
              of resistant bacteria that reaches humans through food.

   3. Environmental Factors:

          1. Poor Sanitation & Hygiene: Inadequate handwashing and waste
              management facilitate the spread of resistant strains.

          2. Global Travel: Rapid movement of people allows resistant bacteria to
              cross borders quickly.

An Antimicrobial Stewardship Program (ASP) is a systematic, multi-disciplinary
approach in healthcare settings designed to optimize the use of antimicrobials. The
primary goal is to ensure the "5 Rights" of antibiotic use: the right patient, right drug,
right dose, right route, and right duration.
Core Objectives
   1. Improve Patient Outcomes: Ensure the fastest and most effective cure for
       infections.

   2. Enhance Patient Safety: Reduce adverse drug events, such as C.
       difficile infections or allergic reactions.

   3. Combat Resistance: Slow the development of multidrug-resistant organisms
       (MDROs) by reducing unnecessary drug pressure.
  4. Reduce Costs: Lower healthcare spending by eliminating unnecessary
     prescriptions and reducing hospital stays.

     Impact of ASPs
When implemented correctly, ASPs have been shown to reduce antibiotic use by 20–
40% without compromising patient safety, and they significantly decrease the incidence
of healthcare-associated infections.
