Types of rash

1. Primary Morphology (Shape and Structure)
These terms describe the individual lesions before they change due to scratching or
healing.
Geeky Medics

    •   Macule: A flat, discolored spot less than 1 cm (e.g., freckles or early measles). If
        larger than 1 cm, it is called a patch.

    •   Papule: A solid, raised bump less than 1 cm (e.g., warts or insect bites).

    •   Plaque: A broad, raised, flat-topped area, often formed by many papules joining
        together (e.g., psoriasis).

    •   Vesicle: A small, clear, fluid-filled blister less than 1 cm (e.g., chickenpox or
        herpes). If larger than 1 cm, it is called a bulla.

    •   Pustule: A small, raised lesion filled with pus (e.g., acne or impetigo).

    •   Wheal (Hive): A temporary, itchy, raised area caused by swelling in the skin,
        often with a pale center (e.g., allergic reactions).

          MSD Manuals +8


2. Secondary Morphology (Arrangement and Changes)
These describe how the rash evolves or spreads.
  MSD Manuals +1

    •   Annular: Ring-shaped with a clear center (e.g., ringworm).

    •   Linear: Lesions that form a straight line, often from scratching or certain plants
        like poison ivy.

    •   Target (Iris): Concentric rings like a bull's-eye (e.g., Lyme disease or Erythema
        multiforme).

    •   Crust (Scab): Dried fluid, blood, or pus on the skin surface (e.g., the "honey-
        colored" crust of impetigo).

    •   Scale: Flaky, silver, or white dead skin cells heaped on the surface (e.g.,
        psoriasis or fungal infections).
    •   Umbilicated: A raised bump with a central "dimple" or indentation (e.g.,
        molluscum contagiosum).

          MSD Manuals +2


  3. Distribution (Where it appears)
    •   Generalized: Spread randomly over most of the body (e.g., viral exanthems).

    •   Symmetrical: Appearing similarly on both sides of the body.

    •   Dermatomal: Following a specific nerve path on one side (e.g., shingles).

    •   Acral: Affecting the distal parts of the body like hands, feet, or ears.

                                                        Measles
Measles (Rubeola) is a highly contagious, acute viral respiratory illness that remains a
leading cause of vaccine-preventable death globally, especially among young children.
Centers for Disease Control and Prevention | CDC.

Causative Agent
Measles is caused by the measles virus, an enveloped, single-stranded RNA virus in
the genus Morbillivirus of the family Paramyxoviridae. Humans are the only natural
reservoir; the virus is transmitted primarily via airborne droplets or direct contact with
respiratory secretions.
National Institutes of Health.

Incubation and Infectivity
     •    Incubation Period: Typically, 10 to 14 days (range 7–21 days) from exposure to
          the onset of the rash. Fever usually begins around day 10–12.

     •    Infectivity Period: Highly contagious from 4 days before to 4 days after the
          appearance of the rash.

          Centers for Disease Control and Prevention | CDC.


Clinical Picture
The disease progresses through distinct stages:
1. Prodrome (2–4 days)
Characterized by a stepwise increase in fever (often peaking at 103°F–105°F) and the
"three Cs": Cough, Coryza (runny nose), and Conjunctivitis.
Centers for Disease Control and Prevention | CDC (.gov) +1

Koplik Spots: Pathognomonic tiny white-to-bluish specks on a red base appear on the
buccal mucosa (opposite the molars) 1–2 days before the rash.
 National Centre for Infectious Diseases +1


2. Exanthem (Rash Stage)
The characteristic maculopapular (morbilliform) rash appears about 14 days after
exposure.
Centers for Disease Control and Prevention | CDC.

     •    Pattern: Begins at the hairline and face, then
          spreads cephalocaudally (downward) to the neck, trunk, and eventually the
          extremities (including palms and soles in severe cases).
     •    Evolution: Initially discrete lesions that later become confluent, especially on
          the face and upper body. As it fades (after 5–6 days), it may leave a coppery-
          brown discoloration and fine skin peeling (desquamation).

          National Centre for Infectious Diseases


Complications
Complications occur in approximately 30–40% of cases.
National Institutes of Health

     •    Common: Otitis media (7–9%), diarrhea (8%), and pneumonia.

     •    Severe: Pneumonia is the leading cause of measles-related death in children.
          Neurological issues include acute encephalitis (1 in 1,000 cases) which can lead
          to permanent brain damage or deafness.

     •    Long-term: Subacute Sclerosing Panencephalitis (SSPE) is a rare (1 in
          10,000–100,000), fatal, progressive brain degeneration occurring years after
          infection.

          Centers for Disease Control and Prevention | CDC


Treatment and Prevention
     •    Treatment: There is no specific antiviral therapy. Management is supportive:
          hydration, antipyretics (avoiding aspirin in children), and treating secondary
          bacterial infections. Vitamin A supplementation is recommended for all
          pediatric cases to reduce mortality and eye damage.

     •    Prevention: The primary strategy is the MMR (Measles, Mumps, Rubella)
          vaccine, typically given in two doses (at 12–15 months and 4–6 years).

     •    Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP): Unvaccinated individuals may receive the
          vaccine within 72 hours or immune globulin (IG) within 6 days of exposure to
          prevent or attenuate the disease.

          National Institutes of Health
                                               German Measles
Rubella, commonly known as German Measles or "three-day measles," is a viral
infection that is generally mild in children and adults but can be devastating if contracted
during pregnancy.
Mayo Clinic +1

Causative Agent
Rubella is caused by the rubella virus, an enveloped, positive-sense, single-stranded
RNA virus. It is the sole member of the genus Rubivirus within the family Matonaviridae.
Humans are the only known reservoir for the virus.
Centers for Disease Control and Prevention | CDC (.gov) +2

Incubation and Infectivity
    •    Incubation Period: Typically, 14 to 17 days, with a broader range of 12 to 23
         days from exposure to the onset of symptoms.

    •    Infectivity Period: Patients are most contagious from 7 days before to 7 days
         after the appearance of the rash. Notably, infants with congenital rubella
         syndrome can shed the virus in their secretions for up to a year or more after
         birth.

         National Institutes of Health (NIH)


Clinical Picture
Approximately 25% to 50% of rubella infections are asymptomatic or subclinical.
Centers for Disease Control and Prevention

1. Prodrome
In children, the rash is often the first sign of illness. In adolescents and adults, a mild
prodrome lasting 1–5 days usually precedes the rash. Cleveland Clinic
    • Symptoms: Low-grade fever (<39°C/102.2°F), headache, malaise, mild
         conjunctivitis, and upper respiratory symptoms (coryza).

    •    Lymphadenopathy: A hallmark feature is tender post-auricular, occipital, and
         posterior cervical lymphadenopathy, which typically appears 5–10 days
         before the rash.

    •    Forchheimer Spots: Small, red spots (petechiae) may sometimes be seen on
         the soft palate, though these are not pathognomonic.
       Centers for Disease Control and Prevention | CDC


2. Rash (Exanthem)
The rubella rash is typically finer and less bright than the measles (rubeola) rash.
   •   Appearance: A pink or light-red maculopapular eruption.

   •   Pattern: It begins on the face and neck and
       spreads cephalocaudally (downward) to the trunk and extremities, usually
       becoming generalized within 24 hours.

   •   Duration: The rash characteristically fades by the third day ("three-day
       measles") in the same order it appeared, without the staining or skin peeling
       seen in measles.

       Centers for Disease Control and Prevention | CDC


Complications
While acute illness is usually benign, complications can occur:
  • Arthralgia and Arthritis: Common in adult women (up to 70%), usually affecting
       small joints of the hands.

   •   Rare Complications: Thrombocytopenic purpura (bleeding issues) and
       encephalitis (1 in 5,000–6,000 cases).

   •   Congenital Rubella Syndrome (CRS): The most severe complication. Infection
       during the first trimester carries an 85–90% risk of fetal damage,
       including deafness, cataracts, and heart defects (the "classic triad").

       National Institutes of Health (NIH)


Treatment and Prevention
   •   Treatment: There is no specific antiviral therapy. Management is strictly
       supportive, including rest, fluids, and acetaminophen for fever.

   •   Prevention: The MMR (Measles, Mumps, Rubella) vaccine is the standard of
       care. It is a live-attenuated vaccine typically given in two doses: the first at 12–15
       months and the second at 4–6 years of age.

   •   Screening: Women of childbearing age should have their immunity confirmed
       via serology before pregnancy, as the vaccine is contraindicated during
       pregnancy.
Congenital Rubella Syndrome (CRS) is a devastating constellation of physical
defects resulting from maternal infection with the rubella virus during pregnancy. Unlike
the mild postnatal "German Measles," the intrauterine version is a major cause of
preventable birth defects and fetal death.
Incubation and Infectivity Period
   •   Incubation: There is no "incubation period" for the syndrome itself; rather, it
       depends on the timing of maternal infection. The risk to the fetus is highest
       during the first trimester (up to 90% risk of defects if infected before 12 weeks
       gestation).

   •   Infectivity: Infants with CRS are highly infectious. They can shed the virus in
       nasopharyngeal secretions and urine for months, and sometimes up to one
       year after birth. This poses a significant risk to healthcare workers and pregnant
       women in contact with the infant.

Clinical Picture
Unlike postnatal rubella, CRS does not have a traditional "prodrome and rash" phase for
the infant. Instead, it presents as a multisystemic developmental catastrophe.
1. The Classic Triad (Gregg’s Triad)
   1. Cataracts/Glaucoma: Leading to "white pupils" and potential blindness.

   2. Sensorineural Deafness: The most common single finding in CRS.

   3. Cardiac Defects: Specifically Patent Ductus Arteriosus (PDA) and pulmonary
       artery stenosis.

2. "Blueberry Muffin" Rash
While not a standard maculopapular rash, infants often present with extramedullary
hematopoiesis. This appears as purplish-red spots on the skin, known as a "blueberry
muffin" rash, representing the skin's attempt to produce blood cells because the bone
marrow is compromised.
3. Other Findings
   •   Microcephaly (small head size) and intellectual disabilities.

   •   Intrauterine Growth Restriction (IUGR) and low birth weight.

   •   Hepatosplenomegaly and jaundice.
Complications
Complications are often lifelong and may be delayed:
   •   Progressive Panencephalitis: A rare, late-onset neurological deterioration.

   •   Endocrinopathies: A significantly increased risk of developing Type 1 Diabetes
       Mellitus and thyroid dysfunction later in childhood or adolescence.

   •   Glaucoma: May develop years after birth.

Treatment and Management
There is no cure or specific antiviral treatment for CRS.
   • Multidisciplinary Care: Management is supportive and involves cardiac surgery
      for heart defects, cataract removal, hearing aids or cochlear implants, and
       intensive developmental therapy.

   •   Isolation: Infants must be managed with contact precautions until they are at
       least one year old or have repeated negative viral cultures.

Prevention
The only effective strategy is pre-conception immunization.
   •   MMR Vaccine: Ensuring high population immunity (herd immunity) through the
       2-dose pediatric schedule.

   •   Screening: Women of childbearing age should be screened for rubella IgG
       antibodies. If non-immune, they should be vaccinated and advised not to
       become pregnant for at least 28 days (as the vaccine is live-attenuated).

   •   No Treatment in Pregnancy: If a non-immune pregnant woman is exposed,
       Immune Globulin (IG) may be considered, but it does not reliably prevent fetal
       infection.
                                      Scarlet Fever
Scarlet Fever (Scarlatina) is a bacterial exanthem that arises as a complication of
Group A Streptococcal (GAS) infection, primarily in children aged 5 to 15 years.
Causative Agent
The disease is caused by Group A Streptococcus (Streptococcus pyogenes). The
characteristic rash is a delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction to Streptococcal
Pyrogenic Exotoxins (SPE-A, B, or C) produced by the bacteria. Only strains of GAS
that carry these erythrogenic toxins cause the rash.
Incubation and Infectivity
   •   Incubation Period: Short, typically 2 to 5 days (range 1–7 days) after exposure.

   •   Infectivity Period: Patients are highly infectious during the acute phase. With
       appropriate antibiotic treatment, they are no longer contagious after 24 hours.
       Without treatment, they may remain infectious for weeks.

Clinical Picture
The presentation is distinct and follows a predictable pattern.
1. Prodrome (Sudden Onset)
Symptoms usually begin abruptly with:
   •   High Fever and chills.

   •   Sore Throat (Pharyngitis) with beefy red tonsils and often white/yellow exudates.

   •   Headache, malaise, and abdominal pain (nausea/vomiting is common in
       children).

   •   Strawberry Tongue: Initially, the tongue has a white coating with red papillae
       (White Strawberry Tongue); after a few days, the coating peels, leaving a bright
       red, bumpy surface (Red Strawberry Tongue).

2. Rash (Exanthem)
The rash appears 12–48 hours after the fever begins.
    • Texture: Described as "sandpaper"—it is finely papular and feels rough to the
       touch.

   •   Pattern: Usually begins on the trunk and neck, spreading to the extremities. It
       blanches under pressure.
   •   Pastia Lines: The rash is more intense in skin folds (axilla, groin, antecubital
       fossa), forming linear streaks of petechiae.

   •   Circumoral Pallor: The face is flushed, but the area around the mouth remains
       characteristically pale.

   •   Desquamation: As the rash fades (after 3–7 days), the skin may peel,
       particularly on the fingertips, toes, and groin.

Complications
Complications are divided into suppurative (local spread) and non-suppurative
(immune-mediated).
   •   Suppurative: Peritonsillar abscess, otitis media, sinusitis, and pneumonia.

   •   Non-Suppurative:

          o   Acute Rheumatic Fever (ARF): A serious inflammatory disease affecting
              the heart, joints, and CNS.

          o   Post-Streptococcal Glomerulonephritis (PSGN): Kidney inflammation
              causing "cola-colored" urine.

Treatment and Prevention
   •   Treatment: The primary goal is to prevent rheumatic fever. Penicillin (oral
       Penicillin V or intramuscular Penicillin G) is the gold standard. Amoxicillin is
       often used in children for palatability. For penicillin-allergic patients,
       cephalosporins or macrolides are used.

   •   Prevention: There is no vaccine. Prevention relies on:

          o   Prompt treatment of "strep throat" to stop the spread.

          o   Hand hygiene and respiratory etiquette.

          o   Exclusion from school/daycare until the child has been on antibiotics for at
              least 24 hours and is afebrile.
                                    Roseola Infantum
Roseola Infantum, also known as Exanthem Subitum or Sixth Disease, is a
common, generally benign viral illness affecting infants and toddlers. It is most famous
for its "surprise" rash that appears just as the child seems to be getting better.
Causative Agent
The primary cause is Human Herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6), and less frequently, Human
Herpesvirus 7 (HHV-7). These are large, enveloped DNA viruses. By age 2, most
children have been infected with HHV-6 [1].
Incubation and Infectivity
   •   Incubation Period: Typically, 5 to 15 days.

   •   Infectivity Period: The virus is shed in the saliva of healthy individuals and
       infected children. It is most contagious during the febrile phase (the high-fever
       stage), before the rash appears. By the time the rash breaks out, the child is
       generally no longer contagious.

Clinical Picture
Roseola is unique because the rash and the fever rarely overlap.
1. Prodrome (Febrile Phase)
The illness begins abruptly with a very high fever (often >40°C or 104°F) that lasts
for 3 to 5 days.
    • Appearance: Despite the high fever, the child often appears surprisingly well
       and alert.

   •   Associated Signs: Mild irritability, slight runny nose, and Nagayama
       spots (small erythematous papules on the soft palate and base of the uvula).

   •   The "Crisis": The fever ends abruptly (the "crisis"), followed immediately by the
       rash.

2. Rash (Exanthem Phase)
The rash typically appears within 12 to 24 hours of the fever's resolution.
    • Characteristics: Small (2–5 mm), pale pink, almond-shaped macules or
       papules. They are non-pruritic (not itchy).

   •   Pattern: The rash starts on the trunk and may spread to the neck and proximal
       extremities. It rarely affects the face.
  •   Duration: It is transient, often lasting only a few hours to two days, and
      disappears without scaling or pigmentation.

Complications
  •   Febrile Seizures: Because the fever rises so rapidly, approximately 10% to
      15% of children with roseola experience a febrile seizure. This is the most
      common complication.

  •   Rare Complications: Encephalitis, hepatitis, or thrombocytopenia, primarily in
      immunocompromised patients.

Treatment
  •   Treatment: Management is entirely supportive.

         o   Antipyretics: Acetaminophen or ibuprofen to manage fever and
             discomfort.

         o   Hydration: Ensuring adequate fluid intake.

         o   Antivirals: Ganciclovir is only considered for severe cases in
             immunocompromised children.
                                Erythema Infectiosum
Erythema Infectiosum, popularly known as Fifth Disease, is a common childhood viral
exanthem famous for its distinct "slapped-cheek" appearance.
Causative Agent
The disease is caused by Human Parvovirus B19, a small, non-enveloped, single-
stranded DNA virus. It specifically targets and replicates in erythroid progenitor
cells (immature red blood cells) in the bone marrow.
Incubation and Infectivity
   •   Incubation Period: Typically, 4 to 14 days, but can extend up to 21 days.

   •   Infectivity Period: Patients are most contagious during the initial viremic
       phase (the prodrome), which occurs before the rash appears. Once the
       characteristic rash develops, the child is considered no longer infectious and
       may return to school or daycare.

Clinical Picture
The illness typically presents in two distinct phases:
1. Prodrome (Viremic Phase)
A mild, non-specific illness occurs about a week after exposure.
    • Symptoms: Low-grade fever, headache, malaise, and mild upper respiratory
       symptoms (coryza).

   •   Significance: This is when viral shedding is at its peak.

2. Rash (Immune-Mediated Phase)
The rash appears 2–5 days after the prodrome and evolves in three stages:
    • Stage 1 ("Slapped-Cheek"): Bright red, erythematous flushing on the cheeks
       with circumoral pallor (paleness around the mouth).

   •   Stage 2 (Lacy/Reticular): 1–4 days later, a pink maculopapular rash appears on
       the trunk and extensor surfaces of the limbs. As it clears centrally, it takes on
       a lacy, marble-like, or reticular appearance.

   •   Stage 3 (Recurrence): The rash can fluctuate in intensity for weeks, often
       reappearing in response to heat, sunlight, exercise, or stress.
Complications
While usually benign in healthy children, Parvovirus B19 can be serious for specific
groups:
   • Arthropathy: More common in adult women; presents as symmetrical joint pain
       (arthritis) in the hands, wrists, and knees.

   •   Aplastic Crisis: In children with high red cell turnover (e.g., Sickle Cell
       Disease or Thalassemia), the virus can temporarily shut down red blood cell
       production, leading to life-threatening anemia.

   •   Fetal Hydrops: Infection during pregnancy (especially the second trimester) can
       cause severe fetal anemia and heart failure, potentially leading to fetal loss.

Treatment and Prevention
   •   Treatment: No specific antiviral exists. Management is supportive (fluids and
       acetaminophen for fever). For those in aplastic crisis, blood transfusions or
       intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) may be required.

   •   Prevention:

          o   There is no vaccine.

          o   Hygiene: Frequent handwashing and avoiding shared utensils.

          o   Exposure: Pregnant women or immunocompromised individuals who
              have been exposed should consult a physician for serological testing.
                               Infectious Mononucleosis
Infectious Mononucleosis (IM), colloquially known as "mono" or the "kissing disease," is
a common contagious syndrome characterized by a triad of fever, pharyngitis, and
lymphadenopathy. While typically a self-limiting illness in healthy adolescents, its
prolonged recovery and potential for rare but serious complications necessitate careful
clinical management.
Causative Agent
The primary causative agent is the Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), also known as human
herpesvirus 4 (HHV-4), which is responsible for approximately 90% of cases. Other
pathogens that can induce a similar "mononucleosis-like" syndrome include:
A.T. Still University (ATSU)

    •    Cytomegalovirus (CMV) (the most common non-EBV cause).

    •    Toxoplasma gondii.

    •    Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) (during acute infection).

    •    Adenoviruses and Human Herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6).

Incubation and Infectivity Periods
    •    Incubation Period: In adolescents and young adults, the incubation period is
         notably long, typically ranging from 4 to 6 weeks (roughly 30–50 days). It may be
         shorter in young children.

    •    Infectivity Period: Transmission occurs primarily through saliva (deep kissing,
         sharing utensils). An individual is most contagious during the acute phase (first 6
         weeks), but EBV can be shed intermittently in saliva for months to a year after
         symptoms are resolved. Once infected, the virus remains latent in B-lymphocytes
         for life and can reactivate periodically.

         Mayo Clinic


Clinical Picture
The disease often progresses through distinct stages:
   1. Prodrome: Lasting 3 to 5 days, this phase is marked by vague symptoms
         including malaise, headache, low-grade fever, and fatigue.
   2. Acute Phase (The Triad):

           1. Fever: Often high (up to 40°C), peaking in the afternoon.

           2. Sore Throat (Pharyngitis): Often severe with enlarged tonsils and
               white/gray exudates.

           3. Lymphadenopathy: Primarily affecting the posterior cervical lymph
               nodes, though axillary and inguinal nodes may also swell.

   3. Rash:

           1. Spontaneous Rash: A faint, non-itchy maculopapular rash occurs in
               roughly 10% of cases.

           2. Antibiotic-Induced Rash: A classic clinical sign occurs when patients are
               misdiagnosed with strep throat and given amoxicillin or ampicillin; up to
               90% of these patients develop a vivid, itchy maculopapular rash.

               A.T. Still University (ATSU)


Complications
Though rare, complications can be life-threatening:
   • Splenic Rupture: Occurs in 0.1%–0.5% of cases, typically in the 2nd or 3rd
       week; it is a surgical emergency.

   •   Airway Obstruction: Severe tonsillar swelling can compromise breathing.

   •   Neurological: Encephalitis, meningitis, and Guillain-Barré syndrome.

   •   Hematological: Hemolytic anemia and thrombocytopenia.

       Johns Hopkins Medicine +7


Treatment and Prevention
   •   Treatment: There is no specific antiviral cure or vaccine for mono.
       Management is primarily supportive:

           o   Rest and Hydration: Critical for recovery and managing fatigue.

           o   Analgesics: Acetaminophen or NSAIDs for fever and pain (avoid aspirin
               in children to prevent Reye’s syndrome).
       o   Corticosteroids: Used only for severe complications like impending
           airway obstruction.

       o   Activity Restriction: Patients must avoid contact sports and heavy
           lifting for at least 3–4 weeks to prevent splenic rupture.

•   Prevention: Focuses on personal hygiene, such as avoiding sharing drinks,
    food, or toothbrushes and refraining from kissing while symptomatic.
