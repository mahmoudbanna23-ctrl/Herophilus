Definition of Infantile Rickets.


Definition:
It is a metabolic disorder of infancy and childhood caused by vitamin D deficiency leading to
Failure of ostoid tissue to calcify.
 It affecting:

1. Growing bones: defective mineralization of osteoid tissue, Delay bone growth and teething

2. Skeletal muscles and ligaments: rickety myopathy (hypotonia) and laxity of ligaments.

NB: Rickets is the disease of infancy (growing bones) i.e. till the age of 2 years.

   Causes of infantile rickets:
I- Vitamin D deficiency: defective supply / synthesis (commonest):

1): Lack of exposure to sun rays to the infant or pregnant mothers’.

▪ Race: black race is more susceptible due to dark skin interfering with ultraviolet rays.
▪ Environmental factors: clouds, smoke, dust and ordinary window glass prevent ultraviolet rays,
  rickets is more common in winter.

2): Relative deficiency (increased need):
    Rapidly growing infants: prematures, twins and infants recovering from malnutrition.


3): Deficiency of vitamin D storage from pregnant mother (congenital rickets).
II- Defective activation of vitamin D to calcitriol (1,25 Hydroxy-D):
1. Liver diseases
2. Chronic renal diseases.
3. Anticonvulsant therapy: phenytoin and phenobarbital



III- Defective Vitamin D absorption:
1) Malabsorption syndromes, celiac disease, chronic diarrhea, etc.
2) Rachitogenic diets like:
      - Yoghurt and unmodified cow’s milk (its high P content decreases the absorption of Ca)
      - Cereals (high phytic acid content which forms insoluble salts with calcium).


IV- End-organ resistance to calcitriol in bones: Vitamin D dependent rickets type II (rare).

Pathology of infantile rickets:


❑ Failure of calcification of the osteoid tissue around the osteoblasts of growing bone will lead to:

❑ At the end of long bones osteoblasts will continue forming excess osteoid tissue which fails to calcify o
  Consequently, a wide, irregular zone of non rigid tissue is produced at the bone-cartilage junction present
at the metaphysis.


❑ This uncalcified osteoid tissue is easily compressible and is responsible for the skeletal abnormalities
   seen in rickets (widening of ends of long bones, rachitic rosary, etc.) as well as the X-ray findings
   (broadening, cupping and fraying of epiphyseal line).

Clinical picture of rickets:

A: Early manifestations (less than 6th month):
1.   Increased sweating, particularly around the head.


2.   Irritability by day.


3.   Insomnia.


4.   Craniotabes (thinning of outer table of skull) is the earliest bony change. Detected by firmly and gently pressing
     over the occiput or posterior part of parietal bone away from sutures. A ping- pong ball sensation will be felt (it is
     preferable not to try to detect it).


5.   Rachitic rosaries and Harrison sulcus.
B: Other manifestations later on:

Symptoms:
1. Delayed sitting, standing, walking (due to hypotonia).

2. Delayed teeth eruption.


Signs:

I.   Bony changes

I.   II. Ligaments and Muscles
I.    Bony changes:
1- Head:
i.   Craniotabes: usually disappears before the end of 1st year.

ii. Anterior fontanel is wider and its closure is delayed than normal.

iii. Frontal and parietal bossings are due to deposits of osteoid tissuearound the centers of ossification of
     these bones.

iv. The head is larger than normal and may remain so throughout life (DD. Large head).

v. Shape: frontal and parietal bossings and the grooves between the bosses may result in square
  skull (caput quadratum). The skull may be permanently asymmetric.
2- Thorax:

i.   Rickety (or rachitic) rosary: prominent enlargement of the costochondral junctions

ii. Pigeon breast deformity: -Longitudinal grooves develop posterior to the rosary.
                             -Flattening of sides of chest cage.
                             -Sternum and its adjacent cartilages are projected forward.

iii.   Harrison's sulcus: horizontal groove corresponding to the line of attachment of the
diaphragm with flaring of the costal margins below.

It is due to inward pull by the diaphragm on the soft rib cage.
3- Extremities

i.    Epiphyseal enlargement is common and is best felt at the wrists and ankles (Marfan sign).

ii.   Deformities: bending of the soft shafts of weight bearing long bones may results in:

      Bowlegs or knock-knees due to deformed tibia and fibula.

iii. Rickety dwarfism occurs if there are deformities of Legs + Spine + Pelvis together.

iv. Greenstick fractures.
    It is a fracture of the soft bony cortex within the intact periosteum.
   There is no pain or swelling. The fracture is detectable only in X-ray.

  Investigations.


1. Radoilogical Investigation: X-ray of wrist is best for early diagnosis, It
   shows signs of active Richets:
1. Broadening + cupping (concave) + fraying (irregularity) of the lower ends of radius and ulna.

2. Increased distance between the calcified ends of radius and ulna and the carpal or metacarpal bones

3. Epiphyseal line is indistinct.

4. Shafts: bone density and prominent trabeculae.

5. Periosteal elevation may be seen.

6. Fractures of greenstick type, or deformities
2- Biochemical (laboratory) findings :


1. Serum calcium: normal due to compensatory hyperparathyroidism
   (normal level = 9 -11 mg/ dl).

2. Serum phosphate: Low (< 3 mg /dl) due to compensatory hyperparathyroidism
   (normal = 4.56.5 mg/dl).

3. Serum alkaline phosphatase activity is high.
   (normal 515 Bodansky Unit /dl, or 145-200 IU/liter).

  Treatment of rickets.

1. A daily oral dose of (4000 - 6000 IU) (50-150 μg) will produce healing in 2 to 4 weeks
   demonstrable in Xray.

2. After healing is complete, reduce the dose to 400 IU/day (or 10 μg/day).



1. We have to be sure that the infants and children are receiving adequate calcium and
   phosphorus.
  Prevention of infantile rickets :

✓ Exposure of both infant, pregnant and lactating mother to ultraviolet rays (direct sun-
  light, clear
  sky, around noon).


 ✓ Supplying the daily requirements of vitamin D orally (syrup or drops) preferably in water
  miscible vehicle:

− For breast-fed infants 400 IU (or 10 g) of vitamin D/ day, starting from birth.

− For prematures, twins and infants of mothers with osteomalacia: 1000 IU/day, starting
  after birth.


− Vitamin D should also be given to the pregnant and lactating mother.

      Hypervitaminosis D
Hypervitaminosis D is usually iatrogenic due to huge or repeated doses of vitamin D.

▪ Anorexia, irritability.

▪ Vomiting with no apparent cause.

▪ Constipation with hard stool pellets.

▪ Excess thirst and polyuria

▪ Evidence of dehydration.

▪ Hypotonia .

▪ Nephrocalcinosis
▪ Renal failure.
Treatment :
1. Stop administration of vitamin D and calcium.

2. Fluids for correction of dehydration.

3. Rachitogenic diet (poor in milk and rich in carbohydrates and cereals).

In severe cases (serum Ca > 12 mg/dl): Prednisone 2 mg/kg/day until serum Ca drops
