                          Iron deficiency anemia
Definition:

   •   Iron deficiency refers to a state in which there is insufficient total body iron to
       maintain normal physiologic functions.

   •   Iron deficiency anemia is defined as hemoglobin concentration that ≥ 2SD below
       the mean Hb for a healthy population of the same age and sex, that results from ID.




Epidemiology:

   •   ID is the most common nutritional deficiency worldwide.

   •   Affects 25% of the global population, nearly 2 billion people.

   •   IDA is the most common anemia worldwide

   •   Highest with low socioeconomic status.



IDA has two peaks of incidence:

   1. Infancy and Toddlerhood (6-24 months)

   2. Adolescence, especially female (abnormal uterine bleeding, heavy menstruation)
Physiology of iron

Iron comes from:

   •   Fetal iron acquisition: about 60% of fetal iron is acquired from the maternal blood
       through the placenta during the third trimester.

   •   Neonatal iron acquisition from:

1. Breast milk ( low iron content but 50% of iron is absorbed), Adequate iron if totally
   breastfed for first 6 months (unless preterm)

2. Formula (supplemented with adequate amounts of iron),

3. Cow’s milk ( low iron content and poor bioavailability, so poor source of iron).

   •   Solids introduced at weaning

                           About 10% only of dietary iron is absorbed.

Etiology of IDA:

1. Inadequate iron supply: Prematurity, multiple twins (decreased iron stores), early
   cow milk feeding (low iron content of cow's milk and blood loss in the stools due to
   cow's milk protein allergy), exclusive breastfeeding after 6 months of age without an
   iron supplement, Low intake of iron-containing foods



2. Increased iron requirements: Failure to meet the increased demands of iron like in:
   Premature infants, During adolescence ( pubertal growth spurt)



3. Decreased gastrointestinal absorption: Celiac disease, Chronic diarrhea,
   Inflammatory bowel diseases, Parasitic infestations.

4. Blood loss Cow’s milk protein allergy, Meckel’s diverticulum, Bleeding diathesis.



Clinical manifestations:

  1. General manifestations of anemia:

     • Pallor (palms, nail beds, mucous membranes of the mouth and conjunctiva).
     • Exercise intolerance, easy fatigability and lassitude.
     • General weakness.
     • Dyspnea and palpitation on exertion or emotion.
     • Headache, lack of concentration, irritability.
     • Syncopal attacks.
     • Hemic murmurs


   2. GIT manifestations:
      Anorexia, atrophic glossitis, dysphagia, pica (ingestion of wall plaster, clay, etc.),
      geophagia (ingestion of earth) and pagophagia (ingestion of ice).

   3. CNS: Short attention span,  alertness,  learning ability and school performance.

   4. Changes in epithelial structures seen in adults, such as spoon-shaped or concave nails,
      are uncommon in infants and children.


   5. Immunological: Upper respiratory infections are more common
Laboratory investigations

   •   CBC: ↓ Hb, ↓ MCV, ↓ MCH, ↑ RDW, normal/low reticulocytes
   •   Iron studies:↓ ferritin, ↓ serum iron ,↑ TIBC, ↓ T-SAT%
   •   Peripheral smear: Anisopoikilocytosis, hypochromia microcytosis, pencil cells.
   •   Consider stool occult blood, celiac screen ( if history or examination suggest a non-
       dietary cause or failure to respond to therapy)


Diagnosis of iron deficiency anemia:
   1. Clinical manifestations.
   2. Laboratory findings.
   3. Therapeutic trial: best diagnostic study. Successful therapy with oral iron will cause:
   •   Improvement in neurologic function after 24 - 48 hrs
   •   Reticulocytosis after 48 - 72 hours, peak 5-7th day
   •   Increase in Hb after 4-30 days (1 g/dl/week)
       Repletion of iron stores in 1 - 3 months


Differential diagnosis:
       Other causes of microcytic hypochromic anemia:
   •   Thalassemia trait
   •   Anemia of chronic disease
   •   Lead poisoning
   •   Sideroblastic anemia


Prevention
   •   Exclusively breastfed infants are at increasing risk of ID after 4 completed months of age.
       Therefore, at 4 months of age, breastfed infants should be supplemented with 1 mg/kg per
       day of oral iron beginning at 4 months of age until appropriate iron-containing
       complementary foods (including iron-fortified cereals) are introduced in the diet.
   •   Preterm infants fed human milk should receive an iron supplement of 2 mg/kg per day by
       1 month of age, and this should be continued until the infant is weaned to iron-fortified
       formula or begins eating complementary foods that supply the 2 mg/kg of iron.


Treatment:
   •   For most children management includes dietary advice and oral iron supplementation.


       Dietary advice:
   •   Promote breastfeeding for at least 6 months, if possible.
   •   An alternate to breastfeeding is iron-fortified infant formula
   •   Increase iron-rich foods and reduce cow milk consumption
   •   Cow milk should not be offered to children <12 months and should be limited to <500
       mL/day in those older than 12 months


       Iron therapy:
       1. Oral iron medication:
   •   Dose: Oral iron salts: 3-6 mg/kg/day.
   •   Iron supplements should be continued for a minimum of 3 months after anaemia has been
       corrected to replenish stores.
   •   Practical considerations
          •   Advise parents that iron preparations can make a child’s stool black in colour and
              may cause constipation
          •   Oral iron preparations may also stain a child’s teeth. Consider having the child
              drink the iron preparation through a straw and brush the child’s teeth following
              administration
          •   Iron is better absorbed if taken with vitamin C eg orange juice
       2. Treatment of etiological factors, e.g. correct diet, treat parasitic infestations, and stop
       cow milk intake.
       3. Parenteral iron therapy: only used in malabsorption, poor compliance with oral iron,
       or the need for rapid correction of severe iron deficiency anemia.
4. Newer iron formulation: liposomal iron preparations, iron bisglycinate, in case of
intolerance to oral iron salts.
5. Blood transfusion: Rarely needed except in severely anemic children with
hemoglobin level below 4 g/dl and signs of cardiac dysfunction or associated with
infection and hypoxia. It is given as small amounts of packed red blood cells (2-3cc/kg)
