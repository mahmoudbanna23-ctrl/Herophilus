--- slide 4 ---
KEY FACTS
Leading cause of death in children worldwide.
Kills an estimated 1.4 million children under age of five every years (more than AIDS, TB, and malaria combined).
Can be prevented by immunization, adequate nutrition and addressing environmental factor.
Only 30% children with pneumonia receive the antibiotics they need.

Community-acquired pneumonia  (CAP)
Is infectious pneumonia in a person who has not recently been hospitalized.
Mostly caused by Pneumococci, 
H.influenzae
,  viruses and atypical bacteria.
 

--- slide 12 ---
Hospital-acquired pneumonia
 ( HAP )
Is pneumonia acquired 72 hours or more after admission to the hospital for another illness or procedure or within 14 day after  discharge from hospital.
Usually caused by MRSA, Pseudomonas , 
Enterobactr,and
 Serratia.

--- slide 13 ---
Ventilator associated pneumonia
 ( VAP )
    Ventilator Associated Pneumonia (VAP) is a subset of      nosocomial  pneumonia which  occurs  after at least 48 hours of intubation and   mechanical   ventilation .

Tissue response
The inflammatory response to 
Streptococcus pneumoniae 
or 
Haemophilus
 influenzae 
often produces lobar consolidation, but these infections rarely result in tissue necrosis .
Staphylococci 
and many 
Gram-negative bacilli 
often produce necrosis, which can lead to 
cavitation
 and even frank abscess formation; a 
peribronchial
 distribution is characteristic, but lobar consolidation may occur. 

--- slide 32 ---
Tissue response
Viruses
 generally produce interstitial inflammation rather than air-space exudates. The infection is usually bilateral and causes diffuse alveolar damage and interstitial edema. Similar tissue responses may be initiated by 
Mycoplasma
, 
Chlamydia
, and 
Legionella
 species.
Mycobacteria
 and 
fungi 
typically evoke a slow 
granulomatous
 response.

--- slide 54 ---
Staphylococcal pneumonia
At the onset is indistinguishable from pneumococcal pneumonia.
 Almost exclusively a disease of infants but can complicate influenza in older children.
 Staphylococcal often cause tissue necrosis and lead to cavitation, abscess formation and 
pyopneumothorax
 in rapid sequence .

--- slide 56 ---
Mycoplasma Pneumonia
Gradual onset, non-productive paroxysmal cough that turn productive. 
Severity of symptoms &gt; clinical findings. 
Non-pulmonary symptoms ( vomiting, diarrhea, myalgia, arthritis, skin rash, myocarditis, hemolytic anemia and 
meningeo
-encephalitis).
Course is prolonged than viral pneumonia and it occurs in school aged children.

--- slide 86 ---
In hospital
Pulse oximetry:
Pulse oximetry should be performed in every child admitted to hospital with pneumonia .
Acute phase reactants:
Acute phase reactants do not distinguish between bacterial and viral infections in children and should not be measured routinely [A].
Urea and electrolytes:
Urea and electrolytes to assess electrolyte imbalance is undertaken if the patient is severely ill or shows evidence of dehydration

Families of children who are well enough to be cared for at home need information on managing pyrexia, preventing dehydration, and identifying any deterioration.
The child cared for at home should be reviewed by a general practitioner if deteriorating, or if not improving after 48 hours on treatment .

--- slide 102 ---
Empiric Antimicrobial for presumed
Viral pneumonia
Viral pneumonia 
caused by 
influenza A 
may be treated with 
Rimantadine
 or 
Amantadine
, while viral pneumonia caused by 
influenza A or B 
may be treated with 
Oseltamivir
 or 
Zanamivir
. These treatments are beneficial only if they are started within 48 hours of the onset of symptoms. 
 There are no known effective treatments for viral pneumonias caused by the  SARS coronavirus, adenovirus, 
hantanvirus
, or parainfluenza virus.

--- slide 103 ---
Oral versus Intravenous Antibiotics:
Intravenous : 
Is needed to achieve high concentration of drug in blood and tissues to ensure eradication of the organism.
Oral:  
Can be used in mild cases.
Switch therapy:  
shorter hospital stay; cost savings in drugs, nursing time, and IV solutions; fewer hospital-related complications, such as phlebitis or falls; and greater patient satisfaction
.

Prevention
Cessation of smoking: 
is important not only because it helps to limit lung damage, but also because cigarette smoke interferes with many of the body's natural defenses against pneumonia. 

--- slide 106 ---
Prevention

: testing and treating pregnant women for Group B Streptococci and  Chlamydia trachomatis. Also suctioning  oropharynx to prevent meconium aspiration.
Vaccination: 
Against Pneumococci and 
Haemophilus
 influenzae in children and  against Influenza .
