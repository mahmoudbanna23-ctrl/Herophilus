       Blood Supply of the Liver

The liver is a very vascular organ which, in an average adult,
receives 1.5 litres of blood per minute or nearly 25% of the
resting cardiac output.

It has dual blood supply: hepatic artery and portal vein.
The portal vein provides 70%–75% of the inflow while the
hepatic artery supplies the remainder 25%–30%.

     Definition of Neonatal Cholestasis

• Neonatal      cholestasis   is   defined   as   conjugated
  hyperbilirubinemia that occurs in the newborn period or
  shortly thereafter (ie, within the first three months of
  life). presenting in infants, defined as a conjugated
  bilirubin level (>1) mg/dL (or (>20%) of total serum
  bilirubin).
        Definition of Neonatal Cholestasis

• Neonatal cholestasis presents with jaundice (beyond 2 weeks),
  hepatomegaly, and pathognomonic pale (clay-colored) stools with
  dark urine (bilirubinuria).

• It is caused by reduced bile flow and is defined by elevated direct
  bilirubin (>1 mg/dL if total ≤ 5 mg/dL, or >20% if total >5 mg/dL).

• It is always pathologic and requires urgent medical evaluation to rule
  out time-sensitive, life-threatening conditions

Clinical presentations of Neonatal Cholestasis

• Persistent Jaundice & Scleral Icterus: Often noted within
  the first 2 weeks.

• Acholic Stools: Pale, clay-colored, or white stools are a
  critical marker of severe bile flow obstruction.

• Dark Urine: Conjugated bilirubinuria makes the diaper
  yellow or brown.
Clinical presentations of Neonatal Cholestasis


• Hepatomegaly: Enlarged liver is common; splenomegaly may
  appear if sepsis, congenital infections or portal hypertension
  exists.

• Fat-Soluble Vitamin Deficiency: Deficiencies in vitamins A,
  D, E, and K can lead to coagulopathy (easy bruising or
  bleeding).
Clinical presentations of Neonatal Cholestasis




Failure to Thrive: Poor weight gain and growth may occur.


Pruritus: Severe itching is common, although less frequent in
the immediate neonatal period.

         Diagnosis of Neonatal Cholestasis
• The diagnostic approach to neonatal cholestasis focuses on
  identifying biliary atresia (BA) immediately, ideally within
  the first 6 weeks of life, to enable timely surgical intervention.

• Key steps involve:
1. Laboratory Investigations to confirming conjugated
   hyperbilirubinemia.
2. Abdominal ultrasound.
3. Liver biopsy
     Diagnosis of Neonatal Cholestasis

1) Initial Laboratory Studies:
• Serum total/direct bilirubin, AST, ALT, Gamma-GT
  (GGT), alkaline phosphatase, and coagulation studies
  (PT/INR).
• CBC, Sepsis workup in sick newborn.
• Reducing substance in urine (Galactosemia)
      Diagnosis of Neonatal Cholestasis

2. Abdominal Ultrasound (US):
Small gall bladder, or the "triangular cord sign" indicative of BA.
May show surgical anomilies as Choledochal Cyst…
3. Percutaneous Liver Biopsy:
Highly accurate (96.9%) for distinguishing obstructive jaundice
from neonatal hepatitis.
4. Operative Cholangiography: The "gold standard" for
diagnosing biliary atresia.
     Diagnosis of Neonatal Cholestasis

5- Further Diagnostic Evaluation: If BA is unlikely, investigate
other causes: [1]
• Metabolic Workup: Evaluate for galactosemia, alpha-1
   antitrypsin deficiency, and cystic fibrosis.
• Infection Screen: Test for TORCH infections (toxoplasmosis,
   rubella, CMV, herpes).
• Genetic Testing: Targeted panels for progressive familial
   intrahepatic cholestasis (PFIC) or Alagille syndrome

                 Key Considerations

• Cholestatic jaundice is always pathologic and indicates
  hepatobiliary dysfunction.

• Early detection by the primary care physician and timely
  referrals to the pediatric gastroenterologist/hepatologist are
  important contributors to optimal treatment and prognosis.
              Key Considerations

• Any infant who has jaundice after age 2 weeks
  should be evaluated for cholestasis including with
  total and direct bilirubin levels.

• Liver Biopsy Timing: Highly recommended before
  6 weeks of age.

• Timely Action: Early Kasai portoenterostomy (KPE)
  is critical for BA survival before 6 weeks of age.
