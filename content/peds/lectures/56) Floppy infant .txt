Pediatric neuromuscular disorders


Heterogenous Group of diseases caused by affection
  of any part of the lower motor neuron pathway
  Types of neuromuscular disorders

• Anterior horn cell disorders
• Nerve root affection
• Peripheral neuropathies
• Affection of neuromuscular
  transmission
• muscle diseases.
Anatomical classification
Etiological classification

According to the cause :
• Hereditary
• Acquired as infection, inflammatory , metabolic etc
         Common clinical features

• Weakness (progressive or static )
• Floppiness (hypotonia)
• Delay in motor milestones
• Gait abnormality or unsteadiness
• Easy fatiguability
• Cramps of muscle (suggesting a metabolic myopathy).
  Approach to a child with suspected neuromuscular
                       disorder



• Detailed History
• Meticulous clinical examination
• Tailored investigations



N.B: history and examination may provide useful clues for localization
                           and diagnosis.
            Important items in the history

• Pregnancy and delivery ( fetal movements – delivery pattern )
• Age of onset
• Onset of weakness ( sudden or gradual)
• Course of weakness ( progressive or stationary)
• Pattern of weakness ( proximal or distal)
• Any periodic pattern of symptoms ?
• Affection of bulbar, ocular or respiratory symptoms ?
• Associated sensory or sphincteric affection ?
• Family history
     Important items in clinical examination

• Muscle bulk ( distal atrophy – calf pseudohypertrophy)
• Muscle tone – power
• Deep tendon reflexes
• Gait assessment – test for Gower sign
• Facial features
• Examine for ocular and bulbar muscle affection
• Spinal examination ( lordosis , scoliosis)
• Sensory examination
• Systemic examination ( cardiac or pulmonary affection – hepatomegaly –
  skin Rash)

           Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA)

• The second most common cause of neuromuscular disease after
  Duchenne muscular dystrophy
• 1 in 6000–10,000 live births.
• Inherited as AR disorder
• Mutations in the SMN1 gene
• Leads to progressive degeneration of the AHC
• Progressive wasting and weakness and of all skeletal muscles
• 4 phenotypes are recognized
      SMA type 1 (Werdnig–Hoffmann disease)


• Severe and progressive
• Onset : from birth to 3 months of age.
• Decreased fetal movements during
  pregnancy
• Tongue Fasciculation
• Symmetrical flaccid paralysis
• Deep tendon reflexes are absent
• Paradoxical breathing pattern
• weak cry due to bulbar muscle weakness
                                        SMA type 1


Natural course : death within first year of life from
respiratory failure
Recent advances:
1. Exon skipping therapy
   • Produce SMN2 gene .
   • Increases production of the smn2 protein
   • improve the clinical expression of SMA in some children.
2. Gene therapy
   All these new treatments are very expensive.

                  Peripheral neuropathies
               Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease
          (hereditary motor sensory neuropathies)


• Distal muscular wasting: symmetrical,
  slowly progressive
• Many clinical types
• Mutations affecting myelin genes.
• Type 1A is the most common type (70%–
  80%)
• 70% of cases >> AD inheritance
• 30% >> de novo mutation.
            Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease type 1A

• Presentation: preschool period
• Foot drop: bilateral
• Ankle reflex absent first followed by knee
  reflex
• Pes cavus ( high arched foot)
• More lower limb affection than upper limbs
• Nerve conduction studies : sensory and
  motor neuropathy .
• The disease is chronic but loss of
  ambulation is rare
  Guillain–Barré syndrome (acute postinfectious
                 polyneuropathy)

• Acute flaccid paralysis
• Progressive
• After respiratory or GIT infection


• Mechanism: autoimmune reaction
 >> antibodies against myelin sheath)
             Guillain–Barré syndrome

• Symmetrical, ascending, progressive, weakness
• Progression over a few days to 2 weeks
• Can occur at any age
• Onset 2–3 weeks after upper respiratory tract or GIT
  infection
• Absent deep tendon reflexes
• Autonomic functions affection.
• affection of bulbar muscles causing difficult chewing and
  swallowing with risk of aspiration.
                Guillain–Barré syndrome

 CSF analysis: protein cell

  cells) after the 2nd week of illness
  Nerve conduction studies >>
  decreased nerve conduction
  velocities (usually after the second
  week).
 MRI of the spinal cord >> nerve
  root enhancement

Course:
Regressive in more than 90% of the cases. Recovery begins in 2-3 weeks
Progressive in few cases. Bulbar involvement leads to dysphagia and aspiration.
 Involvement of respiratory muscles may lead to respiratory failure.
A chronic relapsing form exists in less than 5% of the cases. intermittently or does
 not improve over months or years.

Management :
   Supportive measures
   IVIG infusion or plasma exchange
   Corticosteroids have no effect
Treatment:
 Supportive: In the acute phase, the patient should be transferred to hospital once
  diagnosed, even if his general condition is well. The patient may need mechanical
  ventilation at any time.
 Specific treatment: One of the following treatments OR both together.
– Intravenous immunoglobulin, 0.4 mg/kg/day, for 5 days (total dose for course is 2
  grams).
– Plasma exchange: Five exchanges of 50 ml/kg, over 7-10 days).
– Corticosteroids have no effect
 Physiotherapy to restore normal function.
     Disorders of neuromuscular transmission
                Myasthenia gravis



• Fluctuating muscle fatiguability
• Diurnal variation
• improves with rest or
  anticholinesterase drugs.
        Juvenile myasthenia : pathophysiology

• Caused by antibodies to receptors of acetylcholine on
 the postsynaptic membrane of the neuromuscular
 junction.

• Decrease in number of functional cholinergic
 receptors.
             Clinically :
                Usually present after 10 years of age
                Ptosis ophthalmoplegia , difficult chewing and
                 loss of facial expression
                In some cases : Generalized and proxima
                 weakness may be seen
             Juvenile myasthenia: Diagnosis

• Symptoms improve following intravenous administration of edrophonium: 0.15
 mg/kg. End point of the test is resolution of ptosis.
• Repetitive nerve stimulation study; with decremental response

• Lab testing for autoantibodies :
   Antibodies against acetylcholine receptor (positive in 60%–80%)
   Anti-musk (antimuscle-specific kinase) antibodies >> rare
        Juvenile myasthenia: management

• Pyridostigmine or Neostigmine: Cholinesterase enzyme
 inhibitors

• Immune-modulating drugs: prednisolone, mycophenolate
 mofetil or azathioprine

• Biological agents : Monoclonal antibodies e.g. Rituximab
• Thymectomy : only in presence of thymoma

• Plasma exchange: used in myasthenic crisis.
                Congenital myasthenic syndromes


• Rare genetic syndromes

• Caused by genetic mutations rather than autoimmune

• Symptoms of neuromuscular junction failure in
  neonatal period.

• Clinically: Ptosis, ophthalmoplegia and bulbar and
  respiratory muscle weakness and arthrogryposis.

• Response to anticholinesterase inhibitors is poor
        Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD)


• X-linked recessive disorder

• De novo mutations in about 30% of cases.

• Caused by mutations of dystrophin gene

• dystrophin connects the muscle fibre cytoskeleton to the
  extracellular matrix

• Prevalence: 1 in 3000–6000 male infants.

• Dystrophin mutations cause progressive and gradual, necrosis of
  myofibres
Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD):
       clinical presentation
• Clinical diagnosis around 5 years of age
• Difficult running and going upstairs
• Gowers’ sign
• Calf muscles pseudohypertrophy
• Waddling gait
• Loss of ambulation by the age of 10–14 years.
• Associated respiratory failure and cardiomyopathy.
• Life expectancy is reduced to the late twenties
• about one-third of affected children have learning difficulties
        Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD):
                  management

• Physiotherapy and splinting
• Corticosteroids : preserve mobility and prevent complications
• Surgical interventions may be needed e.g. (scoliosis surgery - Achilles tendon
  lengthening)
• Regular periodic assessment of pulmonary and cardiac functions
• Non ambulant cases should have regular sleep studies.
• In advanced cases: overnight continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) or non-
  invasive ventilation may be needed.
• Recent advances in treatment: exon skipping therapy and gene therapy
              Becker muscular dystrophy

• Milder phenotype of Duchenne muscular dystrophy
• Different types of mutations in the same gene
• some functional dystrophin protein is produced.
• Age of onset is around 11 years
• Clinically : similar to Duchenne muscular dystrophy cases but
  milder and more slowly progresses.
• loss of independent walking is later ( in late twenties)
• life expectancy : middle or old age.
                 Congenital muscular dystrophies

 Autosomal recessive
 Presentation : at birth or early infancy
 Slowly progressive Weakness and contractures.
 In most cases : Feeding and breathing difficulties
 Pathophysiology : decrease in proteins of extracellular
  matrix of muscles such as laminin (most common)
 Muscle biopsy: shows muscle fibres disruption
  (dystrophic pattern)
 In some types : associated CNS abnormalities
                Congenital myopathies


• Presentation: at birth or early infancy

• Defects affecting primarily skeletal muscle
 fibres

• Clinically : hypotonia and muscle weakness.

• Some cases are static – others: slowly
 progressive.

• Serum creatine kinase (CK) is normal or only
 mildly elevated.
                           Metabolic myopathies

Muscles are affected due to :
    Deposition of storage material
    Energy-depleting enzyme deficiencies.
Presentation :
    Floppy infant
    Muscle weakness
    muscle cramps on exercise
The main causes are:
    Glycogen storage disorders
    Disorders of lipid metabolism (fatty acids
     oxidation defect)
    Mitochondrial cytopathies
        The inflammatory myopathies

Benign acute myositis
• This is assumed to be post-viral myositis

• Pain and weakness occur in affected muscles

• Most commonly affect calf muscles and cause gait difficulty .

• Plasma creatine kinase is usually raised.

• self-limiting course.

• In most cases
                           Dermatomyositis

• Systemic illness with onset between 5–10 years of age.
• Gradual onset of proximal myopathy, muscle pain and
  fever
• Characteristic violaceous rash on the eyelids
• periorbital oedema
• Rash extensor surfaces of joints
• CK and other Inflammatory markers are usually raised.
• Muscle biopsy: inflammatory cell infiltrate and atrophy.
• Physiotherapy is needed to prevent contractures.
• treatment : Corticosteroids and other
  immunosuppressants
• Mortality is 5%–10%.

 Clinical signs of floppy infant:
    Head lag
    Frog leg posture
    Abnormal Horizontal suspension response
    Abnormal Ventral suspension response
    Truncal or peripheral hypotonia
    Muscle power may be normal or decresred
    Can be caused by UMNL ( central hypotonia) or
     LMNL ( peripheral hypotonia)
