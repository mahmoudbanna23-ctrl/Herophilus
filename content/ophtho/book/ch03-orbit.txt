# Globe Displacements

Diseases of the orbit can lead to changes in the volume of orbital soft tissues or integrity of
the orbital walls resulting in globe displacement.

**These include:**

1. Exophthalmos / Proptosis
2. Enophthalmos


[Banner heading, grey band, centred:]

# 1. Exophthalmos (Proptosis)

**Definition:**

**Anterior displacement** (forward protrusion) of the globe (measured by a **ruler or by Hertel
exophthalmometer**).

- The measurement represents the distance between the tip of the cornea and the plane of the
  lateral orbital margin.
- Normal values range from **12 mm to 22 mm**.
- **Exophthalmos** generally refers to an **endocrine aetiology** (Thyroid eye disease)
- **Proptosis** generally refers to **other aetiologies** (e.g., orbital mass, orbital , inflammation ,etc)
  [the comma placement is the book's own — "orbital , inflammation ,etc"]

[Figure, left: a young woman's face, front view, both eyes proptosed, with three printed labels
drawn onto the picture — "Bulging eyes" (arrow to the globes), "Eyelids retract" (arrow to the
upper lids), "Redness" (arrow to the conjunctiva).]
Fig (3-1) Exophthalmos

[Figure, right: close-up photograph of both eyes of an adult, marked unilateral forward
protrusion of one globe with widened palpebral fissure.]
Fig (3-2) Proptosis

**Note: Pseudo-exophthalmos :** (must be ruled out)
It is due to:
- Lid retraction
- Contralateral ptosis
- The large globe in high myopia

[Figure, left of pair: two head photographs of the same woman side by side, one without and one
with spectacles (thick lenses).]
High myopia

[Figure, right of pair: close-up of both eyes, one showing an obviously widened palpebral fissure
with sclera visible above the limbus.]
ipsilateral Lid retraction

**General Investigations:**
1. CT/MRI of the head and/or orbits.
2. Ultrasound of the orbits.
3. Thyroid function tests.

# 2. Enophthalmos

**Definition**: **Posterior** [Backward] displacement of the globe
**Investigations**: CT/MRI of the orbits

**Aetiology:**
1. Orbital fat atrophy ( post- traumatic or in old age)
2. Cicatrising tumour metastasis
3. **"Blow-out" fracture :**   ☆☆☆   [three star glyphs printed after the heading]

| | |
|---|---|
| Caused by: | a sudden increase in the orbital pressure from an impacting object that is greater in diameter than the orbital aperture (fist , tennis ball) causing the eye ball to be displaced |
| Fractured bone: | • The fracture most frequently involves **the floor of the orbit** along the thin bone covering the infraorbital canal. Sometimes, **the medial orbital wall** may also be fractured. <br> • Those thin walls of the orbit separate the orbital cavity from the surrounding nasal sinuses. |
| Clinical picture | 1. **Infection** can spread from the nasal sinuses to the orbit with nose blowing <br> 2. **Diagnosis of blow-out fracture :**

- Visual acuity and pupillary size and reaction should be recorded and monitored.
- **Periocular signs:**
  Ecchymosis, oedema, and subcutaneous emphysema.
- **Infraorbital nerve anaesthesia:**
  Involving the lower lid, cheek, side of the nose, upper lip, upper teeth and gums if the
  fracture involves the infraorbital canal.
- **Diplopia due to:**
  1. Haemorrhage and oedema in the orbit with restriction to movement of the globe.
     Ocular motility usually improves as the haemorrhage and oedema resolve.
  2. Mechanical entrapment within the fracture of the **inferior oblique or inferior rectus muscles**,
     or adjacent connective tissue and fat.
     Diplopia occurs in **both upgaze and downgaze.**
     [「inferior oblique or inferior rectus muscles」 highlighted yellow]
  3. Direct injury to an extraocular muscle
- Enophthalmos (severe cases)
- Ocular damage

⟦SHADED PANEL⟧
**CT scan** of the orbit (coronal section) is important to diagnose floor fractures and tissue
(inferior oblique muscle) entrapment.
⟦/PANEL⟧

⟦SHADED PANEL — TUTORIAL MARKER 3. First line is the centred blue word "Tutorial".⟧
                              **Tutorial**

    **CT scan of the orbit (coronal section)**

    - Fracture of the orbital floor ± other orbital wall
    - Tissue entrapment within the fracture (Tear drop sign)     [「Tear drop sign」 highlighted yellow]
    - ± Surgical emphysema
⟦/PANEL⟧

**Management:**
1. Patients should be advised to avoid blowing the nose.
2. Prophylactic antibiotics (systemic Abs.. **tutorial**)     ⟵ TUTORIAL MARKER 4 (inline, blue text)
3. **Surgical repair** will be required in case of:
   - muscle entrapment causing diplopia
   - or large fractures causing marked enophthalmos

# Preseptal Cellulitis

**Definition:**
Infection of soft tissue **anterior** to the orbital septum     [「anterior」 highlighted yellow]

**Aetiology:**
1. Usually  follows **periorbital trauma** or **dermal infection.**
2. Suspect H. Influenzae in children; S. Aureus or Streptococcus in adults.
   [capitalisation is the book's own — "H. Influenzae", "S. Aureus"]

**Treatment**:
1. Systemic antibiotics e.g., **(amoxicillin-clavulanic acid.)**
2. If severe or in a child aged one year or less, treat as orbital cellulitis

[Figure, upper right: two clinical photographs — a child with marked unilateral lid swelling and
closure of the palpebral fissure; below it, the same or a similar child with the lid everted /
lifted showing a quiet white globe underneath.]
Fig (3-4) preseptal cellulitis


[Banner heading, grey band, centred:]

# Orbital Cellulitis

It is an OCULAR as well as a **MEDICAL EMERGENCY.**  [followed by a red flashing-siren glyph with "!"]

**Definition**:
Inflammation of orbital contents **posterior** to the orbital septum.   [「posterior」 highlighted yellow]

[Figure, right: close-up photograph of a face with severe unilateral lid oedema, erythema and
proptosis of the affected side.]
Fig (3-5) Orbital cellulitis

**Incidence**:
Common in children, the elderly, and immunocompromised.

**Aetiology:**
- **Usually** secondary to sinus/ facial/ tooth infections or trauma.
- Can also arise from preseptal cellulitis.

**Treatment:**
1. Hospital admission
2. Blood cultures
3. Orbital CT
4. IV antibiotics **(ceftriaxone + vancomycin) + oral metronidazole** for 1 week
5. Surgically drainage of abscess with close follow up especially in children.
   [「Surgically drainage」 — the book's own wording, [sic]]

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT THE OBJECTIVES PROMISED vs WHAT THE PAGES TEACH
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The objectives flowchart names exactly four things — globe displacements (exophthalmos,
enophthalmos), preseptal cellulitis, orbital cellulitis. All four are taught. Nothing promised
is missing.

But note what the chapter does NOT contain, because a reader expecting an orbit chapter will
look for it:
- No orbital anatomy at all — no walls, no foramina, no apex, no septum anatomy beyond the
  bare phrase "anterior/posterior to the orbital septum".
- No orbital tumours section. "Orbital tumours" appears only as aetiology item 3 of
  exophthalmos, three bullets long (gradual onset, usually unilateral, ± systemic disease).
  No named tumour, no imaging appearance, no management.
- No thyroid eye disease detail — deferred: "(refer to eye and systemic diseases chapter)".
- No retrobulbar haemorrhage detail — deferred: "(refer to ocular trauma chapter)".
- No cavernous sinus thrombosis detail beyond two bullets.
- No carotid-cavernous fistula detail beyond the parenthesis "(Pulsating exophthalmos)".
- No orbital surgery, no decompression, no cosmetic/appearance management of proptosis.
