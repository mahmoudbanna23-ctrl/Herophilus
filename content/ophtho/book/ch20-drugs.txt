## printed p.255  (PDF p.257)  —  TECHNIQUE OF APPLYING DRUGS
================================================================================

`ophthalmology.pdf p.255`

**Instructions to be given to patients when using ocular medications (eyedrops or ointments):**
[Blue bold, underlined.]

- Always check the expiration date of the medication before using it.
- Apply only **one drop**. Instillation of more than one drop in the eye during a single
  application gives no additional benefit and the waste can be costly.
- Allow a minimum of **5 minutes between drops** if using more than one type of medication.
- When using ointments or gels, make them **the last to be used**.

The proper technique to instil eyedrops ensures maximum patient cooperation and adequate delivery
of medication to the eye.

**To instil topical ocular medications, follow the following steps:**
[Blue bold, underlined.]

1. Wash your hands and wear disposable gloves if desired.
2. Instruct the seated patient to tilt the head back and to look up.
3. Expose the palpebral conjunctiva by gently pulling downward on the skin over the cheekbone.
   Avoid direct pressure on the eyeball.
4. Instil the correct amount of medication into the **lower conjunctival fornix**.
   **Avoid applying drops directly to the cornea**, which is the most sensitive part of the eye,
   and avoid touching the tip of the applicator to the patient's lids or eye.
5. Instruct the patient to close both eyes gently for a few seconds.
   Wipe any excess medication from the patient's skin with a tissue.

**To apply an eye ointment:**
[Blue bold, underlined.]

1. Wash your hands and wear disposable gloves if desired.
2. Instruct the seated patient to tilt the head back and to look up.
3. Expose the palpebral conjunctiva by gently pulling downward on the skin over the cheekbone.
   Avoid direct pressure on the eyeball.
4. Apply a small amount of ointment (**5 mm ribbon**) into the lower conjunctival fornix.
5. Gently close the eye.

**-OR-**

1. Apply a small amount of ointment to the clean fingertip of the dominant hand
2. Gently pull down the lower lid with the opposite hand
3. Apply the ointment directly onto the exposed sac
4. Gently close eyelids and roll them while closed to spread the ointment on the ocular surface

**N.B:**
[Blue bold, underlined.]

Before administering any medication, always ask patients if they may be allergic to the agent.

## (3) Mydriatics:
[Blue bold sans with the full-width blue rule.]

Mydriatics are drugs that dilate the pupil; dilation may be necessary for ophthalmoscopy.
The **<u>two main classes</u>** of mydriatics are **<u>cholinergic-blocking</u>** (or
parasympatholytic) drugs and **<u>adrenergic-stimulating</u>** (or sympathomimetic) drugs.

### A) <u>Cholinergic-blocking drugs:</u>
[Blue bold, underlined.]

- Drugs in this category dilate the pupil by **paralyzing the sphincter pupillae muscle**.
- Cholinergic-blocking drugs produce not only mydriasis but also cycloplegia, or <u>paralysis of
  the muscles of the ciliary body</u>. For this reason, these drugs are often referred to as
  **cycloplegics**.
- Cycloplegia produces <u>paralysis of accommodation</u> (focusing), so patients find that their
  near vision is blurred until the effects of the cycloplegic wear off.
- The cycloplegic effect of these drugs facilitates refraction.

[Rounded blue-outlined callout box, right-hand side, level with the last bullet:]
> All cycloplegics are medriatics, but not all medriatics are cycloplegics
> [sic — **"medriatics"** for "mydriatics", twice, exactly as printed. Confirmed at 2.2× zoom.]

### B) <u>Adrenergic-stimulating drugs:</u>
[Blue bold, underlined.]

- These drugs dilate the pupil by **stimulating the dilator pupillae muscle**.
- Only one such drug is in regular use: <mark>**phenylephrine hydrochloride 2.5%.**</mark>
  [Highlighted yellow on the page.]
  - ○ Onset: **30-40 minutes**
  - ○ Duration: **4-6 hours**
- It does not affect accommodation (i.e.: it has <u>no cycloplegic effect</u>). Therefore, the
  patient can use near vision after instillation. However, the mydriasis produced is not as great
  as with tropicamide, and the pupil remains reactive to light. For these reasons, phenylephrine
  is seldom used alone as a mydriatic.
- When maximum mydriasis is required, **<u>phenylephrine in combination with tropicamide</u>** is
  ideal because the effects are additive.
  - ○ This combination is often used to dilate the pupil of a **brown iris** as well because
    mydriatics are less effective in dark-eyed individuals than in blue-eyed ones.
- In **infants**, the combination of **<u>cyclopentolate hydrochloride 0.2% and phenylephrine
  hydrochloride 1.0%</u>** is the safest and most effective.

**<u>Adverse reactions:</u>**

1. **Phenylephrine 10% solution** has been associated with **acute hypertension** and even with
   **myocardial infarction** in some patients.
2. In infants, higher concentration preparations (higher than cyclopentolate 0.2% and
   phenylephrine 1.0%) can result in **hypertension and reduced gastric emptying**.

**<u>Cautions regarding pupillary dilation:</u>**

1. All mydriatics/cycloplegics sting on instillation.
2. Blurred vision will persist after the examination. Therefore, care must be taken when driving
   with the pupil dilated.
3. It is better to wear sunglasses after dilation to reduce discomfort and protect the retina.
4. **Phenylephrine 10%** solution should be avoided especially in patients with **cardiac
   diseases, systemic hypertension or arteriosclerosis**.
5. Pupils of patients with <u>Down's syndrome</u> widely dilate with tropicamide due to autonomic
   imbalance between cholinergic and adrenergic receptors.
   Therefore, it is better to <u>avoid cyclopentolate, atropine or other longer-duration
   mydriatics</u>.
6. In eyes with **subluxated lens**, dilatation can precipitate an attack of **angle closure
   glaucoma**.
7. Patients should be warned about the symptoms of angle closure, **pain, headache, nausea and
   vomiting, halos around light and decreased vision**.

## (4) Decongestants:

- These **weak adrenergic-stimulating drugs** temporarily whiten the conjunctiva through
  **vasoconstriction**.
- They are advertised as effective in relieving eye redness due to minor eye irritations caused by
  smoke, dust, smog, wind, glare, swimming, contact lenses, or fatigue.
- <u>The 3 major drugs in this category are:</u>
  1. Naphazoline hydrochloride **0.012%**
  2. Phenylephrine hydrochloride **0.12%**
  3. Tetrahydrozoline hydrochloride **0.05%**.
  [⚠️ All three re-read from a 2.5× upscaled crop. Naphazoline is **0.012%** — three decimal
   places — and phenylephrine here is **0.12%**, an order of magnitude apart and easy to
   transpose. This 0.12% phenylephrine is the *decongestant* strength, distinct from the 2.5%
   mydriatic and the 10% dangerous strength on printed 260.]
- The widespread belief that the use of these compounds is part of good ocular hygiene is a
  misconception.
  Red, burning eyes may benefit as much from a cold, wet compress to the closed eyelids as they

# Systemic Side Effects of Glaucoma Medications

- Glaucoma treatment is targeted to **decrease intraocular pressure** (IOP) to reduce or stop
  further optic nerve damage and visual field loss. This is done pharmacologically by
  <u>decreasing aqueous production or increasing outflow.</u>
- Glaucoma therapy involves **treating the patient, not just the disease**. Factors related to
  patient compliance, lifestyle, systemic health as well as the expenses of treatment must always
  be taken into consideration.
- <u>Topically administered glaucoma drugs can have potent systemic side effects</u> as any drug
  instilled in the conjunctival cul-de-sac may be absorbed systemically by the **conjunctiva,
  nasopharyngeal mucosa, or gastrointestinal tract (after saliva is swallowed)**.
- Systemically administered carbonic anhydrase inhibitors may cause side effects as well.
- Systemic side effects of glaucoma medications are more prominent and need more attention in
  **elderly** individuals.

**<u>Patients need to know the following about glaucoma treatment:</u>**

1. Glaucoma is a **lifelong disease**. Drops must be taken every day.
2. **Never to stop medications** for any reason (cost, nuisance, discomfort or feeling fine)
   unless advised to do so by the ophthalmologist.
3. After using glaucoma eyedrops, the patient should <u>close the eyes and gently press with the
   index finger against the nasal corner of the palpebral fissure</u> for about **30-60 seconds**
   to close the lacrimal passages, prevent tear drainage and keep the medication on the eye. This
   helps prevent systemic absorption, thereby decreasing side effects.

## 1. Beta-adrenergic blockers

  avoid the pulmonary complications of timolol.

  pulmonary effects have occasionally been noted, and caution should be used when this drug is

# **Examples of Ocular Side Effects of Systemic Medications**

[⚠️ **SECTION BANNER**, not an ordinary heading — centred bold black title on a **pale blue-grey
filled band** running the full text width, the same device the book uses to open a major part.

## **Amiodarone:**  [blue heading, centred, blue rule under it]

Amiodarone is a **cardiac arrhythmia drug** that has been associated with:

- <u>**Corneal deposits:**</u> Amiodarone also produces **whorl-shaped, pigmented deposits in the
  corneal epithelium** (**cornea verticillate** or **vortex keratopathy**).
  These deposits are **dosage-related and reversible if the dosage is decreased, or the drug is
  discontinued entirely.**
  These epithelial deposits **rarely cause visual symptoms**
  [sic — "cornea verticillate", as printed. The usual form is *cornea verticillata*. Recorded, not
  corrected.]

- <u>**Optic neuropathy**</u> (**non-arteritic AION**): Patients present with **mildly decreased
  vision, visual field defects, and bilateral optic disc swelling.**

- <u>**Intracranial hypertension.**</u>

## **Chloroquine & Hydroxychloroquine:**  [blue heading, centred, blue rule under it]

**Chloroquine phosphate** and **hydroxychloroquine sulfate**, originally used to treat **malaria**,
are also used to treat **rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, and other autoimmune
disorders.**

They can produce:
- <u>**Corneal deposits:**</u> They are usually **asymptomatic** but can produce **glare and
  photophobia**. The deposits **regress when the drug is discontinued**
- <u>**Retinopathy:**</u> This is **much more serious**.
  - This drug-induced retinal damage is **insidious, slowly progressive, and usually
    irreversible.**
  - The typical **bull's-eye macular lesions** do not become visible ophthalmoscopically **until
    serious retinal damage has already occurred**

- Chloroquine phosphate is now **rarely used** for the treatment of autoimmune disease due to its
  **greater toxicity** and largely has been replaced by the **less toxic drug hydroxychloroquine
  (Plaquenil).**

- All patients **beginning hydroxychloroquine therapy** should have a **complete baseline dilated
  examination with visual field testing**, as well as **colour vision and fundus photography** to
  document fundus appearance and any other ocular conditions (e.g., macular degeneration, retinal
  dystrophy).
- Patients on hydroxychloroquine **with visual complaints** should be **referred to an
  ophthalmologist** for ocular examination.

## **Corticosteroids:**  [blue heading, centred, blue rule under it]

- Corticosteroids can produce **posterior subcapsular cataracts** *(dark red)*. This phenomenon has
  been **commonly observed in people with asthma, renal transplant, or rheumatoid arthritis.**
- The use of **systemic or inhaled** corticosteroids is associated with **<u>elevated IOP</u>**
  (**steroid-induced glaucoma**) in **susceptible individuals**.
- **Oral and inhaled steroids** may also precipitate or aggravate **acute or chronic central serous
  retinopathy**, a condition resulting in **pooling of fluid under the macula** with the resultant
  **decrease in central vision**.

## **Digitalis:**  [blue heading, centred, blue rule under it]

Used for treatment of  heart failure.
[sic — double space after "of", as printed.]

Its <u>ocular side effects</u> include:
- Decreased vision
- **Abnormal colour vision** (**yellow or blue tinge** and **coloured halos around light**).

Usually, **fatigue and weakness develop concomitantly with digitalis intoxication**, but the
**visual disturbances often dominate the patient's complaints.**

## **Ethambutol:**  [blue heading, centred, blue rule under it]

- Ethambutol is useful in the **chemotherapy of tuberculosis**.
- As a side effect, ethambutol can result in **optic neuropathy** *(dark red)*.
- The onset of vision loss may be <u>within 1 month</u> of starting the drug.
- **Recovery usually occurs when the drug is stopped**, but it may take **months**; occasionally,
  vision loss is **permanent**.

## **HMG-COA reductase inhibitors (Statins):**  [blue heading, centred, blue rule under it]

- **Hydroxymethylglutaryl coenzyme reductase inhibitors (statins)** are **cholesterol-lowering
