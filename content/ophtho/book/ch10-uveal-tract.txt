Header band: **The Uveal Tract**

• The uveal tract (the uvea) is the **vascular middle layer of the eye.**
• It is divided into three parts:
  the iris, the ciliary body and the choroid arranged from front to back:

============================ 1. The Iris: ============================

• It is the **anterior-most** part of the uvea that is responsible for the color
  of the eye.
• It hosts the pupil (the aperture responsible for regulating the amount of light
  entering the eye).
• The high pigment content of the iris blocks light from passing to the retina,
  restricting it to the pupil

• **The iris consists of two layers**:
   1. **The iris stroma**: It is the front pigmented layer. It has no epithelium.
      The stroma contains the muscles of the iris (sphincter pupillae and dilator
      pupillae) which control the size of the pupil.
   2. **The iris pigment epithelial cells** posterior to the stroma.

• **The iris root:**
   o It is the outer edge of the iris which is attached to the sclera and is
     continuous with the ciliary body posteriorly.
   o In front of the root of the iris lies the **trabecular meshwork**, through
     which the aqueous humour drains out of the eye; the trabecular pathway
     (diseases of the iris often have important effects on the IOP)

     N.B:
     The iris along with the anterior ciliary body provide a secondary pathway for
     aqueous humour to drain from the eye (the uveo-scleral pathway)

• **The iris is divided into two major regions:**
   1. **The pupillary zone** is the inner region whose inner edge forms the
      boundary of the pupil.
   2. **The ciliary zone** is the rest of the iris that extends to the ciliary
      body.
   3. **The collarette** is the thickest region of the iris, separating the
      pupillary portion from the ciliary portion.

   [transcribed as printed — the heading says TWO major regions and the list runs
   to THREE items, the third being the collarette. Not corrected here.]

• **The iris crypts:**
   – These are a group of depressions on the front surface of the iris midway
     between the collarette and the origin of the iris.
   – They represent spaces between the bundles of the **dilator pupillae muscle**.

• **Iris color:**
   o The iris is usually strongly pigmented.
   o The iris color varies among individuals (brown, hazel, green, grey, and blue).
   o **Albinism:**
     Occasionally, the colour of the iris is due to a lack of pigmentation, as in
     the pinkish-white color in cases of albinism.

   o **Heterochromia:**
     – It is an ocular condition in which one iris is a different colour from the
       other iris **(complete heterochromia)**, or where the part of one iris is a
       different colour from the remainder **(Partial heterochromia)**.
     – It can be an indicator of an ocular disease, such as congenital Horner's
       syndrome, chronic iritis, or diffuse iris melanoma, but may also occur as a
       normal variant.

     (Two photographs: a face with one brown and one blue iris — complete
      heterochromia; a close-up blue iris with a brown sector — partial
      heterochromia.)

======================= 2. The Ciliary body: =======================

• It is the **middle** part of the uvea attached anteriorly to the iris root and
  posteriorly to the choroid. It is a ring-shaped thickening of tissues that lies
  between the posterior chamber anteriorly and the vitreous body posteriorly.
• It contains blood vessels, connective tissue, and the ciliary muscle.

• **It is divided into 2 parts:**
   1. The pars plicata anteriorly
   2. The pars plana posteriorly

• **The non-pigmented epithelium** of the pars plicata is responsible for aqueous
  humour secretion.

• **The ciliary muscle:**
   o It controls the shape of the lens via the attachment of the zonular fibers to
     the pars plicata of the ciliary body.
   o When the ciliary muscle contracts, the zonules become relaxed and,
     consequently, the lens becomes more convex, improving the focus for near
     objects **(Accommodation).**
   o When it relaxes, it puts tension on the zonular fibers resulting in
     flattening of the lens and improving the focus for far objects.

• **Functions:**
  The ciliary body has three functions:
   1. Accommodation
   2. Aqueous humour production
   3. It provides an attachment to the zonular fibers keeping the lens in place.

========================= 3. The Choroid: =========================

• The most **posterior** part of the uvea. It lies between the retina and the
  sclera.
• The choroid provides oxygen and nourishment to the outer layers of the retina.

• The choroid is divided into **four layers** arranged from outwards to inwards:
   1) An **outermost layer** consisting of larger diameter blood vessels.
   2) Layer of medium-diameter blood vessels
   3) Layer of capillaries (choriocapillaris)
   4) Bruch's membrane which is the **innermost layer** of the choroid.
      The basement membrane of the retinal pigment epithelium is a part of Bruch's
      membrane.

• **Functions**:
   1. **Nutrition and gas exchange**: uveal vessels directly perfuse the choroid,
      ciliary body, and iris, to support their metabolic needs, and indirectly
      supply nutrients to the outer retina, sclera, and lens, which lack blood
      supply.
   2. **Light absorption**: the uvea improves the contrast of the retinal image by
      reducing the reflected light within the eye and absorbing the outside light
      transmitted through the sclera.

from inner to outer — RPE · Bruch's Memb. · Choriocapillaris · **Settler's Layer**
[sic — the standard spelling is Sattler's] · **Haller's Layer** · Sclera. Inset

================================ Uveitis ================================
(section header band)

⇨ Inflammation which can involve one, two, or all three parts of the uveal tract.
⇨ Most commonly idiopathic but can be associated with autoimmune, infectious, or
  malignant causes.
⇨ It must be managed by an ophthalmologist

**Anatomically, uveitis can be classified into:**
   1. Anterior uveitis (iritis/ iridocyclitis)
   2. Intermediate uveitis
   3. Posterior uveitis
   4. Panuveitis (simultaneous inflammation of all three parts of the uveal tract)

Header band: **Anterior Uveitis (Iritis – Iridocyclitis)**

**Definition:**
 – Inflammation involves the iris **(iritis)** and is usually accompanied by
   **cyclitis** (inflammation of the ciliary body), hence the name
   **iridocyclitis**.
 – It is usually **unilateral**

**Aetiology:**
 1. **Idiopathic (75% of cases)**
 2. **Connective tissue diseases** e.g.,
      o HLA-B27 associated: Ankylosing spondylitis, psoriatic arthritis,
        inflammatory bowel disease (ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease)
      o Non-HLA-B27: juvenile idiopathic arthritis.
 3. **Infectious causes**: Syphilis, TB, toxoplasmosis, HSV, HZV, etc.
 4. Other causes:
      • Sarcoidosis
      • **Trauma** (the most common cause of non-idiopathic iridocyclitis)
      • Following ocular surgery

**Clinical picture:**

Symptoms:
 1. **Photophobia :**
    (intolerance to the usual levels of illumination let alone higher levels) due
    to reactive spasm of the muscles of the inflamed iris.
 2. Ocular pain with globe tenderness and brow ache (ciliary muscle spasm)
 3. Lacrimation
 4. Red eye
 5. Decreased vision

Signs:
 ▪ Decreased vision
 ▪ **Perilimbal (ciliary) injection** with violaceous hue
 ▪ **Miosis** (an early sign) with sluggish pupillary light reaction.
 ▪ Anterior chamber (AC) cells (leukocytes in the aqueous humor) due to anterior
   segment inflammation with increased vascular permeability
 ▪ **Hypopyon (pus in the AC)**: it represents a collection of neutrophils and
   exudates in the inferior part of the AC.
   [signs continue on printed 135]

⚠️ AC cells are defined here as a SIGN. No grade, no scale, no count-per-field.

FIGURES: "ciliary injection" (slit photograph of perilimbal violaceous injection);
"Hypopyon in anterior uveitis" (white fluid level in the inferior AC);
"Cells & flare" (slit-beam photograph through a dark AC showing the beam made
visible — the classic Tyndall demonstration; the caption carries no grade).

[Signs of anterior uveitis, continued]

 ▪ **Aqueous flare:** composed of **proteins** circulating in the aqueous
   secondary to inflammation and increased vascular permeability.
   This altered highly viscous aqueous humour is called **plasmoid aqueous.**

 ▪ **Keratic precipitates (KPs)**:
   formation of clumps of cells (WBCs) on the back surface of the cornea (corneal
   endothelium)

 ▪ **IOP :**
   – Usually decreases due to ciliary body inflammation with decreased aqueous
     production (ciliary body shutdown)
   – Sometimes the IOP increases [Secondary inflammatory glaucoma] due to:
       • Trabeculitis as in HSV or HZV iritis
       • Plasmoid aqueous and circulating large-sized WBCs obstruct the trabecular
         meshwork resulting in impaired aqueous drainage

**Complications:**
 • 2ry glaucoma
 • Posterior synechiae → (leads to irregular shape of the iris .. **Tutorial**)
 • Complicated cataract
 • **Calcific band keratopathy** (if the iritis becomes chronic) especially in
   juvenile idiopathic arthritis.
 • Macular edema (if the iritis becomes chronic)

**Treatment:**
 1. **Steroids (topical - periocular - Systemic)** to control the inflammation and
    decrease the incidence of complications
 2. Cycloplegic-mydriatic topical medications: to decrease photophobia and pain
    from iris and ciliary spasm +( to prevent and break recently formed posterior
    synechiae → **Tutorial**)
 3. Antiglaucoma medications in case of 2ry glaucoma **(avoid prostaglandin
    analogues)**
 4. Control of the causative condition (After doing systemic workup especially in
    recurrent cases to reach definitive diagnosis of the cause of anterior uveitis

 └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Header band: **Intermediate Uveitis (Pars planitis -Cyclitis)**

**Definition:**
 ▪ Intraocular inflammation mainly focused on the vitreous and peripheral retina
 ▪ It incorporates pars planitis, posterior cyclitis, and vitritis.

**Epidemiology:**
 ▪ Usually affects **younger** patients, aged **15 to 40 years**
 ▪ Represents 20% of cases of paediatric uveitis
 ▪ It is usually idiopathic in developed countries and due to infectious causes in
   developing countries

**Aetiology:**
 1. Infectious causes: TB, syphilis, toxoplasmosis, toxocariasis, etc.
 2. Non-infectious causes: Sarcoidosis, Multiple sclerosis, etc

**Clinical pictures:**

Symptoms:
 o Presentation is typically **insidious**
 o Initially unilateral later it becomes bilateral but asymmetrical in both eyes
 o **Floaters**
 o Blurred vision

Signs:
 o Associated with anterior uveitis in about 50% of cases
 o Vitreous cells
 o Vitreous condensations with hazy vitreous body
 o **Snowballs:** aggregates of inflammatory cells in the inferior vitreous
 o **Snow banking**: grey, white fibrovascular plaques on the inferior pars plana.

**Complications:**
 1. Cataract
 2. Glaucoma
 3. Macular oedema in 30% of cases

**Treatment:**
 1. Treatment of the cause
 2. Steroids (topical - periocular – intravitreal - systemic)
 3. Immunosuppressive agents in non-responding cases and as steroid-sparing agents
 4. Surgical intervention: vitrectomy, cryotherapy, laser photocoagulation

labels Pars plana, Vitreous, Retina, Iris, Uvea.

**Definition:**
Inflammation of the **choroid** with or without inflammation of the retina.

**Aetiology:**
 1. Infectious causes:
    • **Bacterial**: TB, syphilis, leprosy, etc.
    • **Viral**: HSV, HZV, CMV especially with acquired immunedeficiency [sic]

    • **Fungal**: histoplasmosis, candidiasis,
    • **Parasitic**: **toxoplasmosis (most common)**, toxocariasis.

      and lymphoma

**Clinical pictures:**

Symptoms:
 o **Painless** diminution of vision
 o **Floaters**

Signs:
 o Vitreous haziness, cells, and opacities.

Header band: **Panuveitis**

**Definition:**
Inflammation of all uveal components of the eye with localization to a specific
part of the uvea
[transcribed exactly as printed. The wording is internally odd — "all components …
with localization to a specific part" — and it is repeated verbatim in the p.139
table, so it is the book's settled wording, not a one-off typo.]

**Aetiology**:
 1. Infectious causes:
    • **Exogenous Infections**
      due to introduction of organisms into the eye through a perforating wound or
      ulcer.
    • **Secondary infections:**
      the inflammation of the uveal tract due to its spread from other ocular
      tissues- cornea, sclera, or retina.
    • **Endogenous infections**:
      – organisms primarily lodged in another organ of the body reach the eye
        through the bloodstream.
      – These include:
          ⇨ **bacterial infections** such as syphilis or tuberculosis
          ⇨ **viral infections** such as mumps, smallpox, influenza
          ⇨ **protozoal infections** such as toxoplasmosis.

 2. Non-infectious causes:
    • Autoimmune e.g., Behçet's disease
    • Neoplastic causes as in posterior Uveitis (Masquerade syndrome)
    • Traumatic causes: Ocular trauma can produce features of panuveitis.

**Clinical Pictures:**
The clinical presentation of panuveitis involves the summation of symptoms and
signs of anterior, intermediate, and posterior uveitis

**Treatment:**
 1. Corticosteroids
 2. Cycloplegics
 3. Systemic immunosuppression
 4. Specific treatment based on the underlying disease

================= Vogt-Koyanagi-Harada (VKH) Syndrome =================

VKH Syndrome is an **idiopathic multisystem autoimmune** disease featuring
inflammation of melanocyte-containing tissues such as the uvea, skin, ear, and
meninges.

**The following criteria are required to make a diagnosis of VKH syndrome:**
 1. The absence of a history of penetrating ocular trauma
 2. Absence of other ocular disease entities
 3. Bilateral granulomatous panuveitis
 4. Neurological and auditory manifestations
 5. Affection of the skin and its adnexa e.g., vitiligo, alopecia and poliosis

======================== Sympathetic Ophthalmia ========================

⇨ It is a bilateral granulomatous panuveitis occurring **after penetrating trauma
  to one eye.**
⇨ It occurs due to immune sensitization to melanin or melanin-associated proteins
  in uveal tissues.

**The findings include:**
 • Iris nodules
 • Mutton fat KPs
 • Disc oedema

⚠️ NEITHER ENTITY IS GIVEN A TREATMENT, AN INTERVAL, OR AN INCIDENCE. Sympathetic
ophthalmia is printed WITHOUT the classic latent-period figures and without the
enucleation-timing rule; VKH is printed WITHOUT the sunset-glow fundus and without
staging. Both are one short block each. No exciting/sympathising eye terminology
is printed.
