Header band: **Anatomy of the sclera**

• The sclera "the **white** part of the eye" constitutes **5/6** of the **outer**
  fibrous protective coat of the globe.
• Composed of **irregularly** distributed collagen bundles (therefore opaque)
• Continuous with the **cornea anteriorly** and with the **dura mater covering the
  optic nerve posteriorly**.
• The episclera is a thin layer of vascularized connective tissue covering the
  outer surface of the sclera.

FIGURE (right, unnumbered line diagram): globe in section, annotated "Cornea forms
1/6 th of the outer coat" and "Sclera forms 5/6 th of the outer coat".

**Normal Scleral color**  (++ Additional)
   [⚠️ "(++ Additional)" is the book's own tag on this sub-section — it is NOT the
   Tutorial marker, and its meaning is not defined anywhere in the front matter.
   Transcribed as printed.]
 • In adults, the sclera is **white** in color. This white appearance is because of
   the scattering of all wave lengths of light by dense irregular bundles of
   collagen in sclera.
 • In children, a **bluish** is observed [sic — verb missing] because of the
   extremely thin sclera which allows the visibility of underlying choroid.
 • In older age the sclera may appear slightly yellowish because of the deposition
   of fat.

**Blue sclera**
 • Sclera is whitish in color but can attain a **bluish** discoloration due to
   **thinning and subsequent transparency of scleral collagen**, which allows
   increased visibility of underlying choroidal pigment.
 • This can be seen in some conditions such as:
    o Marfan syndrome
    o Osteogenesis imperfecta

 • A localized protrusion of the sclera lined by uveal tissue (i.e.: **bluish** in
   color) is called **staphyloma.**

FIGURES:
 – (unnumbered) colour photograph of a face with markedly blue sclerae both eyes,
   beside the "Blue sclera" text.
 – **Fig 7-1: staphyloma** — clinical photograph of a globe with a large dark-blue
   bulging protrusion at the limbus/anterior sclera. Teaches what the prose
   defines: the uveal lining is what makes it blue.

⚠️ No type of staphyloma is classified (no anterior/intercalary/ciliary/equatorial/
posterior scheme), and no cause is given beyond "localized protrusion".

Header band: **Episcleritis**

 – Inflammation of the episcleral tissues.
 – Relatively common, benign, self-limited cause of red eye.
 – **(2/3)** of cases are **unilateral**
 – More common in **females** than in males **(3:1)**
 – Recurrent cases may signify the presence of an underlying systemic inflammatory
   disease.

**Clinical types:**

 **Nodular:**
  • A discrete elevated area of inflamed episcleral tissue is present (Less acute
    onset and more prolonged course)
 **Simple:** (More Common)
  • Vascular congestion is present in the absence of an obvious nodule.
  • **It can be either:**
     o Sectoral (2/3 of cases)
     o Diffuse (1/3 of cases)

**Causes:**
 • **Idiopathic (most cases)**
 • Collagen vascular disease (rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus,
   seronegative spondyloarthropathies)
 • Vasculitides (polyarteritis nodosa, temporal arteritis, Behçet's disease),
 • Inflammatory bowel diseases (Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis)
 • Dermatologic disease (Rosacea)
 • Metabolic disease (gout)
 • Atopy
 • Infections (Herpes zoster, Herpes simplex, Syphilis)

**Clinical Picture:**

Symptoms:
 • Acute (simple episcleritis or gradual (nodular) onset of red eye
   [sic — the parenthesis is unclosed as printed]
 • Discomfort photophobia or tenderness.

Signs:
 • Sectoral or diffuse injection
 • Chemosis
 • Tender episcleral nodules that can be easily mobilized over the sclera
 • **Differentiation** between episcleritis and conjunctivitis can be done by
   instilling a drop of **phenylephrine 2.5%** in the affected eye. Blanching of
   the conjunctival is seen in conjunctivitis with the disappearance of hyperaemia
   while not in episcleritis.

 • Episcleritis is a **self-limited disease** that usually clears on its own
   without treatment.
 • In cases with pain or discomfort, supportive measures, such as cold compresses

================================ Scleritis ================================
(section header band)

Inflammation of the sclera, can present as a **painful red eye** with or without
vision loss

**Clinical types :**

**1. Anterior scleritis:**
 – Inflammation anterior to the insertion of the rectus muscles
 – More common (in middle aged females .. **Tutorial**)
 – Diffuse, nodular, or necrotizing in appearance.

 ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
 │ **Tutorial**                                                │
 │ • ± History of autoimmune disease (rheumatoid arthritis)     │
 │ • Eye has a characteristic bluish-violet discoloration:      │
 │     - Engorgement of the deep episcleral plexus              │
 │     - Scleral thinning                                       │
 └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

 – **Necrotizing anterior scleritis** is the most severe form:
    o Characterized by severe pain and extreme scleral tenderness.
    o With or without necrosis and exposure of the choroid.
    o **Scleromalacia perforans** is a rare form of necrotizing anterior scleritis
      without pain.

**2. Posterior scleritis:**
 – Inflammation posterior to the insertion of the rectus muscles.
 – Rare
 – Can be associated with serous retinal detachment and loss of vision as well as
   pain upon eye movement.
 – **U/S:** Scleral thickening ± RD (**Tutorial**)

[Scleritis, continued]

**Causes:**
 • **Rheumatoid arthritis** is the most common association as well as other
   collagen vascular diseases (systemic lupus erythematosus, seronegative
   spondyloarthropathies)
 • Metabolic disease (gout)
 • Infections (TB, Syphilis, Herpes zoster, ...etc)
 • Chemical or physical injuries
 • idiopathic

**Clinical Picture:**

Symptoms:
 o Gradual onset of ocular pain with globe tenderness
   (**pain** is the most important indicator for disease progression)
 o Red eye (localized or diffuse)
 o Decreased vision may be present

Signs:
 • The redness has a characteristic **violet-bluish** hue with scleral oedema and
   overlying chemosis.
 • **No blanching** occurs with **phenylephrine 10%** (D.D. from episcleritis)
 • Other signs vary depending on the location of the scleritis and the degree of
   involvement
 • In the anterior segment there may be associated keratitis with corneal thinning,
   uveitis, and trabeculitis (elevated IOP).
 • With posterior scleritis, there may be serous retinal detachment
 • Non ocular signs are important in the evaluation of the many systemic
   associations of Scleritis

**Management:**

 **Investigations:**
  Ocular B-scan ultrasonography and orbital magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) may be
  used for the detection of posterior scleritis.

 **Treatment:**
  • Scleritis is a **vision-threatening** condition that requires urgent referral
    to an ophthalmologist.
  • Oral non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDS)
  • Systemic (oral or intravenous) corticosteroids & less commonly topical
    corticosteroids.
  • Immunomodulating drugs (methotrexate, cyclophosphamide, mycophenolate,
    cyclosporin, **TNF-X inhibitors** [sic — printed "TNF-X", standard is TNF-α])
    may be required to control the systemic as well as the ocular disease.
  • Surgical intervention may be required in cases of scleral perforation or the
    presence of excessive scleral thinning with a high risk of rupture.

 **Prognosis:**
  - Visual loss is related to the severity of the scleritis.
  - Patients with mild or moderate scleritis usually maintain excellent vision
  - Scleritis may be active for several months or years before going into
    long-term remission.
  - Patients with necrotizing scleritis have a high incidence of visual loss and an
    increased mortality rate.
