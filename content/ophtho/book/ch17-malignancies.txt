# Intro to Ocular Malignancies

- The eye is an **uncommon** site for **primary** malignancies
- The **most common** intraocular malignancies are **secondaries** from other sites (see the eye
  and systemic diseases chapter).
- The most common primary intraocular malignancy in children is **retinoblastoma** (see the
  pediatric ophthalmology chapter).
- The most common primary intraocular malignancy in adults is **choroidal melanoma**.
  [「retinoblastoma」 and 「choroidal melanoma」 printed in dark red]


[Banner heading, grey band, centred:]

# Eyelid Malignancies

- The most common eyelid malignancies are basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and
  sebaceous cell carcinoma.
- **Eyelid malignancies are characterized by:**
  - Ulceration and bleeding
  - Destruction of the normal anatomy of the eyelid (e.g., loss of lashes)


## 1. Basal cell carcinoma (BCC):
[blue heading, underlined by a full-width blue rule]

- It is the **most common** eyelid malignancy.
- It is a locally malignant tumour that rarely metastasises
- Its classical site is the nasal part of the lower lid close to the medial canthus.
  [「lower lid」 and 「medial canthus」 highlighted yellow]

- Characteristics of the BCC ulcer:
  - Beaded (**rolled**) edges.
  - Dilated tortuous vessels on the ulcer margin (telangiectasia)

- It spreads by local invasion of the adjacent tissues
- **Treatment** is by complete excision with a safety margin followed by reconstruction of the
  resultant defect.

[Figure, upper right: close-up photograph of a lower lid with an ulcerated nodular lesion at the
medial end, rolled everted edge, surface vessels, and loss of lashes over the lesion.]
*Basal cell carcinoma*

[Figure, below it: photograph of both eyes of an elderly patient, an ulcerated crusted lesion at
the medial canthal region of one lower lid.]
[no caption]


## 2. Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC):
[blue heading, underlined by a full-width blue rule]

- Occurs more often on the upper lid and the lateral canthus.
  [「upper lid」 and 「lateral canthus」 highlighted yellow]
- Lesions are characterized by the overproduction of **keratin** (keratosis) giving them a flaky
  appearance.
- Ulcers characteristically have **everted** edges.
- **Modes of spread:**
  - Locally via perineural infiltration into the orbit or even the cranial cavity.
  - Lymphatic spread to locally draining lymph nodes.
  - Hematogenous to distant organs.

[Figure, lower right: close-up photograph of an upper lid carrying a raised keratotic irregular
lesion with a flaky surface, distorting the lid margin.]
*Squamous Cell Carcinoma*

## 3. Sebaceous Carcinoma:
[blue heading, underlined by a full-width blue rule]

- Common in the elderly (7th and 8th decades).
- More common in the **upper lid**.
- It arises from the sebum-secreting glands of the eyelid (e.g., sebaceous glands and glands of
  Zeiss located at the roots of the lashes)

- It can present as:
  - Chronic blepharitis
  - Recurrent chalazion
  - Chronic conjunctivitis
- It is highly invasive and metastasises easily.

[Figure, right: close-up photograph of a closed eye of an elderly patient with a firm rounded
nodule in the upper lid, overlying skin intact and slightly reddened.]
*Sebaceous Carcinoma*

⟦SHADED BOX, centred header⟧
                                   **N.B:**
⇨ Any old patient with **recurrent chalazion** must have a **biopsy** to rule out the presence of
  sebaceous carcinoma.
⇨ Old patients with chronic unilateral **blepharitis** or **conjunctivitis** need to be properly
  evaluated for the presence of sebaceous carcinoma.
⟦/BOX⟧

[Banner heading, grey band, centred:]

# Choroidal melanoma

- The **most common primary intraocular malignancy in adults**.   [printed in dark red]
- It is more prevalent in fair-skinned individuals.
- Choroidal melanoma represents more than **80%** of uveal tract tumours.
- It can be symptomless and only detected by chance or it can induce visual symptoms if it lies
  beneath the macula.
- **Ophthalmoscopically**, it appears as a solitary elevated subretinal **grey-brown** dome-shaped
  mass mostly located within 3 mm of the optic disc.

- **Other signs include:**
  - Exudative RD
  - Rubeosis iridis
  - Secondary glaucoma
  - Cataract.

- Distant metastasis (especially to the **liver**) is common.   [「liver」 printed in dark red]
- Evaluation of the presence or absence of spread is mandatory before deciding on treatment.
- **Treatment modalities include:**
  - Radiotherapy
  - Surgical excision of either the whole eyeball (enucleation) or only the tumour depending on
    its size

[Figure, right: two colour fundus photographs side by side. Left one shows a large pale
grey-brown elevated mass occupying the posterior pole with the optic disc visible at its edge;
right one shows a similar dome-shaped subretinal mass obscuring the disc.]

WHAT THE OBJECTIVES PROMISED vs WHAT THE PAGES TEACH
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The objectives flowchart names five things. Two of them are promised and then NOT taught:

- "most common"        → TAUGHT (the four-bullet "Intro to Ocular Malignancies" block).
- "eyelid malignancies" → TAUGHT in full: BCC, SCC, sebaceous carcinoma, plus a two-bullet
                          "other less common" (Kaposi sarcoma, malignant melanoma).
- "Metastases"          → ⚠️ NOT TAUGHT. A banner heading and a one-line cross-reference to
                          chapter 19. Zero content.
- "choroidal melanoma"  → TAUGHT in full (one page).
- Retinoblastoma        → ⚠️ NOT TAUGHT, and not in the objectives either, yet it gets a banner
                          heading on printed 216 with a one-line cross-reference to chapter 16
                          (Paediatric Ophthalmology). The ONLY retinoblastoma content in this
                          chapter is the three-row comparison table on printed 217, which gives
                          its epidemiology (commonest primary intraocular malignancy in
                          children), spread and treatment — nothing else.

DEPTH, stated plainly: this chapter is clinical-recognition level only.
- NO genetics anywhere — no RB1, no chromosome 13, no hereditary/sporadic split, no
  two-hit model, no "13q deletion". The only genetic-adjacent line in the chapter is
  "Screening of siblings/ offspring essential" in the retinoblastoma treatment cell.
- NO staging of anything — no TNM, no Reese-Ellsworth, no International Classification, no
  COMS tumour-size categories.
- NO work-up protocols — no EUA, no biopsy technique (except "must have a biopsy" for recurrent
  chalazion), no systemic metastatic screen, no imaging protocol. Choroidal melanoma gets one
  line: "Evaluation of the presence or absence of spread is mandatory before deciding on
  treatment", plus the tutorial box's ultrasound signs.
- NO histopathology — no Flexner-Wintersteiner rosettes, no spindle/epithelioid cell types,
  no morphoeic/nodular BCC subtypes, no Mohs surgery, no pagetoid spread.
- Treatment is named at modality level only ("complete excision with a safety margin",
  "radiotherapy", "enucleation") with no indications, doses, or decision rules.
