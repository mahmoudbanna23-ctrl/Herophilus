[Banner:] Strabismus (squint)
*You will find nerve supply & action of EOM in Examination chapter (page 15)*

**Definition:**
Strabismus is a disorder in which the eyes are misaligned so that both eyes cannot
be directed toward the object of regard at the same time.

**Pseudostrabismus:**
- It describes the situation when the eyes falsely appear deviated but -unlike true
  strabismus- the visual axes of both eyes are properly aligned.
- Examples include *prominent epicanthal folds and hypertelorism* (increased
  distance between both eyes/orbits).

**Sequalae of Strabismus:**   [sic — "Sequalae"]
  1. **Amblyopia** (before the age of **8-10 years**)
  2. **Diplopia** and confusion (in older age groups)

⚠️ That two-line block is the ENTIRE prose treatment of amblyopia in the chapter.
   Amblyopia is named four more times (printed 198 "strabismic amblyopia",
   printed 201 treatment ×2, printed 202 history "Visual acuity (amblyopia ??)")
   and is never defined, classified or described.

**Types of strabismus:**
There are several ways in which strabismus can be classified.

[Banner:] **A) Types of strabismus according to the direction of deviation:**

  a) **Horizontal strabismus:**
     – **Esotropia**: Eyes turned **inwards**. Can also be called convergent
       strabismus
     – **Exotropia**: Eyes turned **outwards**. Can also be called divergent
       strabismus

  b) **Vertical strabismus:**
     – **Hypertropia**: Eye is shifted **upwards**
     – **Hypotropia**: Eye is shifted **downwards**

[Banner:] **B) Types of strabismus according to commitance:**   [sic — "commitance"]

**a. Comitant strabismus:**   [heading spells "Comitant"; the bullets spell
                               "concomitant" — both as printed]
• Strabismus is called concomitant when the **angle (or degree) of misalignment is
  approximately equal in all directions of gaze.**
  The individual **extraocular muscles are functioning normally,** but both eyes are
  simply not directed toward the same target.
• Most concomitant strabismus has its onset in **childhood**.
• In children, it often causes the secondary development of **suppression** to
  overcome double vision and thus leads to **strabismic amblyopia**.
• Concomitant strabismus in patients **under age 6 is rarely caused by serious
  neurologic disease**.
• Strabismus arising **later** in life may have a **specific and serious neurologic
  basis.**

[FIGURE, uncaptioned: a two-panel clinical photograph of a child's eyes in two
 different gaze positions, the deviation looking the same size in both.
 ⚠️ FIGURE-ONLY: it demonstrates "equal in all directions of gaze"; the prose asserts
 it without illustrating it.]

**b. Incomitant strabismus:**
  o Strabismus is called incomitant when the **degree of misalignment varies with
    the direction of gaze.**
  o Many aetiologies can cause incomitant strabismus: **One or more of the
    extraocular muscles or nerves may not be functioning properly (paralytic
    strabismus) or** normal movement may be **mechanically restricted (restrictive
    strabismus).**
  o This type of strabismus may well indicate either a serious neurologic disorder,
    such as third cranial nerve palsy, or orbital disease or trauma, such as the
    restrictive ophthalmopathy of thyroid disease or a blowout fracture.

[FIGURE, three-row clinical photograph series with a labelled key column —

[Banner:] **C) types according to intermittency:**

**a. Intermittent strabismus:**
• Strabismus is not always manifest. The frequency and timing of the appearance of
  strabismus vary from one patient to another. **Heterophoria** is a condition that
  can be included in the intermittent strabismus spectrum.
• **Heterophoria** is a latent tendency for misalignment of both eyes that
  **becomes manifest only if binocular vision is interrupted**, such as by covering
  1 eye.
• During binocular viewing, both eyes of a patient with heterophoria are aligned
  perfectly; both eyes are directed at the same object of regard. However, when 1
  eye is covered, that eye will drift to its position of rest. Once the cover is
  removed, the eye will realign itself with the other eye.
• A minor degree of heterophoria is **normal for most individuals.**

**b. Constant (Manifest) strabismus:**
• The patient cannot fuse and the strabismus is always manifest. **Heterotropia** is
  another term for strabismus.
• In general, **tropia** refers to a **manifest deviation present when both eyes are
  open (no covers)**. Usually, binocular vision is reduced.
  Some patients, however, can demonstrate an intermittent heterotropia and thus
  achieve binocular vision part of the time.

[Banner:] **D) Types according to etiology:**

**a) Refractive esotropia:**
• This occurs in **children with high hypermetropic** refractive error and usually
  manifests at **age 2-4 years**.
• In uncorrected hypermetropia, the child has to accommodate constantly to see
  better.
• Accommodation is a part of the **triad of the near reflex (accommodation,
  convergence and miosis).**
• Excessive convergence leads to esotropia. This condition can be corrected by
  prescribing **hypermetropic glasses.**

**c) Intermittent exotropia:**
It is a common type of strabismus that presents usually in **children**.
Exotropia is intermittent and the condition usually **deteriorates and requires
surgery**.

[FIGURES, captioned:] **Intermittent Exotropia**
 [two photographs of a young adult, one eye divergent in one and straight in the
  other; plus two photographs of a boy, straight in one and exotropic in the other.
 ⚠️ FIGURE-ONLY: the same patient straight and deviated is what "intermittent"
 means; the prose never illustrates it.]

**d) Sensory strabismus:**
When vision is poor in one eye for a long time this eye may deviate causing sensory
exotropia or esotropia.

**e) Restrictive strabismus:**
– Due to a pathology causing **mechanical limitation** to the movement of the eye.
– A common example is **orbital floor fracture** caused by trauma, leading to
  entrapment of the **inferior rectus muscle**. This will restrict the movement of
  the eye **upwards**.

**f) Paralytic strabismus:**
Caused by paralysis of one of the nerves (**3rd, 4th, 6th**) that move the
extra-ocular muscles.

  a. **Sixth nerve palsy:**
     presents as *limitation of abduction* with *esotropia*
     **(the most common cranial nerve to be affected with elevated intracranial
     pressure)**

  b. **Third nerve palsy:**
     – presents as *exotropia and hypotropia* because only the lateral rectus and
       superior oblique are functioning.
     – The patient will also have *ptosis* (that will mask diplopia) because the
       oculomotor nerve supplies the levator muscle of the lid.
       (**External ophthalmoplegia**)

       **N.B.** *Mydriasis* can be present (Total (external and internal)
       ophthalmoplegia) as the oculomotor nerve supplies the parasympathetic fibres
       to the sphincter pupillae.
       (It may be a sign of the presence of an enlarging aneurysm in the **circle of
       Willis**)

  c. **Fourth nerve palsy:**
     – presents as *hypertropia and head tilt*.
     – **Congenital** fourth nerve palsy is the most common cause of head tilt in
       children.
     – A child with head tilt should be referred to the ophthalmologist to exclude
       superior oblique palsy.

⚠️ CROSS-CHAPTER OVERLAP: chapter 14 Neuro-ophthalmology (printed 182–186) also
   teaches CN III/IV/VI palsy, in more detail and with its own figures. The two
   accounts must be reconciled, not stacked. Chapter 15 adds the strabismus framing
   (which deviation results); chapter 14 adds the pupil rule, the aetiology lists
   and the recovery figures.

**Causes of paralytic strabismus:**
The following causes can be the underlying cause of palsy for **any** of the nerves
responsible for extra-ocular muscles movement:
  1. **Diabetes mellitus (DM)**
  2. **Head trauma**
  3. **Space occupying lesion in the brain and other causes of raised intracranial
     pressure**
  4. **Rarely congenital or idiopathic**

**N.B.:** Cases with **acute onset paralytic strabismus** should be investigated for
**diabetes** and an **MRI of the brain** to exclude serious causes of raised
intracranial pressure.

[Banner:] **Treatment of Strabismus:**

The most effective way to support fusion (binocular vision) is to treat the
amblyopia **and** equalize the vision:

o **Glasses** can treat some or all the esotropia in hyperopic patients and may
  decrease the frequency of deviation in a myopic individual with exotropia.

o **Surgical** correction of the misalignment may still be necessary, particularly
  in those children who develop esotropia **before the age of 6 months** (congenital
  esotropia). Even when binocular vision may not be achievable, the impact of
  disfiguring strabismus on a patient's self-image is a valid indication for surgery.

  [DASHED-BORDER BOX — TUTORIAL MARKER, inline-labelled form:]

**5. Alternate cover test:**

  - **Uses:**
    • Reveals **total deviation (Latent + Manifest)**
    • Detect heterophoria
    • Detect Intermittent squint

  - **Procedures:**
    • Maintain cover over one eye for **2-3 seconds** before rapidly shifting to the
      other eye (**break binocular fusion mechanism**)

  - **Interpretation:** In case of **no manifest squint**
    Note the movement of uncovered eye:
      - If **no movement**: **Orthophoria**
      - If **move rapidly to fixate**: **Heterophoria**
      - If **remain deviated**: **Intermittent squint**
