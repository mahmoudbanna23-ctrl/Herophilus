History / Patient complaint            [grey-banded section header]

  • A standardized eye examination will allow the physician to properly evaluate
    ocular complaints helping to provide either treatment or a perfectly timed
    referral to an ophthalmologist
  • Many serious ocular diseases and/or systemic conditions that manifest with
    ocular involvement are asymptomatic (causing harm in silence).
  • Proper history taking and performing good eye examination can help detection
    of those silent assassins ensuring that patients will be given the care they
    need; undelayed.

[BOX 1, rounded frame, blue heading]
Examples of irreversible blinding diseases that can be treatable if early
discovered:
  o Glaucoma
  o Diabetic retinopathy
  o Macular degeneration
  o Retinal detachment
  o Amblyopia (in youngsters)

[BOX 2, rounded frame, blue heading]
Vision and/or life-threatening disorders that may involve the eye include:
  o Diabetes Mellitus
  o Hypertension
  o Temporal arteritis
  o Embolic phenomena from the left side of the circulation (Heart and
    coronaries)
  o Cerebrovascular accidents (hemorrhagic or ischemic)
  o Aneurysms in the cerebral circulation.
  o Multiple sclerosis.
  o Brain tumors such as meningioma
  o intraocular malignancies such as melanoma and retinoblastoma.

The first step in ocular examination, is proper history taking.
In addition to the name and other basic data (age, gender, occupation, and
residence) that will help give clues to the patient's risk factors for many
conditions, the type of the patient's complaint will help direct the remainder
of the history taking, the examination procedures and determining emergency
interventions that will be needed afterwards.

Complaint:
  • Write the complaint in the patient's own words (In Arabic) and do not try to
    translate it.
  • Complaints encountered in ophthalmology can be classified into <u>visual and
    non-visual complaints</u>.

⚠️ [TUTORIAL — dashed-border side box with the word **Tutorial** printed in a tab
    on its top edge. Marker form (e): whole box is Tutorial.]
  1. <u>General look of the patient:</u>
       - Get idea about vision: e.g. Helped by another person → poor vision
       - Looking at the glasses (myopia, hypermetropia)
       - Abnormal head posture: paralytic squint
       - Chin elevation & wrinkled forehead: ptosis
       - One eyed person (overestimation & special handling)
  2. <u>Personal history:</u>
       - Name: communication
       - Age
       - Sex
       - Occupation:
            • Visual needs
            • E.g., Welders: FB & photophobia
       - Address / Tel
[end Tutorial box]

A. Visual complaints                     [blue heading, ruled]

  1. **Diminution (loss - decrease) of vision**

  2. **Night blindness**
     [FIGURE, two photographs side by side of the same night street scene,

  3. **Colour blindness**
     [FIGURE, two-row panel. Top row labelled "NORMAL VISION": three apples

  4. **Photophobia:** intolerance to the usual levels of illumination
  5. **Glare:** difficulty of seeing in the presence of bright light.
  6. **Diplopia:** double vision - seeing 2 images or seeing an additional
     shadow of any visualized object
  7. **Polyopia:** seeing more than two images or shadows of the same object.
  8. **Photopsia:** seeing flashes of light in the absence of a light source.
  9. **Floaters:** seeing floating black shadows in bright illumination
 10. **Duskiness:** sensation of poor illumination of the surrounding
     environment regardless of the actual level of illumination
 11. **Central Scotoma:** Seeing dark spots within the visual field of one or
     both eyes (affecting central vision or fixation)

 12. Other visual complaints related to <u>**macular diseases**</u>:
       • **Macropsia** (sensation of objects being larger than their actual size)
       • **Micropsia** (sensation of objects being smaller than their actual
         size)
       • **Metamorphopsia** (perception of distorted images of presumably regular
         objects)

History of the present illness:          [grey-banded section header]

Analysis of the chief complaint is important to help direct the examination and
form an appropriate proper differential diagnosis of the presenting case.

<u>**Analysis of any complaint should fulfil the following items:**</u>
  • Monocular or binocular
  • **Mode of onset:** sudden, acute, accidentally discovered, gradual or
    insidious.
  • **Course:** progressive, stationary, regressive, intermittent, or remittent.
  • Duration
  • fluctuating during the day or persistent
  • **Associated symptoms** e.g, headache, discharge, tearing, double vision,
    central vision affection, visual field defect, etc.

<u>**It is essential to record what has been done to the chief complaint e.g.,**</u>
  • Seeking medical advice
  • Medications prescribed and, if possible, their doses
  • Compliance to medications
  • Response to those medications
  • Interventions performed and whether the condition improved or worsened after
    such interventions.

  o Some patients interpret a visual field defect on one side (**homonymous
    hemianopia**) as a diminution of vision related to the eye on the same side,
    so it is of extreme importance to have patience and ask questions to clarify
    the complaints while analyzing symptoms.

<u>N.B:</u>
  • It is important not to jump to a diagnosis after hearing or analyzing the
    chief complaint e.g., diminution of vision can have many causes.
  • <u>By the end of the history taking process, you should've had a differential
    diagnosis list</u> in your mind that will provide you with additional
    questions that might facilitate evaluating the case and that will guide
    performing the examination (by searching for something you are already
    expecting).

  • It is <u>not uncommon to have two or more coexisting conditions</u> (e.g.,
    cataract and diabetic retinopathy) so it is more intelligent to Wait after
    completing examination before deciding the diagnosis or a brief differential
    diagnosis list for such a complaint.
  • All other complaints should be similarly analyzed individually and in
    relation to the chief complaint.
  • Treatment begins by taking proper history.
  • Sometimes, the patient's main goal is only to deliver the history (being told
    before that there is no treatment for his condition).
  • Many ocular diseases have no definitive treatment, and their management
    requires assurance through a good patient-doctor rapport (relationship).
    Ideally, the ultimate relationship is characterized as warm, therapeutic, and
    conducive to good care.
  • Maintaining proper communication with patients will facilitate educating them
    about their conditions, discussing and explaining treatment plans and
    surgical procedures, ensuring compliance, and building confidence.

  o Diabetes mellitus
  o Hypertension
  o Thyroid disease
  o Autoimmune diseases
  o Malignancy

<u>**Past ocular history (Medical and surgical):**</u>
  o History of similar conditions in the same eye or the other eye.
  o History of previous surgical interventions in the same eye or the other eye.
  o History of using spectacles or of being told to need spectacles.

  o History of using medications, especially those being used for long durations

A. The Anterior Segment:                 [grey-banded section header]

It consists of all structures lying <u>anterior to the vitreous humour</u>,
including the ocular surface.
It is composed in order <u>from anterior to posterior</u>, of <u>eight
structures</u>:

**1. Conjunctiva:**
  – It is the thin, vascular; clear mucous membrane that covers the white opaque
    part of the eyeball; the sclera (**bulbar conjunctiva**) and lines the back
    surface of the eyelids (**Palpebral conjunctiva**).
  – It protects and lubricates the lids' movement over the eyeball.
  [FIGURE, right: sagittal section with two coloured callout pills reading
   "Bulbar Conjunctiva" and "Palpebral Conjunctiva".]

**2. Cornea:**
  – It is the **transparent part of the outer coat** of the globe.
  – Its primary function is to refract (bend) light entering the eye onto the
    lens, which then focuses it onto the retina.
  – Its optical power comprises about **2/3** of the total optical power of the
    eye.
  – It is avascular and extremely sensitive to pain.

NUMBER: cornea = about **2/3** of total optical power of the eye.

**3. Sclera:**
  – It is the tough, fibrous, **white opaque part of the outer coat** of the
    globe.
  – The function of the sclera is to provide protection for intraocular contents,
    protect the retina from overexposure to light, and <u>serve as the attachment
    for the extraocular muscles</u> which are responsible for moving the eye.

**4. Anterior Chamber:** discussed before

**5. Iris:**
  – The iris is the **coloured portion** of the eye. It lies behind the cornea
    (separated from it by the AC), and in front of the lens.
  – It separates the anterior and posterior chambers of the eye.
  – Contractions of the iris smooth muscles (**sphincter pupillae and dilator
    pupillae**) help <u>regulate the amount of light that enters your eye</u> by
    controlling the size of the central aperture of the iris, the pupil.
  [FIGURE, right: colour photograph of an eye with a cut-away wedge of the iris
   drawn in red. Labels: **Dilator muscle**, **Sphincter muscle**, Iris, Pupil.
   ⚠️ FIGURE-ONLY: it is the only place the *radial* dilator vs *circular*
   sphincter arrangement is shown.]

**6. Pupil:**
  – Its reactions to both light and near stimuli help the evaluation and
    diagnosis of many ocular and central nervous system diseases.
  – The colour of the pupil is <u>jet black in the newly born and starts to
    acquire a greyish tinge as the person gets older</u> due to the continuous
    addition of new lens fibres throughout life causing light scattering; a
    process known as **senile sclerosis**.
  – **In the absence of the lens** (surgically removed or posteriorly dislocated)
    the pupil regains its **jet-black colour**

**7. Lens:**
  – It is the elastic, transparent, biconvex structure located directly behind
    the iris.
  – It is suspended in place by a series of fine fibres (**zonules**) that
    connect it to the ciliary body.
  – The function of the lens is to focus light rays onto the retina for both near
    and far objects (via changing its shape in response to contraction and
    relaxation of the muscles in the ciliary body respectively).
  [FIGURE, right, two-panel accommodation diagram, uncaptioned. Panel 1 labels:
   Iris · Pupil · "**Lens allowed to bulge**" · "**Muscle contracts like a
   sphincter on a bottom**" · "**Suspensory ligaments are slack**" ·
   Ciliary muscle. Panel 2 labels: "**Lens pulled thin**" · "**Suspensory
   ligaments are pulled tight**".
   ⚠️ FIGURE-ONLY: the mechanics of accommodation — contraction slackens the
   zonules and lets the lens bulge — is stated only here.]

**8. Ciliary body:**
  – It is the <u>middle part of the uveal tract</u> that connects the iris
    anteriorly to the choroid posteriorly.
  – It has two functions:
      1. Secretion of the aqueous humour.
      2. Accommodation to near and far vision through the effect of contraction
         and relaxation of the ciliary muscle transmitted to the lens via the
         zonules.

Age-Related Changes in the Eye and its Adnexa
                                         [grey-banded section header]

  – In the ophthalmologist's office, involutional conditions are responsible for
    most of the clinic visits.
  – Involutional (age related changes) can be categorized into three major
    categories:

**1. <u>Extraocular changes</u>:**
  • The levator aponeurosis, the tendinous insertion of the levator palpebrae
    superiors [sic] muscle, may partially <u>detach from the superior tarsal
    plate</u>, causing the upper eyelid to move downwards closer to the visual
    axis and restrict the superior field of vision (A condition called
    involutional, senile, or aponeurotic **ptosis**)
  • The lower eyelid suspensory ligaments lose their firmness causing the eyelid
    margin to rotate either towards (**entropion**) or away from (**ectropion**)
    the eyeball.
  • The previous disorganization of the normal anatomical relationships between
    the eyelids and the globe may predispose to chronic tearing (**epiphora**)
    due to dysfunction of the lacrimal pump drainage mechanism (eyelid movement
    that normally propels tears toward the puncta).
  • The conjunctiva loses both accessory lacrimal glands and goblet cells over
    time, increasing the incidence of **dry eye.**

**2. <u>Intraocular changes</u>:**
  • The crystalline lens continues to grow throughout lifetime.
    In old age, it may result in crowding of the anterior chamber angle which may
    predispose the patient to **angle-closure glaucoma**, especially in the
    hyperopic patient with an originally shallow anterior chamber.
  • Other changes in the lens include also yellowing and opacification of the lens
    nucleus (**Nuclear sclerosis**)
  • Filtration of aqueous fluid through the trabecular meshwork slows, allowing
    for a progressive increase in IOP and, finally, an increase in the incidence
    of **open-angle glaucoma**.
  • The vitreous humour degenerates and condenses (**vitreous syneresis**) which
    predisposes to the separation of the vitreous from its attachments to the
    retina and optic disc, called a posterior vitreous detachment (**PVD**).
    This can predispose the patient to retinal traction, retinal tears, and, in
    the worst-case scenario, retinal detachment.
  • Decrease of the corneal <u>endothelial</u> cell density (cells that are
    important to maintain corneal clarity) may cause some predisposed patients to
    **chronic corneal oedema**.
  • The eye **pupil becomes smaller and less reactive.**

**3. <u>Vascular and neurologic changes</u>:**
  • Arteriosclerotic changes may predispose the patient to:
      ✓ cranial third, fourth, and sixth nerve palsies
      ✓ retinal artery and vein occlusions
      ✓ anterior ischemic optic neuropathy
  • Aging causes a delay in visual pigment regeneration in the retina which can
    affect dark adaptation and may lead to **difficulty with night vision**.
  • Age-related changes in the retinal vasculature and fewer neural cells in the
    retina and visual cortex may predispose to decreased visual acuity, contrast
    sensitivity, and/or visual fields.

**1. Visual acuity (VA):**               [grey-banded numbered section header]

<u>**Definition:**</u> It is a measurement of the smallest object a person can
identify at a given distance from the eye compared to a standard.

<u>**Distance visual acuity:**</u>
  • It is usually recorded as a ratio comparing patient performance with the VA
    of a normal person.

⚠️ [TUTORIAL — dashed side box with a **Tutorial** tab on its top edge.
    Marker form (e).]
  **How to measure distance visual acuity?**
  -  <u>Equipment:</u>
       ➢ Visual acuity chart: e.g., Snellen or E chart
       ➢ Plain occluder or card
       ➢ Pinhole occluder
       ➢ Torch or flashlight
[end Tutorial box]

  [FORMULA, printed as a display fraction in blue italic:]

                              Testing distance (6 meters)
      **Visual Acuity = ─────────────────────────────────────────────────**
                    The smallest line the patient can see on the visual acuity chart

  e.g., **Visual acuity of 6/12** indicates that the patient can recognize at
  **6 meters** a symbol that a person with normal acuity can recognize at
  **12 meters**.

  • Alternative methods for recording distance visual acuity are:
      1. **Decimal notation** (e.g., **6/6 = 1.0, 6/12 = 0.5, 6/60 = 0.1**, etc.)
      2. **Feet notation** (e.g., **6/6 = 20/20, 6/9 = 20/30, 6/36 = 20/120**,
         etc.)

  • Practically, a **6 meters** distance may be considered equal to optical
    infinity.

   "**Tumbling E-chart**" — rows of the letter E in four rotations, rows

   "**Snellen's chart**" — the classic letter chart. Rows numbered 1–11 at the

<u>**Technique:**</u>
To test distance visual acuity with the Snellen visual acuity chart, tumbling E
chart or the Landolt C chart, the <u>following must be done</u>:

  1. Make sure the patient is at **6 meters** from a well-illuminated chart.
     <u>N.B:</u> If glasses are worn for distance vision, visual acuity must be
     tested **without** glasses **and with** the patient wearing them.

  2. **The right eye is tested and recorded first.**
     <u>N.B:</u>
       • Completely occlude the left eye using an <u>opaque occluder</u> or the
         <u>palm</u> of the patient's hand.
       • With children, make sure the eye is completely covered all the time to
         prevent "peeking."

  3. Ask the patient to designate the direction in which the strokes of the E
     point or the C letter's opening (or to read the letters on the Snellen
     chart) starting with the **6/6** line.

  4. If the patient could not see the **6/6** symbols, go to the larger size
     **6/9**. If, also, not seen → keep going through the **6/12, 6/18, 6/24,
     6/36** till reaching the **6/60** line.

  5. Record the acuity measurement as a ratio in which the **numerator**
     represents the distance at which the test is performed, and the
     **denominator** represents the number written beside the line the patient
     could read.

  6. Repeat the procedure for the other eye,

**Pinhole test:**                        [blue heading]
  o If the recorded visual acuity is **6/12 or less** in one or both eyes, repeat
    the test with the patient viewing the test chart through a pinhole occluder.
    The pinhole occluder may be used over the patient's glasses.
  o If VA **improves** the pinhole than that recorded before then the patient has
    a **refractive error** that requires correction with glasses or may require a
    new glass prescription if he/she is already using them.
  o ⚠️ If vision get worse, it indicates **macular lesion** **(tutorial)**
    [marker form (d): this whole third bullet is printed in blue and tagged.]

**If a patient cannot see the largest Snellen letters:**   [blue heading, ruled]

  1. <u>Reduce the distance</u> between the patient and the chart, [ **1 meter at
     a time** ] .
     → record the new distance as the numerator of the acuity designation (e.g.,
     **5/60** if the patient is 5 meters from the chart, **4/60, 3/60, 2/60 or
     1/60**).
  2. If the patient is unable to see the largest Snellen letter at one meter,
     hold up 1 hand, extend 2 or more fingers, and ask the patient to **count the
     number of fingers**.
     → record the distance at which counting fingers is done accurately (e.g.,
     **CF 50 cm**).
  3. If the patient cannot count fingers, determine whether he/she can **detect
     the movement of your hand**.
     → record a positive response as **hand motion (HM)**.
  4. If the patient cannot detect hand motion, use a penlight to determine
     whether he or she can **detect light**. → record the patient's response as
     **PL (perception of light)**, or **NPL (no perception of light)**.

<u>**Legal blindness:**</u> The **WHO** defines blindness as a best corrected
visual acuity of **3/60** in the better-seeing eye.

**<u>Near visual acuity testing:</u>**   [blue heading]
  o May be performed if the patient has a complaint about near vision or if
    distance testing is difficult or impossible (e.g., bed-ridden patients).
    In such conditions, testing near vision may be the only feasible way to the
    visual acuity.
  o If the patient wears glasses for reading, he/she should wear them during
    testing.
  o The patient holds the test card <u>at a distance of **30-40 cm**</u>. While
    the examiner occludes 1 of the patient's eyes, the patient reads the smallest
    characters legible on the card. The test is then repeated for the other eye.
  o If a standard near vision card is not available, a printed matter such as a
    newspaper may be substituted.

**<u>For the non-cooperative patient</u>**   [blue heading]
  A. Visual acuity estimation is difficult in such patients.
  B. A change in the facial expression in response to light or sudden movement
     indicates the presence of vision.
  C. A brisk pupillary response to light also suggests the presence of some
     degree of vision. The exception to this is the patient with **cortical
     blindness** (check pupillary examination).
  D. In almost all cases, referral to an ophthalmologist is recommended.

**<u>Visual acuity evaluation in newborns. infants. and children:</u>**
                                         [blue heading, ruled; the full stops in
                                          the heading are the book's own]

**A. <u>Newborns</u>:**
  – Visual acuity cannot be measured in newborns.
  – The general ocular status should be assessed through **corneal light reflex
    testing**, evaluation of the **red reflex**, **pupillary testing**, and, if
    possible, **fundus examination**.

**B. <u>Infants to 2-year-olds</u>:**
  • **To test for amblyopia** in infants (from a few months to about age of 2),
    <u>cover each eye in turn</u> with the hand or, preferably, an adhesive patch
    and note how the child reacts. The <u>infant should be able to maintain
    central fixation with each eye</u>.
    If amblyopia is present, the child will likely resist covering the "good"
    eye.
  • **Visual function**, including ocular motility, may be further assessed by
    <u>passing an interesting object</u>, such as a ring of keys, before the baby
    and noting how the infant watches and follows the moving object. Normal
    visual function is noted as **CSM** (**C**entral, **S**teady, and
    **M**aintained fixation)
  • **The rotating optokinetic drum** (a drum with vertical alternating black and
    white stripes) can provide an estimation of the visual function in such ages
    by inducing **optokinetic nystagmus** where the child follows a stripe in
    slow motion and when it disappears moves the eye quickly back to pick a new
    stripe.
  • **A picture card** may be used to test visual acuity in children <u>between 2
    and 3 years</u> of age. A card with distinct figures is given to the child.
    One figure is highlighted on the chart, and the child points to the
    corresponding figure on the card.

**2. <u>Light projection:</u>**          [blue heading]
  o It is performed when the visual acuity of the eye tested is **less than
    1/60**.
  o Using the penlight, with the patient properly occluding the other eye, light
    is directed at the eye tested from different quadrants and the patient is
    asked to determine the direction of illumination.
  o Testing can be done for the previously shown quadrants in the confrontation
    method or the **4 cardinal quadrants** (superior, inferior, temporal, and
    nasal)
  o Results are recorded as either **good light projection (GLP)** or **bad light
    projection (BLP)**.

**3. <u>Other methods of assessment of the visual field:</u>**   [blue heading]
  o <u>Automated perimetry</u>: an accurate quantitative test that can be used
    for assessment and follow-up.
  o <u>Amsler grid</u>: it is used to check for field defects in the **central 10
    degrees** (central field) for each eye at a time.

**3. Inspection:**                       [grey-banded numbered section header]

  • With adequate room light, the examiner can inspect the eyelids, surrounding
    tissues, and palpebral fissure.
  • Palpation of the orbital rim and eyelids may be indicated, depending on the
    history (e.g., trauma, mass lesion) and symptoms.
  • Inspection of the conjunctiva and sclera is facilitated by using a penlight
    and having the patient look up while the examiner retracts the lower eyelid
    or look down while the examiner raises the upper eyelid. The penlight also
    aids in the inspection of the cornea and the iris.

**<u>Eversion of the upper eyelid (Conjunctiva):</u>**   [blue heading]
  o It is sometimes required to search for conjunctival foreign bodies or other
    conjunctival signs.
  o Topical anaesthetic facilitates this procedure, but it can be done without
    anaesthesia.
  o The patient is asked to look down, and the examiner grasps the eyelashes of
    the upper eyelid between the thumb and the index finger.
  o A cotton tipped applicator is used to press gently downward over the superior
    border of the tarsal plate as the eyelid margin is pulled upward by pulling
    the lashes.
  o With the penlight the examiner inspects the exposed conjunctival surface of
    the upper eyelid for a foreign body or other abnormality.
  o A cotton-tipped applicator soaked in topical anaesthetic can be used to
    remove a foreign body.
  o To return the eyelid to its normal position, the examiner releases the eyelid
    margin, and the patient is instructed to look up or, simply, blink.

**4. Assessment of the depth of the anterior chamber:**
                                         [grey-banded numbered section header]

  • Usually, the iris has a flat contour. When the anterior chamber is shallow,
    the iris becomes convex as it is bowed forwards. In such a case, the nasal
    iris is seen in shadow when a light is directed from the temporal side. As
    the shallowness of the anterior chamber increases, so does the shaded area of
    the nasal iris.
  • A shallow anterior chamber may indicate **narrow angle glaucoma** (also
    called **angle-closure glaucoma**) or a narrow angle that could dose [sic,
    for "close"] with pupillary dilation, thus inducing an attack of
    angle-closure glaucoma.
  • If a patient is <u>suspected of having a narrow angle</u>, he/she should
    <u>not be dilated</u> and should be <u>referred to an ophthalmologist</u> for
    evaluation.

**<u>Technique for assessment of the depth of the AC:</u>**   [blue heading]
  1. Shine a light from the **temporal** side of the head across the front of the
     eye **parallel to the plane of the iris**.
  2. Look at the **nasal** aspect of the iris. → If **two-thirds or more** of the
     nasal iris is in shadow, the chamber is probably **shallow** and the angle
     narrow.
  3. Repeat the test for the other eye.

  – Pupillary testing is an integral part of any neuro-ophthalmic examination.
    Pupillary size is a function of resting autonomic tone, the light reflex, the
    near reflex, and local mechanical factors.
  – Proper pupil examination requires a **dim room**; a small bright light source
    such as a bright penlight or an ophthalmoscope and a **distant object** on
    which the patient will be asked to fix.

**A. Size and shape of the pupil:**      [blue heading, ruled]

    **1 mm** is a **normal** variation in up to **20%** of the population).
  • For differences of **greater than 1 mm**, the difference in pupil size
    **should be recorded** both in very bright and very dim lighting.

**B. The Direct Light Reflex and the Near Reflex:**   [blue heading, ruled]
  o In dim room light
  o The patient is told to fix at a distant object
  o Then a bright light is shone directly into each pupil **slightly temporal to
    the visual axis**.

  [BOX, rounded frame]
  <u>N.B:</u> Placing yourself or the light directly in front of the patient will
  cause the pupils to constrict due to accommodation.

  o There should be a **brisk, simultaneous, and equal** response from both
    Pupils.

**The near reflex:**                     [dark-red heading]
  • Normally, when a person focuses on a near target, the pupils constrict due to
    a fixed neural relationship known as the **near reflex (triad): convergence,
    accommodation, and pupillary constriction**.
  • For pupils that react poorly to light, it is important to test the pupil for
    constriction with accommodation.
  • This is performed by having the patient focus with both eyes on a small,
    easily seen target (such as the patient's own finger or a pen) as it is
    slowly moved toward the patient's face.
  • The pupil that reacts weakly to light but strongly to accommodation is termed
    **light-near dissociation**. This **can signify an abnormality of the
    midbrain or ciliary ganglion.** (**MRI** is, then, mandatory to exclude a
    compressive lesion)

**C. The Consensual Reflex (Swinging-flash Light Test):**   [blue heading, ruled]

  • The swinging-flashlight test is an objective and sensitive method of

<u>**Technique:**</u>
  o Perform the test in the same way as for the light reflex; except that the
    light is swung between the 2 eyes to compare the intensity of the light
    reflex.
  o It is essential that the light is shone onto each eye for **equal amounts of
    time and from the same angle**
  o To perform the test, the light is shone into 1 eye for about **3 Seconds**,
    moved rapidly across to the other eye, shone for **3 seconds**, and then
    shifted back to the first eye.
  o This procedure is repeated several times until the examiner is certain of the
    responses.
  o Normally, the pupils should remain the same size or constrict slightly as the
    light is swung between the 2 eyes.
  o A RAPD is present when there is **repeatable dilation of both pupils without
    initial constriction** as the light is swung from the normal eye to the
    abnormal eye.

  • A RAPD almost always indicates a lesion in the **optic nerve** on the
    affected side, although diffuse retinal abnormalities or central retinal
    disorders may produce an RAPD.
  • **Cataract and vitreous hemorrhages do not affect the pupillary reaction**.

  [BOX, rounded frame]
  <u>N.B:</u>
    o An eye with an RAPD **never** presents with **anisocoria**. (Since
      stimulation of the normal eye will lead to a similar pupillary constriction
      in the affected eye)
    o A **RAPD cannot be bilateral** (since the test is essentially dependent on
      the comparison between both eyes).

**6. Extraocular muscle testing (ocular alignment and ocular movements):**
                                         [grey-banded numbered section header]

  o The patient is asked to follow an object to the **6 cardinal positions of
    gaze**. This allows testing of each muscle in its primary field of action.
  o For a case of **squint** (strabismus), testing a patient (children & adults)
    consists of (**general inspection, the corneal light reflex test,** and
    **the cover test**).

<u>N.B:</u>
  o Children up to age **3 or 4 months** (till the development of the **fixation
    reflex**) usually have uncoordinated eye movements and temporary intermittent
    strabismus.
    However, **Constant deviations at any age should be referred to an
    ophthalmologist.**
  o Children with **epicanthus** (skinfold covering the medial canthus and
    extending towards the upper eyelid and brow) in whom the nasal bridge is
    broad and flat, may give the **false appearance of having convergent squint**
    (apparent squint - pseudostrabismus – pseudoesotropia), especially if the
    head or eyes are turned slightly to the right or left.
    As the child's head grows and the nose bridge develops, the epicanthus
    becomes less obvious, and the eyes appear normal.

The cover test and evaluation of the corneal light reflex will distinguish
between pseudostrabismus (epicanthus) and true strabismus. However, it is
important to keep in mind that strabismus can also occur in the presence of
epicanthus.

**A. Inspection**                        [blue heading, ruled]
  o For infants and older children, a general inspection may reveal an obvious
    deviation.
  o The strabismic patient is asked to <u>**look in the 6 cardinal positions**</u>
    of gaze:
      – It will show whether the deviation is:
          • **concomitant** (angle of deviation equal in all positions of gaze),
            **OR**
          • **incomitant** (angle of deviation varies in different positions of
            gaze)
      – The muscle with weakness (paresis) or paralysis will be easily detected.
  o Involuntary eye jerks known as **nystagmus** may be detected. (All infants or
    children with nystagmus should be examined and followed up by an
    ophthalmologist)
  o The patient may assume an **abnormal head posture** (i.e., a tilt or turn to
    one side)
      – To reduce the nystagmus and improve the visual acuity, **OR**
      – To obtain binocular vision (avoid diplopia) in cases of cranial-nerve
        palsy.

**B. Corneal Light Reflex**              [blue heading, ruled]
  o It checks for ocular alignment.
  o It may be the only possible means for testing for strabismus in **newborns
    and young children**.
  o With the patient's eyes in the primary position of gaze (looking straight
    forward to a fixed point on the horizon with the head erect), a penlight is
    held directly and shone on the eyes by the examiner at a distance of
    approximately <u>**2/3 of a meter**</u>.
  o The examiner aligns his or her eye with the light source and compares the
    position of the light as reflected by the cornea of each eye.
  o Normally, the light is reflected on each cornea symmetrically and in the same
    position relative to the pupil and visual axis of each eye.
  o In a deviating eye, the light reflection will be eccentric and, in a
    direction, opposite to that of the deviation.
  o The amount of deviation can be estimated by the amount of displacement of the
    light reflex.

**C. The Cover Test(s):**                [blue heading, ruled]
  • They are easy to perform, require no special equipment, and detect almost
    every case of **true squint** (tropia).
  • They can be used on any patient <u>over the age of **6 or 7 months**.</u>

<u>**Technique:**</u>
  o The patient is asked to <u>look at a fixation point</u>, such as a detailed or
    interesting target (e.g., a toy) or the Snellen chart.
  o <u>Cover the fixating</u> eye and observe the other eye.
  o If the uncovered eye moves to pick up the fixation, then it can be deduced
    that this eye was not directed toward the object of regard originally when
    both eyes were uncovered.
  o If the eye moves **inward** to fixate, then it must have been deviated
    outward (**exotropic**).
  o If the eye moves **outward** to fixate, then it was deviated inward
    (**esotropic**).
  o If the eye moves **up or down**, then it is **hypotropic or hypertropic**,
    respectively.
  o **No shift** on cover testing means there is **no tropia**
  o If there is no tropia, **phoria** (latent squint or tendency of the eye to
    deviate that is corrected by the brain to achieve fusion of the images seen
    by both eyes) could still be present.
  o A **phoria** is detected by the **cover-uncover test**.
    When removing the cover, the examiner will find that the <u>eye that was
    covered will move to regain fixation</u>. The patient has an **esophoria** if
    the uncovered eye moves outward to fixate and **exophoria** if the eye moves
    inward to fixate.

**<u>Alternate cover test:</u>**         [blue heading]
  – Alternating the cover between both eyes reveals the **total deviation, both
    latent and manifest**
  – Maintain cover over one eye for **2-3 s** before rapidly shifting to the
    other eye.

**7. Intraocular pressure (IOP) measurement:**
                                         [grey-banded numbered section header]

  o Intraocular pressure (IOP) is determined largely by the outflow of aqueous
    humour from the eye: the greater the resistance to outflow, the higher the
    IOP.
  o IOP varies among individuals. An IOP of **15 mm Hg** represents **the mean**
    in a "normal" population. However, an IOP in the range from **10 to 21 mm
    Hg** is considered the **normal average**.
  o Measurement of IOP is part of a glaucoma screening examination, along with the
    ophthalmoscopic assessment of the optic cup.
  o Diagnosing open-angle glaucoma requires additional testing not available to
    primary care providers.
  o However, IOP determination can be useful when the diagnosis of acute
    angle-closure glaucoma is being considered.

**<u>IOP assessment methods include:</u>**   [blue heading]

**1. The Digital Method:**               [blue heading, ruled]
  • It is a rough estimation of the IOP.
  • It is useful when IOP is markedly high or significantly low (mild elevations
    cannot be accurately tested by this method)
  • It is also useful when comparing the patient's eyes.
  • It needs experience, so take time to practice it: first on yourself and then
    on your colleagues (with their permission).

  **N.B:** If you do not detect anything abnormal, the eye pressure may still be
  dangerously high. If the history or symptoms suggest glaucoma, or if the
  patient is using steroid medication or has recently undergone eye surgery, you
  must refer them to an ophthalmologist where the patient's IOP can be accurately
  assessed.

<u>**Technique:**</u>
  1. Ask the patient to close her or his eyes and look **down**.
  2. Place the tips of **both index fingers** on either side of the closed upper
     eyelid **above the upper border of the tarsal plate**.
  3. The middle, ring and little fingers must be resting on the patient's
     forehead (for one hand) and temple (for the other hand).
  4. Keeping both fingertips in contact with the upper eyelid, apply gentle
     pressure through the closed eyelid, first gently pressing on the eye with
     the right index finger, then with the left, and then with the right again
     (similar to testing for fluctuation in other parts of the body)
  5. Repeat the same steps on the other eye.
  6. A <u>normal</u> eye should give a feeling of <u>moderate firmness</u>; not
     hard and not very soft.
  7. It is important to compare the two eyes with one other. An eye with a very
     high IOP will feel abnormally hard and solid.

  [FIGURE, left, captioned "**Digital palpation of the eye for assessment of
   IOP**": a line drawing of two hands with the index fingertips on a closed
   upper lid and the remaining fingers on the brow and temple.]

  [FIGURE, middle, uncaptioned schematic: a globe in cross-section with two large
   arrows labelled "**Applied force F1**" and "**F2**" pressing on it, two small
   double arrows labelled "**Disp. X1**" and "**Disp. X2**", and a box labelled

**10–21 mm Hg**.

**2. Indentation (Schiötz) Tonometry:**  [blue heading, ruled]

Although the Schiötz tonometer does not give as precise measurements as other
types of tonometers, it has advantages that make it well suited for screening and
remote or mobile clinics.

<u>**These include being:**</u>
  o Inexpensive and Simple to use
  o Durable and requires little maintenance
  o It does not have electronics and does not require batteries
  o It can be stored for years between uses.

<u>**The Schiötz tonometer consists of:**</u>
  o A hollow barrel with a concave footplate and a holder.
  o A free-floating, rod-like plunger with a **5.5 gm weight** attached fits
    inside the barrel.
  o When held vertically on top of the eye, the plunger will move downwards by
    gravity and indent the cornea.
  o This very small up-and down movement is magnified by a lever arm to move a
    needle that gives a reading on a horizontal scale numbered from **0-20**.
  o A <u>firmer eye, due to higher IOP, will result in a lower indentation and a
    lower reading on the scale.</u>
  o Since the Schiötz tonometer does not measure pressure directly, a **conversion
    table**, supplied with the instrument, is used to translate scale readings
    into estimates of IOP in mmHg. To confirm the measured pressure, other
    weights (typically **7.5 g and 10 g**) are supplied that can be added to the
    plunger.

**4. Other types of tonometers:**        [blue heading, ruled]
  o <u>Handheld electronic tonometers</u> are becoming increasingly available in
    hospital emergency departments to measure IOP.
  o These battery-operated devices <u>can be used with the patient in any
    position</u>, as opposed to other devices that require the patient to be
    either seated or supine.
  o The IOP results are obtained rapidly with the electronic tonometer and
    correlate highly with those obtained by the Goldmann applanation tonometer.
  o Electronic tonometers are <u>expensive</u> and <u>require daily
    calibration</u>.
  o To perform electronic tonometry, the practitioner instils topical anaesthetic
    in the patient's eyes, separates the eyelids, and gently applies the
    calibrated tonometer to the patient's cornea. The pressure reading is noted
    in the patient's record.

<u>N.B:</u>
  o <u>Topical anaesthetics</u> applied for tonometry have little effect on the
    margins of the eyelids. If the tonometer touches the eyelids, the patient
    will feel it and squeeze the eyelids together, impeding IOP measurement. This
    can be avoided by holding the patient's eyelids wide apart with the free hand
    while applying the tonometer tip with the other hand.
  o Take care not to apply pressure to the eyeball while holding the eyelids
    apart, as it may produce a **falsely high** pressure reading.
  o If the patient is wearing contact lenses, they must be removed before IOP is
    measured.
  o Tonometry should **never** be attempted in a patient suspected of having a
    **ruptured globe**.

**<u>Technique of ophthalmoscopy:</u>**  [blue heading]

  1. Have the patient comfortably seated. With the room lights dimmed, instruct
     the patient to look at a point on the wall straight ahead, trying not to
     move the eyes.
  2. Set the **aperture wheel** to select the **large, round, white light** and
     set the **focusing wheel to zero**.
  3. Begin to look at the **right** eye about <u>**1 foot**</u> from the patient.
     <u>Use your **right** eye with the ophthalmoscope in your **right** hand</u>.
     When you look straight down the patient's visual axis at the pupil, you will
     see the **red reflex**.
  4. Place your free hand on the patient's forehead or shoulder to aid you to
     keep yourself steady.
  5. Slowly come close to the patient at an angle of about **15° temporal** to the
     patient's visual axis. Try to keep the pupil in view.
  6. When a retinal vessel comes into view, follow it as its calibre becomes
     larger to the **optic disc** which lies **nasal** to the center of the
     retina. (Consider the joining of blood vessels as arrows that point to the
     optic disc)
  7. Examine the <u>optic disc, retinal blood vessels, retinal background, and
     macula</u>.
  8. Repeat for the patient's **left** eye, holding the ophthalmoscope in your
     **left** hand and viewing with your **left** eye.

<u>N.B:</u>
  o The patient's eyeglasses must be removed before ophthalmoscopy
  o Most examiners prefer to remove their own glasses as well (optional).
  o Contact lenses worn by either patient or examiner <u>may be left in place.</u>

**The Optic Disc:**                      [grey-banded sub-header]

**<u>The normal optic disc :</u>**       [green heading]
  o It is slightly **oval in the vertical meridian** and has a **pink** colour

  o The disc edge or margin is **sharp** (easily identified from the surrounding

    (An average ratio range is **0.2-0.4**).

  o Lesions seen with the ophthalmoscope can be measured in **disc diameters**
    (<u>**1 disc diameter** equals approximately **1.5 mm**</u>).

**<u>Normal variations in the appearance of the optic disc:</u>**  [green heading]
  o The size of the physiologic cup varies among individuals.
  o The pigmented coats of the eye —the retinal pigment epithelium and the
    choroid— frequently do not reach the margin of the optic disc, producing a
    **hypopigmented crescent** Such crescents are especially common in **myopic
    eyes on the temporal side** of the optic disc.
  o On the other hand, an excess of pigment may be seen in some eyes, producing a
    **heavily pigmented margin** along the optic disc.
  o The retinal nerve fibres (i.e., ganglion cell axons) ordinarily are
    nonmyelinated at the optic disc and in the retina, but occasionally
    **myelination** may extend on the surface of the optic disc and retina,
    producing a **dense, white superficial opacification with feathery edges**.

**Retinal Circulation:**                 [grey-banded sub-header]
  • The retinal circulation is composed of the **central retinal arteries and
    veins**, visible with the ophthalmoscope
  • The central retinal artery branches at or on the optic disc into divisions
    that supply the **4 quadrants** of the inner retina.
  • A similarly arranged system of retinal veins collects at the optic disc, where
    **spontaneous pulsation** (with collapse during systole) may be observed in
    **80% of normal eyes**.
  • The ratio of normal **vein-to-artery diameter is 3:2**.
  • Arteries are usually **lighter in colour** and typically have a **more
    prominent light reflex** than veins.
  • The examiner should follow arteries from the disc and veins back to the disc
    in each quadrant, giving special attention to the **arteriovenous (A/V)
    crossing patterns**, which may uncover a diagnosis of **hypertension**.

**Fundus Background:**                   [grey-banded sub-header]

**9. Additional tests:**                 [grey-banded numbered section header]

**a. Fluorescein staining of the cornea:**   [blue heading]
  o Corneal staining with fluorescein (a **yellow dye** that **gives a green
    colour when illuminated with the cobalt blue filter** emitted light in the
    ophthalmoscope or the slit lamp) is useful in diagnosing defects of the
    corneal epithelium.
  o Fluorescein is applied in the form of a **sterile filter-paper strip**, which
    is moistened with a drop of sterile water, saline, or topical anaesthetic and
    then touched to the **palpebral conjunctiva**.
  o A few blinks will spread the fluorescein over the cornea.
  o Areas of **bright green** staining denote <u>absent or diseased epithelium</u>

  o <u>**Keep the following precautions in mind when using fluorescein:**</u>
      1. Using fluorescein-impregnated **strips** is much better than **stock
         solutions** of fluorescein because such solutions are susceptible to
         contamination with **Pseudomonas**.
      2. Have the patient remove **soft contact lenses** before application to
         avoid discolouration of the lenses.

**b. Colour vision testing:**            [blue heading]
  o The normal retina contains **3 colour-sensitive pigments: red, green, and
    blue**.
  o A developmental deficiency in the concentration or the function of 1 or more
    of these pigments causes various combinations and degrees of congenital
    colour vision defects.
  o Most of these defects occur in males through an **X-linked** inheritance
    pattern, and **8% of males** and **0.5% of females** have a **red or green**
    color deficiency.
  o Colour vision abnormalities also may be **acquired** in individuals with
    retinal or optic nerve disorders.
  o Colour vision testing is performed with the use of **pseudoisochromatic
    plates** (e.g., **Ishihara plates**), which present numbers or figures
    against a background of coloured dots. The person with abnormal colour
    discrimination will be confused by the pseudoisochromatic plates.
  o The patient should wear his/her glasses during colour vision testing if they
    are normally worn for near vision.
  o The colour plates are presented consecutively (**to each eye separately**)
    under good illumination, **preferably natural light**.
  o The type of colour defect can be determined by recording the specific errors
    and using the instructions provided with the plates.

**When to Refer an Apparently Normal Patient?**   [grey-banded header, centred]

  1. It is recommended that **patients ages 40-65** be examined by an
     ophthalmologist **every 2-4 years** (after receiving a baseline exam at
     **age 40** if not previously done), and **every 1 or 2 years for patients
     over age 65**.
  2. Children should undergo an evaluation in the **first few months of life,
     then again at 6 months, 3 years, and 5 years of age** by their primary care
     physician. Any abnormalities should be evaluated by an ophthalmologist.
  3. <u>**Decreased visual acuity:**</u>
       a) Unless the patient has been seen by an ophthalmologist and the
          condition has been confirmed as stable, the following must be done:
       b) **Visual acuity less than 6/6:**
          Any patient with visual acuity less than **6/6** <u>with a visual
          complaint(s)</u> in one or both eyes should be referred to an
          ophthalmologist
       c) **Visual acuity less than 6/12:**
          • Any patient with visual acuity less than **6/12 in both eyes** should
            be also referred to an ophthalmologist <u>even in the absence of
            complaints.</u>
          • Although many of those patients suffer only from uncorrected
            refractive errors, undetected painless but progressive loss of vision
            occurs in many disorders of the eye.
       d) **Asymmetrical visual acuity:**
          Patients with a difference in visual acuity between the eyes of **2
          lines or more** on the Snellen chart should be referred, <u>even if the
          visual acuity in 1 or both eyes is better than **20/40**.</u>
          Generally, visual function is nearly identical between the eyes; thus,
          in the absence of known causes of reduced vision, <u>asymmetry of
          visual acuity may be a sign of occult disease</u>. Patients may be
          unaware of even severe vision loss in 1 eye if the other eye sees
          normally.
       e) **Presbyopia:**
          • Presbyopia is manifested by reduced near vision with no change in
            distance visual acuity.
          • Middle-age or elderly patients complaining of this need referral for
            the prescription of appropriate correcting lenses.
  4. <u>**Abnormal appearance of the fundus:**</u>
     Fundus abnormalities can indicate significant ocular or systemic diseases.
  5. <u>**Shallow AC Depth or Elevated IOP:**</u>
     A patient suspected of having shallow anterior chamber depth (at risk for
     angle-closure glaucoma) should be referred to an ophthalmologist for further
     evaluation.

NUMBERS ON THIS PAGE: screening **40–65 y every 2–4 y**, baseline at **40**,
**>65 y every 1–2 y**; children at **first few months, 6 months, 3 y, 5 y**;
refer <**6/6** with complaint, <**6/12** both eyes without complaint,
**≥2 Snellen lines** difference even if better than **20/40**.

================================================================================
NOT COVERED BY THIS CHAPTER
================================================================================
Checked page by page. "Not covered" means the term does not appear anywhere in
printed 2–44.

EXAMINATION METHOD
  1. **Slit-lamp illumination techniques** — diffuse, direct focal, optical
     section, retro-illumination, sclerotic scatter, specular reflection — and
     what each demonstrates. Completely absent; the slit lamp gets three
     bullets.
  2. **Indirect ophthalmoscopy**, and any direct-vs-indirect comparison
     (field of view, magnification, stereopsis, image inversion, ability to see
     through media opacities). **Explicitly excluded by the book**, p.33.
  3. **Gonioscopy** and any angle grading (Shaffer, Van Herick). The chapter
     assesses AC depth by penlight shadow only.
  4. **Amsler grid** technique and appearance beyond one line naming it.
  5. **Automated perimetry** — anything beyond one line calling it accurate and
     quantitative. No Humphrey/Goldmann, no reliability indices, no printout.
  6. **Refraction** — retinoscopy, subjective refraction, autorefraction, the
     trial frame, cycloplegic refraction. The pinhole is the only refractive
     tool here. (Ch.2 is Optics and Errors of Refraction.)
  7. **Keratometry, corneal topography, pachymetry**, and central corneal
     thickness as an IOP correction factor.
  8. **Exophthalmometry / Hertel**, and any proptosis measurement. Acute
     proptosis is listed as a life-threatening emergency (p.44) with no method
     of measuring it.
  9. **Ocular imaging** — OCT, fundus fluorescein angiography, B-scan
     ultrasound, CT, MRI. (MRI is named once, p.26, as mandatory in light-near
     dissociation — not as an examination technique.)
 10. **Stereopsis / binocular single vision testing** — Titmus, Lang, Worth
     four-dot, Bagolini, synoptophore.
 11. **Prism cover test** and any measurement of deviation in **prism dioptres**.
     Deviation is estimated only in degrees, and only in a figure caption.
 12. **Park's three-step test**, forced duction test, saccades and pursuit
     testing as named manoeuvres.
 13. **Contrast sensitivity testing** as a method (contrast sensitivity is named
     once, p.17, as an age-related decline).
 14. **ETDRS / logMAR** notation. Only Snellen, decimal and feet are given.

LACRIMAL AND OCULAR SURFACE
 15. **Schirmer test**, tear break-up time, Rose Bengal or lissamine green
     staining. Fluorescein is present but only for epithelial defects.
 16. **Jones I and Jones II**, syringing, probing, dacryocystography.
 17. **Krause and Wolfring** glands by name; the **tear film's three layers**
     and any thickness figure.
 18. **Lid eversion, double** (double eversion with a Desmarres retractor).

PUPIL
 19. **Horner syndrome** workup, cocaine / apraclonidine / hydroxyamphetamine
     testing. Horner is named once, in the anisocoria table (p.25).
 20. **Adie's tonic pupil**, **Argyll Robertson pupil**, pharmacologic pupil —
     none named. Light-near dissociation is described without naming any entity
     that causes it.
 21. **Pupil size in millimetres** as a normal range. Only the 1 mm anisocoria
     threshold is given.

ANTERIOR SEGMENT DETAIL
 22. **Corneal sensation testing** (cotton wisp, aesthesiometer).
 23. **Van Herick** peripheral AC depth grading at the slit lamp.
 24. **Grading of cells and flare** (SUN or any scale). "+++" is used in the
     red-eye table without a defined scale.
 25. **Seidel test**.

POSTERIOR SEGMENT DETAIL
 26. **Fundus drawing conventions**, retinal quadrant nomenclature, clock hours.
 27. **Vitreous examination** as a step of its own.
 28. **Disc-swelling assessment** beyond C/D ratio — no papilloedema grading.

GENERAL
 29. **Any consent, hygiene, hand-washing or infection-control step** in the
     examination sequence.
 30. **Documentation conventions** — OD/OS/OU, the abbreviations used in an eye
     record. The chapter records VA as ratios and CF/HM/PL/NPL and stops there.
 31. **The corrections to the fifteen "popular misconceptions"** on p.44. The
     book lists them and says nothing about which are true or why.
