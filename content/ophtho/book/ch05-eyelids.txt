Anatomy of Eyelids                                       [banner heading]

  – These are the outer structures that protect the eyeball and help lubricate
    the ocular surface.
  – The upper and the lower lids in each eye join at the medial and lateral
    canthi.
  – The space between the upper and the lower lids when the eye is open is
    called the "palpebral fissure".
  – The lashes are present on the rounded anterior border of the lid margin and
    are normally directed outwards and superiorly in the upper lid and outwards
    and inferiorly in the lower lid.
  – The posterior lid margin is right-angled and is in close coaptation with the
    globe.
  – Each eyelid is composed of 5 layers arranged from anterior to posterior into:
      1. Skin
      2. Subcutaneous tissue.
      3. Muscular layer containing fibers of the orbicularis oculi, the muscle
         responsible for blinking and forcible closure of the eyelids. It is
         supplied by the facial (VII) nerve.
      4. Tarsal plate: A rigid fibrous structure forming the skeleton of the
         eyelid and containing the meibomian glands.
         The meibomian glands empty their oily secretions through orifices
         located on the lid margin. The virtual line connecting these orifices
         is called the "white line".
      5. The palpebral conjunctiva.

N.B:
Another line on the lid margin is the grey line which is an avascular line
marking the separation of the lid surgically into two leaflets:

  1. An anterior leaflet composed of the skin, subcutaneous tissue and the
     orbicularis muscle.
  2. A posterior leaflet composed of the tarsal plate and the palpebral
     conjunctiva.

⚠️ THE WHITE LINE AND THE GREY LINE ARE THE CHAPTER'S TWO NAMED LID-MARGIN
   LANDMARKS and the grey line is explicitly the SURGICAL splitting plane. The
   trichiasis operation on printed 78 ("mm graft in gray line") depends on it.

Chalazion (Meibomian Cyst)                               [banner heading]

Definition:
  Sterile chronic granulomatous inflammatory lesion of the meibomian glands
  caused by retained sebaceous secretions.

Incidence:
  • The most common benign non-neoplastic lesion of the eyelids
  • Commonly associated with posterior or seborrheic blepharitis.

Clinical picture:
Symptoms:
  Gradually enlarging painless rounded nodule.
Signs:
  • A firm nodule within the tarsal plate
  • An associated conjunctival granuloma may be present

Treatment:
  1. Warm compresses with gentle massage several times daily may help
     resolution, especially in early lesions
  2. Surgical treatment:
     • Intralesional steroid injection
     • Incision and curettage through the conjunctival side
     • A recurrent chalazion (especially in elderly individuals) should be
       biopsied to exclude an underlying malignancy (meibomian gland
       carcinoma).

⚠️ "Incision and curettage through the conjunctival side" is the whole of the
   operative detail — no vertical-vs-horizontal incision, no clamp, no
   anaesthetic is named.
⚠️ THIS IS THE CHAPTER'S ONLY MENTION OF AN EYELID MALIGNANCY, and it is a

Blepharitis                                              [banner heading]

Definition:  Inflammation of the lid margin that involves the lashes and their
associated glands.

Clinical picture:
There are two main clinical types of blepharitis:

1. Anterior blepharitis
  • It affects the area surrounding the bases of the eyelashes.
  • It may be seborrheic or staphylococcal

a. Seborrheic:
  – Associated with generalised seborrheic dermatitis
  – Characterized by:
      • Hyperaemic lid margin
      • The presence of greasy soft scales on the lashes. (no ulceration)
  – Treatment:
      1. Lid hygiene (warm compresses and lid scrubs with diluted baby shampoo)
      2. treatment of associated scalp seborrhoea.

Eyelid Malpositions                                      [BANNER heading]

  ⇨ Eyelid malpositions can involve both upper and lower lids.
  ⇨ Eyelid malpositions include: ptosis, entropion, ectropion, and retraction.

1. Ptosis                                                [banner heading]

Definition:
  – Drooping of the upper eyelid below its normal position, so that it covers
    more than 2 mm of the superior limbus in the primary position.

PAGE 76 (PDF 78) — Ptosis aetiology and treatment · 2. UPPER EYELID RETRACTION
================================================================================

Aetiology (Clinical types):   (important)
   ["important" is highlighted yellow in the original.]

  1. Congenital
     • Frequently sporadic, but may have a hereditary origin
     • Due to lack of development of levator muscle, resulting in a smooth lid
       with loss of tarsal fold [poor levator function]

  2. Paralytic:
     o Due to III nerve (oculomotor nerve) palsy

  3. Myogenic:
     • Due to a disease of the levator muscle e.g., muscle dystrophy, or
       myasthenia gravis
     • (Asymmetric bilateral ptosis that is more marked at the end of the day)

  4. Sympathetic:
     • As in Horner's syndrome  (affecting Muller's muscle)

  5. Aponeurotic (Senile):
     – Due to disinsertion or dehiscence of the levator aponeurosis.
     – Associated with advancing age, trauma, or ocular surgery
     – Typically shows a good levator function.

  6. Mechanical:
     • Increased lid weight from a lid mass, lid oedema or palpebral vernal
       catarrh.

  7. Traumatic

⚠️ THE LEVATOR-FUNCTION AXIS RUNS THROUGH THE WHOLE LIST AND DECIDES THE
   OPERATION: congenital = POOR levator function (printed in blue brackets);
   aponeurotic/senile = GOOD levator function. The surgery figure below turns

3. Entropion                                             [banner heading]

  • The eyelid margin is rotated inwards toward the eye.
  • This results in the lashes rubbing against the conjunctiva and cornea, with
    reflex lacrimation, foreign body sensation and red eye
  • May cause corneal abrasions with secondary corneal scarring.
    (If infectious keratitis occurs, it may lead to loss of vision)
  • Most commonly affects the lower lid.

Clinical types:
  1. Congenital
  2. Spastic (orbicularis muscle spasm)
  3. Involutional (senile) entropion:
     due to laxity of the lower lid retractors and lateral canthal ligament.
  4. Cicatricial entropion:
     – due to a shortening of the conjunctival surface of the eyelid, causing
       it to rotate inward.
     – Unlike other causes of entropion that affect lower lids only, this type
       may affect both upper and lower lids.
     – Shortening of the conjunctiva can be caused by:
         o Trauma
         o Chronic inflammation (Autoimmune ocular surface diseases e.g.,
           Stevens-Johnson syndrome)
         o Infection (trachoma)

Treatment:
  • Surgical treatment.
  • Topical medications to control ocular surface irritation and inflammation
    (mainly lubricants)

⚠️ "Surgical treatment." is the entire operative content for entropion — no
   named procedure. Contrast with ptosis, trichiasis and lagophthalmos, all of
   which name operations.
⚠️ CICATRICIAL IS THE ONLY TYPE THAT TAKES THE UPPER LID — stated for entropion
   here and again for ectropion on printed 78. It is the chapter's most
   repeated discriminator.

4. Ectropion                                             [banner heading]

  • The eyelid margin is rotated outward away from the eye.
  • This results in exposure of the palpebral conjunctiva, which becomes dry
    and irritated.
  • The patient may, also, have some difficulty closing the eye completely,
    resulting in dryness of the cornea.
  • The eyelids cannot effectively push tears toward the tear drain, resulting
    in epiphora.
  • It mainly affects the lower lid.

Lagophthalmos                                            [BANNER heading]

  – It is the inability to close the eyelids completely with the palpebral
    fissure remaining partially open during a blink and sleep.
  – The ocular surface become subject to damage from exposure.

Aetiology:                                                    [section heading]

  1. Facial nerve dysfunction (paralytic lagophthalmos):
     o It leads to weakened eyelid closure.
     o Facial nerve can be damaged by:
         a) Trauma (fractures to the skull base or mandible, surgery as in
            post-ptosis or blepharoplasty surgeries)
         b) Cerebrovascular accident
         c) Bell's palsy (a type of facial paralysis that results in a weakness
            of facial muscles on one side of the face.
            It can be idiopathic in most cases or may result from the
            reactivation of herpes simplex/zoster. Other infectious causes of
            Bell's palsy include Lyme disease, varicella, mumps, poliomyelitis,
            Guillain-Barré syndrome, leprosy, diphtheria, and botulism)
         d) Tumours in the cerebellopontine angle.

  2. Excessive scar tissue or excessive eyelid removal during surgery
     (cicatricial lagophthalmos)

  3. Physiologic lagophthalmos (Nocturnal lagophthalmos):
     • Incomplete eyelid closure during sleep is relatively common in the
       general population.
     • The presence of Bell's phenomenon, by which the eyeball (and thus the
       cornea) rotates upward and inwards upon eyelid closure, particularly
       during sleep, may serve to protect the cornea in cases of nocturnal
       lagophthalmos.
