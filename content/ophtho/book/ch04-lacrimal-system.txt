The tear film consists of three layers:                       [section heading]

  1. Outer oily layer:
      • Secreted by the Meibomian glands.
      • Its main function is to prevent/decrease tear evaporation

  2. Middle Aqueous (watery) layer:
      • It forms the main bulk of the tear film
      • Secreted from the main lacrimal gland as well as the accessory lacrimal
        glands in the conjunctiva.
      • There are two types of tear secretion:
          – Basic secretion: constant to keep the ocular surface lubricated all
            the time
          – Reflex secretion: associated with ocular irritation or emotion

  3. Inner mucous layer:
      • Secreted by the conjunctival goblet cells
      • Helps maintain the adherence and stability of the tear film on the
        ocular surface, especially the cornea.

⚠️ NO LAYER THICKNESS IS PRINTED. The three layers are named, sourced and given
   functions; no micrometre value, no percentage, no relative-thickness
   statement appears.

The lacrimal system is composed of two main parts:             [section heading]

1. Secretory:
   ▪ Composed of the main lacrimal gland, the accessory lacrimal glands,
     conjunctival goblet cells and the Meibomian glands.

2. Excretory:
   ▪ Tears drain from the ocular surface through the upper and lower lacrimal
     puncta via the superior and inferior canaliculi (the presence of a common
     canaliculus is not uncommon) into the lacrimal sac.
   ▪ From the lacrimal sac, tears flow through the nasolacrimal duct into the
     nasal cavity, behind the inferior turbinate.
     (Please refer to the anatomy section in chapter one for more details)

PAGE 64 (PDF 66) — DRY EYE DISEASE (DED) — definition and aetiology
================================================================================

Dry Eye Disease (DED)                                    [banner heading]

  – Dry eye disease is one of the most common problems affecting the general
    population.
  – It can cause problems that range in severity from mildly irritating to
    debilitating.
  – DED is a general term that describes the state of the ocular surface in
    response to a breakdown of its naturally coating tear film.
  – DED is a major reason for visits to ophthalmologists.
  – Its prevalence varies widely depending on the population studied, their
    geographic location, urbanization of communities and the criteria upon
    which DED is defined and assessed.
  – The prevalence is estimated to range from 8%-40%.
  – It is quite common (usually in a mild form) above the age of 40 (even in
    otherwise healthy individuals)

Aetiology:                                                    [section heading]

1. Aqueous tear deficiency:

   Sjögren Syndrome ( Keratoconjunctivitis Sicca [KCS] ):
     • Has an autoimmune aetiology
     • It is characterised by dry mucous membranes throughout the body.
     • It can be either primary or secondary to a variety of rheumatological
       conditions (e.g., Rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus,
       ... etc).
     • Lacrimal gland infiltration in sarcoidosis can also result in reduced
       tear production

   Non- Sjögren Syndrome causes:      [printed as a two-column list; both
                                       columns transcribed in printed order]
     o Allergies (especially ocular and nasal allergies)
     o Decreased hormones associated with aging
     o Pregnancy and associated hormonal changes
     o Thyroid eye conditions (can be due to evaporative element too)
     o Some medications e.g. ( psychiatric medicines, over the counter (OTC)
       cold medicines, antihistamines, beta-blockers, pain relievers, sleeping
       pills, diuretics, hormonal replacement, and oral contraceptives)
     o Chemical exposures / injuries to the eyes
     o Eye surgery
     o ↓ corneal sensation :
         - Contact lens use
         - Neurologic conditions e.g., trigeminal nerve dysfunction
         - Post refractive surgery ( LASIK or Photorefractive Keratectomy
           [PRK] ): typically, transient but can become chronic in some
           individuals
     o Diabetes
     o Vitamin A deficiency (rare in developed countries except in certain
       diseases such as Crohn's)

2. Evaporative dry eye disease:
     • Thyroid eye disease with lid retraction, proptosis, infrequent blinking
       and/or lagophthalmos
     • Eyelid inflammation (blepharitis)
     • Infrequent blinking, associated with staring at computer or video
       screens (nowadays these are the most frequent contributors to DED)
       →(computer vision syndrome or digital eye syndrome .. T).
       ⚠️ TUTORIAL MARKER, INLINE, form (d) — the marker covers only the two
          syndrome NAMES; the rest of the bullet is core text.
     • Environmental (dusty, windy, hot/dry)
     • Neurologic conditions e.g., stroke, Bell's palsy, Parkinsonism
     • Exposure keratitis (eg., lagophthalmos)
     • Mask-associated dry eye (MADE) became more prevalent in the COVID-19 era.

3. Mixed aetiologies:  common

PAGE 65 (PDF 67) — DED clinical picture and treatment

Clinical picture:                                             [section heading]

Symptoms:
  • Foreign body sensation or grittiness of the eyes.
  • Burning, stinging, and itching.
  • Redness
  • Tearing (the eyes become dry to an extent that reflex tearing is stimulated)
  • Discharge (mucous discharge accumulates on the lids during sleep)
  • Blurry or fluctuating vision (made worse when reading, using the computer,
    watching television, driving, or playing video games)
  • Light sensitivity
  • Heaviness of the eyelids
  • Eye fatigue

Signs:
  • Decreased tear film breakup time (TBUT)
    normally more than 10 seconds
    (if less, it is an indication of an unstable tear film)
    ─── [arrow labelled "Tutorial"] ──▶ [FRAMED BOX, blue border:]
        ⚠️ TUTORIAL MARKER, form (b) — an arrow labelled "Tutorial" pointing

        Tear breakup time test
          ▪ Fluorescein (yellow)
          ▪ Cobalt blue light (yellow + blue = green)
          ▪ Time from complete blink to appearance of the first dry spot
            (black spot)
          ▪ Normal  ≥ 10 sec
  • Ocular surface staining with fluorescein sodium dye will show punctate
    epithelial erosions (superficial punctate keratopathy [SPK]) in severe
    cases.
  • Complications may be seen like corneal erosions and/or scarring

Treatment:                                                    [section heading]

1. Medical:
   a. Artificial tears (with preservatives or preservative-free in severe cases)
      N.B: Long term use of artificial tears with preservatives should be
      avoided .
   b. Longer acting agents such as artificial tear gel and ointments at bedtime.
   c. Cyclosporine A 0.05% eye drops (increase tear production)
   d. Topical steroids for short durations help control the inflammatory aspect
      of the disease.

2. Interventional:
   a. Punctal plugs insertion (to prevent tear drainage)
   b. Lid tapping overnight or tarsorrhaphy (suturing upper and lower lids
      together to make the palpebral fissure narrower and reduce the surface
      area exposed to evaporation) in severe cases of lagophthalmos.

3. Treatment of the cause:  e.g., blepharitis

Epiphora                                                 [banner heading]

Definition:
Excessive watering of the eye with the overflow of tears at the lid margin as a
result of insufficient tear drainage.

Causes:                                                       [section heading]

1. Overproduction of tears:
   Secondary to anterior segment diseases such as:
     • Emotions and/or pain.
     • Dry eye (reflex lacrimation)
     • Ocular surface inflammation (conjunctivitis - keratitis - corneal
       foreign body)
     • Ocular surface irritation ( entropion - trichiasis)

2. Decreased drainage of tears:
   Due to a compromised lacrimal drainage system as in:
     • Malposition of the lacrimal puncta (e.g. ectropion)
     • Obstruction along any point of the lacrimal drainage system from the
       puncta to the orifice of the nasolacrimal duct under the inferior
       turbinate (eg., punctal stenosis, NLD obstruction→ Tutorial)
       ⚠️ TUTORIAL MARKER, INLINE, form (d) — the marker covers only the
          parenthetical examples "punctal stenosis, NLD obstruction"; the
          obstruction bullet itself is core text.
       ⚠️ NOTE: this line says the NLD orifice is UNDER THE INFERIOR TURBINATE,
          consistent with printed 63 but not with the "middle meatus" of
          printed 67. Recorded, not reconciled.
     • lacrimal pump failure (functional obstruction) which may occur 2ry to :
         o lower lid laxity (aging process) or
         o weakness of the orbicularis muscle (e.g., facial palsy)

Diagnosis:                                                    [section heading]
  ⇨ Check for punctal occlusion, stenosis (using slit lamp), anterior segment
    inflammation or irritation and for malposition of the puncta, lashes or
    lids.
  ⇨ Fluorescein dye evaluation of the patency of the lacrimal drainage system
    (Refer to drugs chapter)

PAGE 67 (PDF 69) — Epiphora treatment · DACRYOCYSTITIS — acute
================================================================================

Treatment:                                                    [section heading]

  1. Treatment of causes of ocular surface irritation and lid malposition.
  2. Infants with congenital nasolacrimal duct obstruction: (very Important)
       a. Digital massage of the lacrimal sac with topical antibiotics before
          the age of one year (85% resolve within the first year)
       b. Nasolacrimal duct probing after the age of one year.  It can be
          repeated only once if the first attempt was not curative.
       c. If second probing fails, silastic intubation of the lacrimal passages
          is done.
       d. Dacryocystorhinostomy is indicated if intubation fails.
  3. Dacryocystorhinostomy (DCR) (surgically connecting the lacrimal sad [sic]
     and the middle meatus of the nasal cavity with placement of either
     temporary or permanent tubes)

N.B: Probing is not curative in adults.

⚠️ THE NUMBERS ON THIS PAGE ARE THE CHAPTER'S MOST EXAM-SHAPED: 85 % resolve
   within the first year · massage BEFORE age one · probing AFTER age one ·

   ladder is four rungs and it is ordered.

   Transcribed as printed with [sic], not corrected in place.

Dacryocystitis                                           [banner heading]

  • Acute or chronic infection of the lacrimal sac.
  • It is usually secondary to Obstruction of the nasolacrimal duct.
  • The most common organisms are staphylococci or streptococci.

Clinical Features:                                            [section heading]

A. Acute Dacryocystitis:
   Symptoms:
     • Pain, redness and swelling over the lacrimal sac below the medial
       canthal area
     • Epiphora
     • Fever may be present

   Signs:
     – Very tender and tense swelling with overlying skin erythema.
     – It can progress to abscess formation.
     – Preseptal cellulitis may be present

   Treatment:
     ▪ Warm compresses and nasal decongestants.
     ▪ Systemic and topical antibiotics.
     ▪ Once the infection resolves, consider dacryocystorhinostomy DCR
     ▪ For abscess : incision & drainage (Tutorial)

B. Chronic Dacryocystitis:
   Symptoms:
     • Chronic epiphora
     • Chronic or recurrent unilateral conjunctivitis
     • Painless lacrimal sac swelling (mucocele) at the inner canthus.
   Signs:
     – In the absence of mucocele, pressure on the lacrimal sac may result in
       reflux of mucopurulent discharge from the lacrimal sac through the
       punctum (positive regurge test)
     – As in epiphora fluorescein dye evaluation of the patency of the lacrimal
       drainage system can be implemented.
   Treatment:
       Dacryocystorhinostomy (DCR)

⚠️ ACUTE-VS-CHRONIC IS TAUGHT BY CONTRAST, NOT BY TABLE. The discriminators the
   book prints are: acute = painful/very tender/tense/fever/preseptal
   cellulitis/abscess; chronic = PAINLESS mucocele + recurrent UNILATERAL
   conjunctivitis + positive regurge. Treatment differs: acute = compresses +
   decongestants + antibiotics, then DCR after resolution; chronic = DCR.

Dacryoadenitis                                           [banner heading]

Acute or chronic inflammation of the lacrimal gland (Appears in the outer
one-third of the upper lid).
(It may be a sign of a more serious pathology underlying the inflammation)

Aetiology:                                                    [section heading]

  • Infections
      – Most commonly viral Epstein-Barr virus.
      – Less common viral causes include adenovirus,varicella zoster, herpes
        simplex, rhinovirus, cytomegalovirus, or mumps.
      – Less commonly bacterial and rarely fungal or parasitic

  • Autoimmune disorders such as Thyroid Eye Disease, Sjogren's syndrome, etc.

  • Other aetiologies include inflammatory causes such as non-specific orbital
    inflammation (NSOI), sarcoidosis, or neoplastic.

       one-third of the upper lid
     – Ptosis with S-shaped deformity of the upper lid.

  • Computed tomography (CT) scan or magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) of the

  • X-ray Chest if sarcoidosis is suspected.
  • There are no specific laboratory tests (only done to diagnose an underlying
    cause e.g.. autoimmune diseases)
  • Biopsy for tissue diagnosis for atypical infections or refractory disease.

Treatment:                                                    [section heading]

  • Symptomatic treatment for viral dacryoadenitis.
  • Bacterial dacryoadenitis will require systemic broad-spectrum antibiotics.
  • Oral corticosteroids may be considered especially in idiopathic
    dacryoadenitis.
  • If chronic, treat the underlying disorder
