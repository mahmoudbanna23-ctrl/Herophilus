Dorsal midbrain syndrome:
  • Due to damage or compression of the upper brainstem (midbrain)
  • Characterised by bilateral: (3 Ls)
      – Light near dissociation ( ✗ light reflex + ✔ near reflex)
      – Loss of upgaze
      – Lid (eyelid) retraction (Collier sign).            [MNEMONIC: "3 Ls"]
  • Causes:
      o Hydrocephalus
      o Compressive lesions of the midbrain
      o Multiple sclerosis
      o Stroke
      o Midbrain haemorrhage.
  • MRI is mandatory in any patient showing manifestations of dorsal midbrain
    syndrome

[FIGURE, captioned on the image "Collier's Sign (lid retraction)": photograph of
 a face with marked bilateral upper-lid retraction, sclera visible above both
 limbi.]
[FIGURE, two-panel line drawing:

   lower — "Pupils DO constrict on a near object. ("accommodation reflex")"
 This is the only place light-near dissociation is drawn rather than named.]

Horner syndrome:
  • Caused by dysfunction of the sympathetic supply.
  • Characterised by:
      – Miosis (with anisocoria more in the dark)
      – Ptosis and anhidrosis of the ipsilateral face and neck
      – Enophthalmos
      – Heterochromia of the iris in congenital cases.
  • Aetiology:
      o Apical lung tumours (pancoast's tumour)  [life-threatening]
      o Thoracic aortic aneurysm                 [life-threatening]
      o Carotid artery dissection                [life-threatening]
      o Cavernous carotid aneurysm               [life-threatening]
      o Neck, thoracic, or spinal trauma / surgery.
      o Cervical rib
      o Demyelinating diseases
      o Brain stem infarction
    [⚠️ the tag "[life-threatening]" is printed in red beside exactly the first
     four causes and no others — a deliberate triage signal.]
  • Investigations:
      o MRI examination from the base of the brain to the mid-thoracic region
      o Carotid angiography (in acute and / or painful cases)
      o Many patients will show normal results of these investigations
        (idiopathic Horner syndrome)

Myasthenia Gravis (MG):
  • Definition:
    A chronic autoimmune condition that interferes with the neuromuscular
    transmission in skeletal muscles                  ["skeletal muscles" highlighted]
  • Epidemiology: it can affect any age or gender.
  • Clinical picture:
      1. Ptosis and double vision are the presenting signs in half of the patients
      2. May simulate any other ocular motor paresis/paralysis (3rd , 4th , or 6th
         nerves)
      3. THE PUPIL IS NEVER AFFECTED IN MG.               [highlighted, capitals]
      4. Weakness of other skeletal muscles in the body leading to fatigue with
         sustained effort.
      5. For patients with unexplained ptosis or ocular movement problems,
         myasthenia gravis should be suspected.

[FIGURE, uncaptioned: photograph of a woman with bilateral ptosis.]

III. The Optic Nerve                                          [part heading]

  ⇨ Diseases of the optic nerve can be slow and insidious or acute. They have a
    wide variety of causes:
      ▪ congenital, inflammatory, autoimmune (Demyelinating), neoplastic,
        vascular, nutritional, toxic. or idiopathic.
      ▪ Optic nerve atrophy is due to chronic optic nerve damage
  ⇨ The optic nerve consists of the axons of the ganglion cells of the retina, as
    they converge to create the nerve, they pass through the lamina cribrosa of
    the sclera creating the optic nerve.
  ⇨ The optic disc is the start of the optic nerve, which makes examining the
    optic disc through the fundus examination unique and it can give an idea about
    neurological diseases, sometimes, even before neuroimaging.

IV. Visual Pathways and their Related Field Defects.          [part heading]

  • Lesions anywhere in the visual system, from the retina to the occipital lobes,
    can produce visual field defects.
  • It is important to understand the following terms used to describe visual
    field loss:

TABLE — terminology
+------------------------------------+-----------------------------------------+
| Scotoma                            | an area of reduced or lost retinal      |
|                                    | sensitivity surrounded by areas of      |
|                                    | normal retinal sensitivity              |
+------------------------------------+-----------------------------------------+
| Hemianopia                         | Loss of half the visual field. Usually, |
|                                    | this involves loss of either the right  |
|                                    | or the left half of the visual field in |
|                                    | both eyes                               |
+------------------------------------+-----------------------------------------+
| Altitudinal hemianopia             | Loss of the superior or inferior half   |
|                                    | of the visual field                     |
+------------------------------------+-----------------------------------------+
| Homonymous hemianopia              | Loss of either the right or the left    |
|                                    | half of the visual field in both eyes   |
+------------------------------------+-----------------------------------------+
| Bitemporal (heteronymous)          | Loss of the temporal half of the visual |
| hemianopia                         | field in both eyes                      |
+------------------------------------+-----------------------------------------+

  • Optic nerve lesions:
      – produce visual field defects in the ipsilateral eye.
      – central scotoma or altitudinal hemianopia
      – accompanying afferent pupillary defect.

  • Optic chiasm lesions:
      o Give rise to visual field defects that affect both eyes.
      o Bitemporal hemianopia caused by pituitary adenoma, craniopharyngioma, or
        parasellar meningioma

  • Lesions affecting the visual pathways behind the optic chiasm:
      o Damage to the optic tract, the lateral geniculate body, the optic
        radiation, or the occipital cortex induces homonymous hemianopia.
      o Stroke is the most common cause of homonymous hemianopia, followed by
        trauma and mass lesions.
  • In addition to the characteristic visual field defects seen according to the
    site of the brain lesion, fundus exam could reveal papilledema due to
    increased intracranial pressure and mass effect of the lesion.
  • Nerve palsies and extraocular muscle paralysis could also be present if there
    is involvement of the 3rd , 4th , and/or 6th cranial nerves or their nuclei.

NOT COVERED — what a reader would expect a neuro-ophthalmology chapter to teach
and this chapter does not
================================================================================

Nothing below is supplied from outside the book. Each item is listed because it
is a standard neuro-ophthalmology topic that this chapter does not contain; a
few are explicitly delegated elsewhere and that is said.

PUPIL
  1. Adie's tonic pupil / Holmes-Adie syndrome — not mentioned anywhere.
  2. Relative afferent pupillary defect as a topic: named repeatedly as a sign,
     but the swinging-flashlight test is never described in prose — only drawn
     in the Tutorial figure on printed 187. Grading of RAPD: absent.
  3. Pharmacological pupil testing — cocaine, apraclonidine, hydroxyamphetamine
     for Horner; pilocarpine dilute/full for Adie and pharmacological mydriasis:
     entirely absent. Horner's investigation is imaging only.
  4. Congenital Horner and the "first/second/third order" localisation logic: the
     three orders appear in a figure label only; no localisation algorithm.
  5. Physiological anisocoria: absent.
  6. Near reflex triad (miosis, accommodation, convergence): never stated.

MOTILITY
  7. Internuclear ophthalmoplegia / MLF, one-and-a-half syndrome: absent.
  8. Gaze palsies, saccades and pursuit, supranuclear vs nuclear vs infranuclear
     lesions in general: absent (only dorsal midbrain "loss of upgaze").
  9. Cavernous sinus syndrome, superior orbital fissure syndrome, orbital apex
     syndrome: absent — the chapter never groups CN III/IV/VI lesions by site.
 10. Aberrant regeneration of CN III, pseudo-Graefe sign: absent.
 11. Duane retraction syndrome, Brown syndrome, congenital cranial dysinnervation:
     absent (may belong to ch.15 Squint — not checked).
 12. Park's three-step test for CN IV palsy: absent. The chapter gives the head
     tilt but never the diagnostic manoeuvre.
 13. Thyroid eye disease as a motility differential: absent from this chapter.
 14. Myasthenia: Cogan's lid twitch, fatigability testing at the bedside,
     ocular vs generalised MG classification, thymoma/thymectomy: absent.
     (Ice test IS present, in the Tutorial box.)
 15. Nystagmus: the full classification (jerk vs pendular defined, gaze-evoked,
     downbeat/upbeat, see-saw, spasmus nutans, latent nystagmus, null point and
     the head posture that goes with it) is absent. Only three benign forms are
     tabulated plus a causes list.

OPTIC NERVE
 16. Optic disc drusen / pseudopapilloedema and how to distinguish it from true
     disc swelling: absent — a notable omission given the chapter's whole
     "optic disc swelling" section.
 17. Foster Kennedy syndrome: absent.
 18. Leber hereditary optic neuropathy and the hereditary optic neuropathies:
     absent (optic atrophy lists "Congenital" as a cause with no detail).
 19. Compressive optic neuropathy as its own entity, optic nerve glioma and
     meningioma: absent here (tumours may sit in ch.17 — not checked).
 20. Traumatic optic neuropathy: absent here (likely ch.18 — not checked).
 21. Papilloedema grading (Frisén), Modified Dandy criteria for IIH, opening
     pressure in cmH2O: absent. LP is ordered but no number is given.
 22. Optic neuritis treatment in the book's own text: the only regimen printed is
     inside a Tutorial box (printed 191). The core text prescribes nothing.
 23. ONTT (Optic Neuritis Treatment Trial) and the evidence base, oral prednisone
     alone being contraindicated: absent.
 24. Neuromyelitis optica / MOG antibody disease: absent.
 25. OCT of the retinal nerve fibre layer, visual evoked potentials: absent as
     investigations anywhere in the chapter.
 26. GCA: the ACR criteria, steroid dosing figures, IL-6 inhibitors, biopsy skip
     lesions / specimen length: absent. Treatment is "high dose of systemic
     steroid" with no dose.

VISUAL PATHWAY
 27. Visual field TESTING methods — confrontation, Goldmann, automated perimetry,
     Amsler: absent from this chapter (delegated implicitly to ch.1).
 28. Why macular sparing occurs (dual blood supply of the occipital pole):
     absent — macular sparing is only labelled in a figure.
 29. Cortical blindness, Anton syndrome, Riddoch phenomenon, blindsight: absent.
 30. Visual agnosias, higher cortical visual processing, dorsal/ventral streams:
     absent.
 31. Chiasmal syndromes beyond bitemporal hemianopia — junctional scotoma /
     Wilbrand's knee: absent.
 32. Migraine aura and transient visual phenomena, amaurosis fugax as an entity:
     amaurosis fugax is named once inside the arteritic Tutorial box only.
 33. Functional / non-organic visual loss: absent.

EXPLICITLY DELEGATED (present in the book, but NOT in this chapter)
 34. Abnormal pupillary size examination → "the examination chapter" (ch.1).
 35. Pupillary examination for Argyll-Robertson → "chapter 1".
 36. Giant cell arteritis proper → "the chapter on Ocular Manifestations of
     Systemic Conditions and Diseases" (ch.19).

L21) Optic Nerve and L22) Pupil and Visual pathway are 45 pages between them and
their cached text holds 264 words in total. This chapter's 18 printed pages
cover all three of their subjects — pupil (printed 179–181), optic nerve
(187–194), visual pathway (195) — plus a fourth part the lecture titles do not
name at all (Motility Disorders, printed 182–186: CN III/IV/VI, myasthenia
gravis, nystagmus).

not re-derived: the 3 Ls of dorsal midbrain syndrome and Collier sign; the four
life-threatening Horner causes flagged in red; the ARP mnemonic; the pupil-rule
algorithm and the vasa-nervorum mechanism of pupil sparing; the CN VI figures
(20 %, < 45 y, > 55 y, 3–4 months); the ice test at 2 minutes; the three benign
nystagmus forms; the Papilledema-vs-Papillitis table; the pseudotumor cerebri
table with fenestration; the 50 % MS conversion; the NAION-vs-arteritic table
with ESR > 60 mm/hr; the four types of optic atrophy; the full field-defect
terminology table and the numbered pathway figure.
