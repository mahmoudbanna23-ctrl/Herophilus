SLIDE 3 — Corneal tumors: Surface Epithelial Tumors
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Surface Epithelial Tumors
Surface epithelial growths comprise the most common category of tumors
affecting the corneal surface.

 • Ocular Surface Squamous Neoplasia (OSSN): A spectrum of non-melanocytic
   abnormal growths ranging from mild dysplasia to carcinoma in situ.
 • Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC): The most common primary malignant tumor of
   the cornea. It appears as a white or reddish elevated plaque and can
   impair vision.
 • Squamous Papilloma: A benign epithelial growth that can present as an
   exophytic (outward-growing) mass.
 • Epithelial Inclusion Cysts: Benign, fluid-filled pockets that form from
   trapped surface cells.

SLIDE 5 — Corneal tumors: Melanocytic (Pigmented) Tumors
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Melanocytic (Pigmented) Tumors
These lesions develop from pigment-producing melanocytes and often extend
across the limbus.

 • Nevus: A common, benign, congenital or early-onset freckle or mole on the
   ocular surface.
 • Primary Acquired Melanosis (PAM): A flat, brown precancerous pigmentation
   area that requires close monitoring.
 • Malignant Melanoma: A rare but aggressive cancer that can arise directly
   on the cornea or extend from a conjunctival melanoma.

SLIDE 19 — Retinoblastoma: definition and incidence (no heading printed)
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
It is the most common primary intraocular malignancy of childhood
which arises from primitive retinal cells
 • Incidence:
   - 1 in 17000 live births
   - About 3% of all childhood cancers
 • No sexual or racial predisposition
 • Mean age of diagnosis:
   - known history: 4 months
   - bilateral disease 40% : 12 months
   - unilateral disease 60% : 24 months
   - 90% of cases : under 3 years of age
 • Trilateral RB : bilateral RB with ectopic intracranial RB (usually pineal
   gland or parasellar region)

SLIDE 20 — Retinoblastoma: Clinical features
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Clinical features

 • Symtoms:        [sic — Symptoms]
 1. Child is brought to ophthalmologist with history of yellow /white reflex
    in pupillary area sometimes called cat's eye
 2. Squint, usually convergent, at times divergent
 3. Cataract/ Bulging eye/ large eye
 • Signs:
 1. Leukocoria (60%)
 2. Strabismus (20%)
 3. Secondary glaucoma
 4. Buphthalmos (large eye, corneal edema, blue sclera)
 5. Pseudohypopyon /ocular inflammation /Proptosis
 6. Mydriasis/ hyphema /dysmorphic appearance

SLIDE 22 — Retinoblastoma: management (examination)
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
management

 • Red reflex testing
 • Examination under anesthesia
    - General examination
    - Tonometry
    - Measurement of corneal diameter, axial length of the eye
    - Examination with hand-held slit-lamp
    - Ophthalmoscopy, documenting findings with color drawing or photography
 • Ocular U/S (B-Scan)
    - size of tumor, detects calcification

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
management

 • Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI):
    - optic nerve involvement
    - the presence of an associated intracranial lesion→ Tri-lateral RB
    - cannot detect calcification
 • Systemic assessment
    - High-risk cases bone scans, bone marrow aspiration,  lumber puncture
      for CSF study        [sic — "lumber" for lumbar]

SLIDE 77 — Orbital tumors: Benign Orbital Tumors
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Orbital tumors

Benign Orbital Tumors
Benign growths are non-cancerous but can still threaten vision by
compressing internal structures or displacing the eyeball.

 • Cavernous Hemangioma: The most common benign orbital tumor found in
   adults. They are slow-growing, encapsulated vascular masses that
   typically cause a gradual protrusion of the eye (proptosis).
 • Orbital Dermoid Cyst: A congenital choristoma containing trapped
   developmental tissue elements (like hair follicles or sweat glands).
   They frequently present in childhood as a firm, smooth, painless mass
   along the upper-outer edge of the orbit.
 • Meningioma: Slow-growing tumors arising from the protective sheaths
   surrounding the brain or optic nerve. Optic nerve sheath meningiomas
   directly compress the nerve, causing painless, slowly progressive
   vision loss.
 • Schwannoma (Neurilemmoma): A benign, well-encapsulated tumor that
   originates from the Schwann cells covering peripheral nerves within the
   socket.

SLIDE 78 — Orbital tumors: Malignant Orbital Tumors  (LAST SLIDE)
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Orbital tumors

Malignant Orbital Tumors
Malignant tumors are cancerous, grow rapidly, can destroy nearby orbital
structures, and pose a risk of spreading to other parts of the body.

 • Lymphoma: The most common malignant orbital tumor in older adults. It
   frequently affects the tear-producing lacrimal gland or the orbital fat.
   It often presents as a painless, firm mass with a characteristic
   "molded" shape around the eyeball.
 • Rhabdomyosarcoma: The most common primary malignant orbital tumor found
   in children. This highly aggressive cancer of the soft tissues causes a
   sudden, rapidly worsening protrusion of the eye, often accompanied by
   swelling and redness.
 • Adenoid Cystic Carcinoma: A highly malignant and aggressive tumor
   originating inside the lacrimal gland. It is characterized by severe
   pain due to its tendency to invade local nerve fibers.
