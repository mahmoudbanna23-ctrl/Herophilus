              ① Epithelium (50 µm)                       ② Bowman's Layer (12 µm)

•   5–6 cell layers thick                     •   Acellular, condensed collagen

Bacterial Keratitis: Treatment

      CLINICAL PEARL
  Corneal scraping should be performed in most cases BEFORE starting antibiotics so that the
  causative organism can be cultured and its sensitivity determined.




  •   First-line: intensive topical fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin or ofloxacin) – hourly day 1, then taper
  •   New generation fluoroquinolones (moxifloxacin, gatifloxacin) for resistant or Gram-positive
      organisms
  •   Cycloplegic drops: reduce pain from ciliary spasm, prevent synechiae
  •   AVOID topical steroids until infectious cause is excluded or controlled

         Herpes Simplex Keratitis
Most common infectious cause of corneal blindness in developed countries
Herpes Simplex Keratitis: Types & Features

        Epithelial (Dendritic / Geographic)

•   Active virus replication in epithelium
•   Classic dendritic ulcer with terminal buds
•   Fluorescein: stains the ulcer bed
•   Rose Bengal: stains virus-laden margins
•   Geographic = large irregular lesion



           Necrotizing Stromal Keratitis

•   Combination of active virus + immune response
•   Progressive stromal necrosis & thinning
•   May lead to perforation
•   Very difficult to treat
•   Combined antiviral + steroid (careful balance)
    Disciform (Stromal Immune)

•     Immune-mediated endotheliitis
•     Central corneal disc-like oedema
•     Keratic precipitates under area of oedema
•     Treat with TOPICAL STEROIDS + antivirals
Herpes Simplex Keratitis: Critical Point

      CLINICAL PEARL
  NEVER use topical steroids in EPITHELIAL herpes keratitis – this promotes viral replication
  and can result in corneal perforation.




  •   Steroids ARE used in disciform (stromal immune) keratitis – always combined with antiviral cover
  •   The dendritic ulcer: look for linear branching pattern with terminal buds on fluorescein staining
  •   HSV is the commonest infectious cause of corneal disease in developed countries
  •   Prophylactic oral aciclovir 400 mg twice daily reduces recurrence rate by ~50%

     Ectasia · Dystrophies · Degenerations · Neurotrophic
Corneal Ectasia:

•   Group of conditions characterized by progressive corneal       Ectasia Types
    thinning & protrusion
•   Main types: Keratoconus (most common), Pellucid marginal
    degeneration, Keratoglobus
•   Iatrogenic ectasia: post-LASIK (due to insufficient residual
    stromal bed)
•   All cause irregular astigmatism → reduced best spectacle-
    corrected visual acuity
•   Screening for ectasia is MANDATORY before refractive surgery
    (corneal topography)
Keratoconus: Diagnosis & Management

                       Diagnosis

 •   Progressive bilateral (often asymmetric) corneal
     thinning and protrusion
 •   Onset: puberty; progression until 3rd–4th
     decade
 •   Risk factors: eye rubbing, atopy, Down syndrome,
     connective tissue disorders
 •   Vogt's striae: vertical stromal stress lines (stretch
     marks)
 •   Munson's sign: V-shaped deformity of lower lid
     on downgaze
 •   Acute hydrops: sudden stromal oedema from
     Descemet's rupture
•   Topography: inferior steepening, asymmetric bowtie
    pattern
                            Management




•   1. Spectacles (early, mild)
•   2. Soft / rigid contact lensesManagement
•   3. Scleral lenses (advanced astigmatism)
•   4. Corneal collagen cross-linking (CXL) – stabilize
    progression with UV-A + riboflavin
•   5. Intracorneal ring segments (ICRS / Intacs)

Corneal Collagen Cross-Linking (CXL): A Modern Tool

     CLINICAL PEARL
  CXL uses riboflavin (vitamin B2) drops to photosensitize the cornea, then UV-A irradiation to
  strengthen collagen crosslinks – it can halt or even partially reverse ectasia progression.




 •   Indication: documented progressive keratoconus in young patients


 •   CXL does NOT improve vision – it stabilizes progression
 •   Combined CXL + ICRS or topography-guided ablation for selected patients

Fuchs' Endothelial Dystrophy

           Pathophysiology & Diagnosis                     Management

 •   Abnormal endothelial cell function → loss of
     dehydrating pump
 •   Collagen deposited on Descemet's membrane →
     guttata (excrescences)
 •   Early: guttata visible on specular reflection (dark
     spots disrupting mosaic)
 •   Progressive → 'beaten metal' endothelium
 •   Corneal oedema (central stromal → epithelial) →
     bullous keratopathy
 •   Characteristic: worse vision in morning (↑
     oedema overnight)
 •   Risk factors: female sex, older age (5th–6th
     decade)

Corneal Graft Rejection: Recognition & Emergency Treatment

     CLINICAL PEARL
  In a patient with acute corneal graft rejection, EARLY intensive treatment greatly improves
  the likelihood of reversing the rejection episode and saving the graft.




 •   Signs of rejection (remember: KSS – Krachmer Rejection Line, Subepithelial infiltrates, Stromal
     oedema):
 •   • Epithelial rejection: elevated rejection line of abnormal epithelium (3 months post-op)
 •   • Stromal/endothelial rejection: KPs on donor endothelium (Krachmer spots), corneal oedema,
     Khodadoust line
 •   Symptoms: sudden blurred vision, pain, redness, photophobia → EMERGENCY
 •   Treatment: INTENSIVE topical steroids (prednisolone acetate 1% hourly), systemic steroids for
     severe cases

Clinical Scenario 1: The Contact Lens Wearer

     CLINICAL PEARL
  A 25-year-old contact lens wearer presents with acute severe eye pain, photophobia, and
  mucopurulent discharge. Slit lamp shows a 3mm central infiltrate with epithelial defect,
  hypopyon, and stromal oedema. What is the IMMEDIATE management?



 •   Correct Answer: Corneal scraping for Gram stain & culture → then start intensive topical
     fluoroquinolone (ciprofloxacin / moxifloxacin) hourly
 •   Key Teaching: Always scrape BEFORE starting antibiotics
 •   Suspect: Pseudomonas aeruginosa (contact lens, rapid progression)
 •   Add cycloplegic (cyclopentolate 1%) for pain and synechiae prevention
 •   NEVER prescribe topical anaesthetics for outpatient analgesia
 •   Admit if: central ulcer >2 mm, threat of perforation, poor compliance, or no improvement in 48 h
Clinical Scenario 2: The Dendritic Ulcer

      CLINICAL PEARL
  A 30-year-old presents with a 3-day history of mild eye pain, photophobia, and redness. Slit
  lamp with fluorescein shows a linear branching lesion with terminal buds in the central
  cornea. What should you do?



  •   Correct Answer: Topical antiviral (aciclovir 3% ointment 5x/day or ganciclovir gel) – NO TOPICAL
      STEROIDS
  •   Diagnosis: Herpes simplex epithelial (dendritic) keratitis
  •   Key feature: terminal buds on branches = pathognomonic of HSV dendrite
  •   Do NOT use steroids at this stage → risk of geographic ulcer → perforation
  •   Debridement of infected epithelium (swab) can be adjunctive
  •   Recurrences: consider prophylactic oral aciclovir 400 mg BD
Clinical Scenario 3: The Blurred Morning Vision

     CLINICAL PEARL
  A 55-year-old woman presents with gradually worsening blurred vision, worse in the
  morning and improving through the day. Slit lamp shows subtle guttata on the endothelium
  and mild central corneal oedema. What is the diagnosis?



 •   Correct Answer: Fuchs' Endothelial Corneal Dystrophy
 •   Classic symptom: morning visual blur (overnight corneal dehydration fails → maximum oedema on
     waking; evaporation reduces oedema through day)
 •   Guttata: collagen excrescences on Descemet's → 'beaten metal' endothelium on specular
     microscopy
 •   Conservative: hypertonic NaCl 5%, hair dryer held 10 cm from face
 •   Surgical: DMEK (preferred) or DSAEK when vision significantly affected
 •   Endothelial cell density: monitor with specular microscopy
