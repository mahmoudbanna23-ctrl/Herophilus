SLIDE 14 — Investigations
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 • Computed tomography (CT) is useful for depicting bony structures and the
   location and size of space-occupying lesions.
 • Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can demonstrate orbital apex lesions,
   orbital tumours and imaging orbital inflammatory disease.
 • Plain X-rays are little used except for the initial diagnosis of traumatic
   bony injury.
 • Ultrasonography
 • Fine needle biopsy in suspected neoplastic disease.

SLIDE 15 — THYROID EYE DISEASE / Graves ophthalmopathy
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 • Graves disease, the most common form of hyperthyroidism, is an autoimmune
   disorder in which IgG antibodies bind to thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH)
   receptors in the thyroid gland and stimulate secretion of thyroid hormones.
 • It is more common in females.
 • Symptoms including weight loss despite good appetite, increased bowel
   frequency, sweating, heat intolerance, nervousness, irritability,
   palpitations, weakness and fatigue.
 • There may be enlargement of the thyroid gland, tremor, palmar erythema and
   warm and sweaty skin.
 • Cardiac manifestations may include sinus tachycardia and other arrhythmias

SLIDE 24 — Orbital infections: 1) Preseptal Cellulitis
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 • Infection of the subcutaneous tissues anterior to the orbital septum.
 • Organisms are Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus pyogenes
 • Causes including:
   ➤ Local skin trauma such as laceration or insect bites
   ➤ Spread from periocular infection as an acute hordeolum, dacryocystitis,
     conjunctivitis or sinusitis
   ➤ Haematogenous spread from remote infection such as the upper respiratory
     tract or middle ear.
 • Manifests with a swollen, often firm, tender red eyelid that may be very
   severe.
 • However, in contrast to orbital cellulitis, proptosis and chemosis are
   ABSENT and VA, pupillary reactions and ocular motility are UNIMPAIRED.

SLIDE 29 — Bacterial Orbital Cellulitis: Investigations and Treatment
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Investigations:
 • Ascertainment of tetanus immunization status in cases of trauma.
 • White cell count.
 • Blood cultures.
 • Culture of nasal discharge.
 • High-resolution CT of the orbit, sinuses and brain.
 • MRI.
 • Lumbar puncture if meningeal or cerebral signs develop.

Treatment:
 • Hospital admission is mandatory,
 • Intravenous Antibiotics (aerobe and anaerobe coverage till culture and
   sensitivity are obtained)
 • Intravenous antibiotics should be continued until the patient has been
   apyrexial for 4 days, followed by 1–3 weeks of oral treatment.
 • Monitoring of optic nerve function
 • Surgery — Drainage of an orbital abscess should be considered at an early
   stage.
 • Severe optic nerve compression may warrant an [orbital decompression — text
   cut off in the original]

SLIDE 30 — Orbital infections: 3) Rhino-orbital mucormycosis
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 • Mucormycosis is a rare aggressive and often fatal infection caused by fungi
   of the family Mucoraceae.
 • It typically affects patients with diabetic ketoacidosis or immunosuppression
 • Invasion of blood vessels by the hyphae results in occlusive vasculitis with
   infarction of orbital tissues.
 • Infarction superimposed on septic necrosis is responsible for the classic
   BLACK ESCHAR

Treatment
 • Correction of the immune status
 • Intravenous antifungal treatment.
 • Daily packing and irrigation of the involved areas with antifungal agent.
 • Wide excision of devitalized and necrotic tissues.
 • Exenteration may be required
 • Adjunctive hyperbaric oxygen may be helpful

SLIDE 32 — NON-NEOPLASTIC VASCULAR ABNORMALITIES: 1) Cavernous sinus thrombosis
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 ➤ Clotting within the cavernous sinus, usually resulting from infection such
   as sinusitis, orbital or preseptal cellulitis or otitis.
 ➤ There is a high mortality rate: 20% treated and up to 100% untreated die.
 ➤ Rapid onset and may include severe headache, malaise, nausea and vomiting
 ➤ Unilateral or often BILATERAL proptosis, chemosis, congestion of the facial,
   conjunctival and retinal veins
 ➤ Reduced vision and, ocular motility signs (III to VI CRN affection)
 ➤ Diagnosis is MRI and MRI venography.
 ➤ Treatment consists of intravenous antibiotics and sometimes surgical
   drainage. [last line partly cut off in the original]

SLIDE 33 — NON-NEOPLASTIC VASCULAR ABNORMALITIES: 2) Carotid–cavernous fistula
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 • Development of an arteriovenous fistula between the carotid artery and the
   venous cavernous sinus with a rise in venous pressure in the sinus and
   structures draining to it.

Carotid–cavernous fistulae are classified:
 • Direct fistulae are HIGH-FLOW SHUNTS in which carotid artery blood passes
   directly into the cavernous sinus through a defect in the wall of the
   intracavernous portion of the internal carotid artery as a result of
   TRAUMA (75%).
 • Indirect fistula ('dural shunt'), the intracavernous portion of the internal
   carotid artery remains intact. Arterial blood flows through the meningeal
   branches of the external or internal carotid arteries indirectly into the
   cavernous sinus and the clinical features are subtler than in a direct
   fistula such that the condition may be overlooked. [text cut off in the
   original]


─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 34 — Carotid–cavernous fistula: DIRECT CCF
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Presentation may be days or weeks after head injury with a CLASSIC TRIAD of
PULSATILE PROPTOSIS, CONJUNCTIVAL CHEMOSIS and a WHOOSHING NOISE IN THE HEAD
 • Visual affection
 • Marked epibulbar vascular dilatation
 • Chemosis
 • Pulsatile proptosis associated with a bruit and a thrill, both of which can
   be abolished by ipsilateral carotid compression in the neck.
 • Increased IOP due to elevated episcleral venous pressure
 • Anterior segment ischaemia, characterized by corneal epithelial oedema,
   aqueous cells and flare and in severe cases iris atrophy, cataract and
   rubeosis iridis.
 • Ptosis due to third nerve involvement.
 • Fundus examination may show optic disc [swelling — text cut off in the
   original]
