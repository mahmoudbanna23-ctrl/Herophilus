⚠️ CONVENTIONS ADOPTED HERE.
  • Field-defect figures in this deck use ONE convention throughout: the BLACK
    part of the circle is the part of the field that is LOST, the grey (or
    white) part is the part still seen. Slide 24 makes this explicit by
    labelling its two columns "Left monocular visual field" / "Right monocular
    visual field". Every field figure below is reported as which half or
    quadrant of which eye is black, i.e. lost.
  • For clinical photographs the deck never says "right eye" or "left eye", so
    laterality below is given as "the eye on the left/right of the
    photograph". That is what the slide actually shows; inferring the
    patient's side from a picture would be a guess.
  • Slides 6, 11, 14 and 24 were additionally re-read from their single-slide
    renders, enlarged, because the four-up cell was too small to settle which
    pupil was which and which quadrant was black. Nothing else needed it —
    headings, bullets and small print all read cleanly at four-up.
  • Slides 17, 18 and 19 carry anatomical photographs annotated with the
    NUMBERS 1–12 in yellow. NO KEY TO THOSE NUMBERS IS PRINTED ANYWHERE IN THE
    DECK. That is recorded in each block and again in the NOT COVERED list.

SLIDE 2 — Autonomic Nerve Supply
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Heading only. The whole slide under it is one labelled DIAGRAM.

DIAGRAM — a face-on drawing of the iris and pupil, the iris drawn in blue with
the radially arranged dilator fibres shown as pale spokes and the circular
sphincter drawn as a pale ring immediately around the black pupil. Two nerves
run up to the iris from below, each ending in a labelled neurotransmitter box.

Labels printed on the diagram, clockwise from the top right:
  • sphincter pupillae   (points to the pale circular ring at the pupil margin)
  • iris                 (points to the blue body of the iris)
  • pupil                (points to the black centre)
  • dilator iridis       (points to the radial spokes)
  • short ciliary nerve  (the nerve on the right, running up to the sphincter)
  • ACh                  (boxed, on the short ciliary nerve)
  • long ciliary nerve   (the nerve on the left, running up to the dilator)
  • NE                   (boxed, on the long ciliary nerve)
  • 'postganglionic neuron'  (bottom left, on the long ciliary nerve)
  • 'postganglionic neuron'  (bottom right, on the short ciliary nerve)

So the diagram teaches, without a word of prose: parasympathetic → short
ciliary nerves → ACh → sphincter pupillae; sympathetic → long ciliary nerves →
NE → dilator iridis.

SLIDE 6 — Relative Afferent Pupillary Defect
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Heading. The only other printed text is a red caption:

Marcus Gunn Pupil

PHOTOGRAPH SERIES (left of the slide) — four stacked clinical photographs of
the SAME patient's two eyes, a swinging-flashlight test read from top to
bottom. A black arrow is drawn under the eye being illuminated in each frame:

  Frame 1 — no arrow. Both pupils small and equal (baseline).
  Frame 2 — arrow beneath the eye on the LEFT of the photograph. BOTH pupils
            are dilated.
  Frame 3 — arrow beneath the eye on the RIGHT of the photograph. BOTH pupils
            are constricted.
  Frame 4 — arrow beneath the eye on the LEFT of the photograph again. BOTH
            pupils are dilated again.

So the eye on the LEFT of the photograph is the defective one: light on it
produces less constriction — the pupils actually dilate as the torch swings
across to it — while light on the fellow eye constricts both.

DIAGRAM (right of the slide) — a simple line drawing of two eyes as two large
circles. The eye on the LEFT has a SMALL black pupil; the eye on the RIGHT has
a LARGER black pupil. A small square light source is drawn below and between
them with rays directed at the left circle. Same teaching as the photographs,
reduced to a line drawing: light directed into one eye, unequal constriction.

SLIDE 12 — Anisocoria
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Heading, then two large close-up clinical photographs side by side with a
caption under each. Those two captions are the only body text on the slide:

  In the Light   (printed in white, under the left photograph)
  In the Dark    (printed in blue, under the right photograph)

PHOTOGRAPH 1 (In the Light) — a close-up of a single hazel/brown iris in bright
illumination, with a bright corneal light reflex on the lower nasal cornea. The
pupil is SMALL.

PHOTOGRAPH 2 (In the Dark) — a close-up of the same style of hazel/brown iris

smaller. The pupil is LARGER than in photograph 1, but not hugely so.

The pair is printed to make one point that is never written down: an
anisocoria must be measured in both light and dark, because which of the two
pupils is the abnormal one depends on which condition exaggerates the
difference.

SLIDE 16 — Optic Chiasma
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Same heading as slide 15, a different lesion. One picture, one field figure,
one red caption. No bullets.

Red caption (right):
  Binasal Hemianopia

FIELD FIGURE (top of the white panel) — two circles, and this pair IS labelled,
which makes it the deck's clearest statement of the field convention:
  • Circle labelled "LE" — the RIGHT half of the circle is SOLID BLACK; the
    left half is clear.
  • Circle labelled "RE" — the LEFT half of the circle is SHADED (stippled
    grey-green); the right half is clear. A small semicircular notch is drawn
    at the centre of the shaded half.
i.e. the INNER (nasal) half of each eye's field is lost — BINASAL HEMIANOPIA.
The LE loss is drawn solid and the RE loss stippled; the deck does not say
whether the difference in shading means a difference in density.

PICTURE (below) — a stylised drawing of the chiasm in YELLOW seen from above,
with the two optic nerves running forward and the tracts back, and RED masses
drawn pressing on the chiasm from BOTH SIDES (the lateral aspects), one on each
side. Dotted outlines show the uncompressed course. A black arrow points at the
centre of the chiasm and a blue arrow at the right-hand tract. No text labels
are printed on this picture.

The teaching point drawn but not written: bitemporal loss comes from CENTRAL
(midline) chiasmal compression, binasal loss from BILATERAL LATERAL
compression of the chiasm.
