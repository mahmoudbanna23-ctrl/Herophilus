   Hyperopia:
                                         Causes

1. Axial hypermetropia:
                AL is shorter than normal (22-24.5mm)
2. Refractive hypermetropia:
      • Curvature hypermetropia:
                    Decrease curvature of the cornea (Corneal flatening)
       • Index hypermetropia:
             Decrease refractive index of lens
             Aphakia (Absence of the lens) or posterior dislocation of the lens
Hyperopia:

• Accommodation by the ciliary muscle can correct hypermetropia in younger
  patients but this ability decreases with age as the lens elasticity becomes
  less.


• By changing its shape, the crystalline lens can focus on objects at various
  distances, thus allowing a sharp image of the object of interest to be formed
  on the retina.
   Hyperopia:
                                Clinical presentation

❑ Young age (1st, 2nd and 3rd decades of life):

   ➢ Usuallydo not require glasses for correction of far vision (as hyperopia is
     compensated by accommodation)


   ➢ May complain of asthenopia (eye exhaustion with eye strain, headache and blurring
     of vision) especially with prolonged near work)


   ➢ Uncommonly develop accommodative esotropia in childhood period
   Hyperopia:
                               Clinical presentation

❑ Middle age (4th and 5th decades of life):
  Usually complain of blurred near vision and may require near vision correction (reading
  glasses) earlier than usual (early presbyopia)


❑ Old age (Above the age of 50):
  Blurring of both distance and near vision (more for near) due to markedly reduced
  amplitude of accommodation.

                                 Astigmatism

➢ It is a state of refraction in which parallel rays of light entering the eye will not
  uniformly focus to a single point on the retina but instead into two focal lines (a
  = no, stigma=point)



➢ It is due non-spherical surface of the cornea (more common) or the lens with
  the resultant difference in the refractive power at different meridians (i.e., the
  cornea surface or the lens surface have different radii of curvatures instead of
  being a part of the sphere with a single radius of curvature.

                                    Presbyopia
                            The near point of the eye

➢ The nearest point at which an object can be placed in front of the eye and still form a
  focused image on the retina.

➢ A person with myopia has a near point that is closer to the eye than normal
➢ A person with hyperopia has a near point that is farther from the eye than normal


➢ The normal accommodative power (accommodative amplitude) is 14 dioptres at the age
  of 10. It diminishes to 3 dioptres by the age of 40 and is completely lost after the age of
  60
                                     Presbyopia

➢ Definition:
           Recession of the near point (i.e., it becomes farther from the eye) with age.



➢ Near images can no longer be appropriately focused on the retina using accommodation.



➢ It is a normal aging process (usually in persons 40 years of age or older)
                                    Presbyopia

➢ Causes:


      1.    Hardening (reduced deformability) of the lens with age


      2.    Reduced accommodative amplitude with age

                                  Anisometropia
➢ A difference in the refractive status between both eyes



➢ It is the most common cause of amblyopia in children.



➢ If the difference is not high, the error can be corrected with glasses



➢ If the difference is high (usually more than 4 D difference), correction with glasses might
  lead to diplopia due to difference in the size of the image formed on the retina of both
  eyes (Aniseikonia) which is an obstacle to fusion of both images.
          Anisometropia


             Anisometropia
 The two eyes have different refractive power




               Anisiekonia
Unequal size of the retinal image of both eyes.
