SLIDE 9 — Conjunctiva: Eversion of the upper eyelid
- It is sometimes required to search for conjunctival foreign bodies or other conjunctival signs.
- The patient is asked to look down, and the examiner grasps the eyelashes of the upper eyelid
  between the thumb and the index finger.
- A cotton-tipped applicator is used to press gently downward over the superior border of the
  tarsal plate as the eyelid margin is pulled upward by pulling the lashes.
[diagram: "Twist cotton-tipped swab upward" / "Look downward"]

SLIDE 10 — Conjunctiva: Eversion of the upper eyelid
- With the penlight the examiner inspects the exposed conjunctival surface of the upper eyelid
  for a foreign body or other abnormality.
- A cotton-tipped applicator soaked in topical anaesthetic can be used to remove a foreign body.
- To return the eyelid to its normal position, the examiner releases the eyelid margin, and the

SLIDE 16 — Cornea: Staining with fluorescein
- Fluorescein: a yellow dye that gives a green colour when illuminated with the cobalt blue
  filter emitted light in the ophthalmoscope or the slit lamp.
- Corneal staining with fluorescein is useful in diagnosing defects of the corneal epithelium.
- Fluorescein is applied in the form of a sterile filter-paper strip, which is moistened with
  topical anaesthetic and then touched to the palpebral conjunctiva.
- A few blinks will spread the fluorescein over the cornea.
- Areas of bright-green staining denote absent or diseased epithelium

SLIDE 24 — Assessment of the depth of the anterior chamber
1. Shine a light from the temporal side of the head across the front of the eye parallel to the
   plane of the iris.
2. Look at the nasal aspect of the iris. If two-thirds or more of the nasal iris is in shadow,
   the chamber is probably shallow and the angle narrow.
[diagram: "Light source" arrow across a Deep iris (evenly lit) versus a Shallow iris (nasal half

SLIDE 29 — Size and shape of the pupil
- The first step in pupillary examination is to observe the resting state of the pupils in room
  light.
  - Normally, both pupils are round and equal in diameter (Isocoria)
  - Difference in size between both pupils termed anisocoria
[diagram labelled Isocoria / Anisocoria]

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 30 — Size and shape of the pupil   ⚠️ HIGH YIELD
- Anisocoria greater than 1 mm, should be assessed both in very bright and very dim lighting.
  - Anisocoria that is MORE IN DIM LIGHTING may indicate dysfunction of the dilating sympathetic
    nervous system (Horner syndrome).
  - Anisocoria that is MORE PRONOUNCED IN BRIGHT LIGHTING signifies abnormal constriction and may
    indicate parasympathetic dysfunction (third nerve palsy) or pupillary trauma.

SLIDE 45 — 1. Test for ocular alignment in primary position using corneal light reflex
Corneal light reflex
- Normally, the light is reflected on each cornea symmetrically, relative to the pupil and visual
  axis of each eye.
- In a deviating eye, the light reflection will be eccentric and, in a direction, OPPOSITE to
  that of the deviation.
- The amount of deviation can be estimated
[three photographs of children showing symmetric and eccentric reflexes]

SLIDE 49 — Red Reflex
- Light reflected off the fundus of the patient normally produces a red reflex when viewed
  through the ophthalmoscope.
- Normal red reflex is not interrupted by shadows, and is evidence that the cornea, anterior
  chamber, lens, and vitreous are clear and are not a significant source for decreased vision.
[photograph of symmetric red reflexes in a child]

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 50 — Abnormal forms of the reflex
- Opacities in the media
  (e.g., corneal scar, cataract, vitreous haemorrhage)
  appear as black shadows and can be best seen when the pupil has been dilated.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 51 — Abnormal forms of the reflex   ⚠️⚠️ HIGH YIELD

SLIDE 55 — Direct Ophthalmoscopy
1. Have the patient comfortably seated. With the room lights dimmed, instruct the patient to look
   at a point on the wall straight ahead, trying not to move the eyes.
2. Set the aperture wheel to select the large, round, white light and set the focusing wheel to
   zero.
3. Begin to look at the right eye about 1 foot from the patient. Use your right eye with the
   ophthalmoscope in your right hand. When you look straight down the patient's visual axis at
   the pupil, you will see the red reflex.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 56 — Direct Ophthalmoscopy
4. Place your free hand on the patient's forehead to aid you to keep yourself steady.
5. Slowly come close to the patient at an angle of about 15° temporal to the patient's visual
   axis. Try to keep the pupil in view.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 57 — Direct Ophthalmoscopy
6. Starts to use dial to get focus. When a retinal vessel comes into view, follow it to the optic
   disc which lies NASAL to the center of the retina. (Consider the joining of blood vessels as
   arrows that point to the optic disc)
7. Examine the optic disc, retinal blood vessels, and macula.
8. Repeat for the patient's left eye, holding the ophthalmoscope in your left hand and viewing
   with your left eye.
