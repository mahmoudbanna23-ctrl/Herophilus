SLIDE 2 — Chemical Injuries
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 ➤ They are the MOST URGENT ophthalmic emergency.   [printed in red]
 ➤ They require emergency management by the first person who sees the affected
   patient.
 ➤ Alkali burns are TWICE AS COMMON as acid burns, since alkalis are more widely
   used both at home and in industry.


─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 3 — Chemical Injuries: severity
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
The severity of a chemical injury is related to:
 ➤ The properties of the chemical
 ➤ The area of affected ocular surface
 ➤ Duration of exposure


─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 4 — Chemical Injuries: alkalis vs acids
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 • ALKALIS cause mor [sic] damage as they tend to PENETRATE more deeply than
   acids and continue penetration for WEEKS.
 • On the other hand, ACIDS COAGULATE SURFACE PROTEINS, forming a PROTECTIVE
   BARRIER against further penetration.


─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 5 — Chemical Injuries: clinical picture (1)
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 ➤ Damage to the conjunctival and corneal epithelium.
 ➤ Occlusion of the limbal vessels (LIMBAL ISCHAEMIA) and loss of LIMBAL STEM
   CELLS may lead to persistent corneal epithelial defects with sterile corneal
   ulceration and perforation.


─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 6 — Chemical Injuries: clinical picture (2)
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 ➤ Deep penetration into the cornea results in corneal stromal opacification due
   to denaturation of the corneal collagen bundles.
 ➤ Later, dry eye, symblepharon, cicatricial entropion, corneal scarring and
   vascularization can develop.


─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 7 — Grading of chemical injuries of the eye  ★ TABLE
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
| Grade | Limbal ischemia | Corneal involvement                                  | Prognosis |
|-------|-----------------|-----------------------------------------------------|-----------|
| I     | No              | Cornea clear, only epithelial damage                | Good      |
| II    | < 1/3           | Corneal haze, visible iris details                  | Good      |
| III   | 1/3 – 1/2       | Total epithelial loss, stromal haze, obscured iris details | Guarded |
| IV    | > 1/2           | Opaque cornea, with obscured iris and pupil         | Poor      |

(NOTE: `L26) Trauma II.txt` prints the SAME grading with the prognosis wording
"excellent / good / guarded / very poor". The four grades and the limbal-ischaemia
fractions are identical in both decks.)

SLIDE 8 — Grading of chemical injuries (photographs)
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 9 — Chemical Injuries: First aid treatment (1)
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 ➤ Should be STARTED IMMEDIATELY by the health care provider and an
   ophthalmologist should be called.
 ➤ COPIOUS IRRIGATION (with saline 2 L for 15–30 min) is very important to:
   • To restore the pH in the conjunctival sac back to normal as much as possible
     rapidly.
   • Decrease the duration of contact with the chemical.


─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 10 — Chemical Injuries: First aid treatment (2)
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 ➤ EVERSION OF THE UPPER EYELID: any retained particulate matter trapped in the
   conjunctival fornices is identified and removed by a sterile cotton swab.


─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 11 — Chemical Injuries: medical treatment
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Following emergency measures, medical treatment is started in the form of:
 ➤ Topical cycloplegia.
 ➤ Topical antibiotics.
 ➤ Eye patching


─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 12 — Chemical Injuries: referral
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Referral to ophthalmology emergencies for further management by:
 ➤ Topical steroids
 ➤ Systemic ascorbic acid
 ➤ Systemic DOXYCYCLINE as collagenase inhibitors.
 ➤ Elevated IOP: Oral acetazolamide or topical medications
 ➤ Prevention of SYMBLEPHARON formation by lysis of newly forming adhesions by a
   sterile GLASS ROD.


─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 13 — Chemical Injuries: surgical intervention
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Surgical intervention may include:
 ➤ Limbal stem cell transplantation
 ➤ Amniotic membrane grafting
 ➤ Conjunctival grafting
 ➤ Correction of cicatrising lid deformities e.g., cicatricial entropion.
 ➤ Keratoplasty for opaque corneas
 ➤ Keratoprosthesis (in severe cases with blindness and total limbal stem cell
   deficiency)

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

SLIDE 27 — Hyphema: Complications
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 ➤ RE-BLEEDING common in the FIRST 5 DAYS
 ➤ Uncontrolled high IOP which can cause:
   • Optic nerve damage
   • BLOOD STAINING OF THE CORNEA
   • CRAO


─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 28 — Hyphema: Treatment
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 ➤ Hospitalization: NOT EVERY CASE (children, sickle cell disease, uncontrolled
   high IOP, coagulation or bleeding disorders)
 ➤ Bed rest (45 degree)
 ➤ Cycloplegics (atropine)
 ➤ Topical steroid
 ➤ Antiglaucoma if increased IOP
 ➤ Surgical evacuation:
   • Persistent elevated IOP
   • Total hyphema > 5 days

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 49/50 — Ruling out open-globe injury  ★ CORE
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 ➤ MOST DEVASTATING of all eye injuries
 ➤ RULE OUT BEFORE detailed history and focused exam
 ➤ Suspicious history: e.g. HAMMERING, …. etc.
 ➤ Signs of suspected open globe may include:
   • PEAKED PUPIL
   • Shallow or deep anterior chamber
   • BULLOUS SUBCONJUNCTIVAL HEMORRHAGE
   • A positive SEIDEL TEST will help detect leaking aqueous


─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 51 — If you suspect an open globe or globe is ruptured  ★ CORE
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 ➤ Place EYE SHIELD & CALL for OPHTHALMOLOGIST immediately
 ➤ DO NOT PLACE A PATCH as pressure can extrude intraocular contents
 ➤ DO NOT CHECK EYE PRESSURE
 ➤ No further eye exam or eye drops or ointment on an open globe
 ➤ Instruct the patient to withhold food and fluids (NPO) preparing for GA


─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLIDE 52 — If you suspect IOFB  ★ CORE
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 ➤ The presence of an intraocular foreign body (IOFB) suggests globe penetration.
 ➤ Investigation may include:
   • Plain film x-rays of the head
   • CT SCAN
     – Determine the location and presence of a metallic IOFB.
     – Show the shape and integrity of the traumatized globe.
   • DO NOT DO MRI SCAN IF A METALLIC IOFB IS SUSPECTED.
   • Ultrasound may be useful.

CLINICAL PROBLEM 1 — Your neighbor, a 43-year-old woman, is cleaning her swimming
pool. While she is pouring some concentrated algaecide into the pool, a large
amount of this solution splashes into her right eye. When you hear her screams,
you come to her aid less than 30 seconds after the injury.
Which of the following should you do first?
 A. Bundle her into your car and speed off for the nearest emergency center.
 B. Run back to your study to look up the specific antidote for algaecide
 C. Carefully examine her eye for evidence of ocular hyperemia.
 D. Copious irrigation with water from the nearest source instructing her to hold
    her eyes open to flush out chemical
 [NO KEY PRINTED. Slide 9 answers it: irrigation is started immediately.]

CLINICAL PROBLEM 2 — 25-year-old man complaining of decreased vision and pain in
his right eye after being involved in a fist fight. There are edema and ecchymosis
of the eyelids. His visual acuity is OD 6/12 and OS 6/6, pupils are round and
reactive, normal IOP and no restriction of ocular motility. On examination of the
anterior segment, you notice a diffuse haze in the anterior chamber and early
layering of blood inferiorly.
Regarding your diagnosis; which of the following would be the most appropriate
treatment?
 A. Bed rest in 45 degree and close follow up.
 B. Assurance of patient, and no need for follow up.
 C. Hospitalization and giving patient IV mannitol.
 D. Surgical evacuation as soon as possible.
 [NO KEY PRINTED. Slide 28 answers it: hyphaema with normal IOP in an adult with
 no risk factor does not need hospitalisation — bed rest at 45° and follow-up.]

CLINICAL PROBLEM 3 — 28-year-old male patient has pain, tearing and blurred vision
in his right eye. Earlier in afternoon, while he had been working on his car with a
hammer and chisel, he noticed that something flying into his right eye. Examination
revealed that visual acuity is 6/60 in the right eye. There is conjunctival
hyperemia. The pupil seems to be peaked and pointing to the 7 o'clock position of
the limbus. There is a small, dark, slightly elevated body at the 7 o'clock position
of the limbus. The left eye is normal.
Which of the following would be the appropriate initial management for this
situation?
 A. Irrigation of the limbal foreign body
 B. Application of a protective shield
 C. Removal of the limbal foreign body using a cotton-tipped applicator
 D. Removal of the limbal foreign body using forceps
 [NO KEY PRINTED. Slides 49–51 answer it: hammering + peaked pupil = suspected open
 globe → shield and call the ophthalmologist; do not manipulate.]
