## Q1 (marked page p.232)
Endophthalmitis causes severe intraocular inflammation but usually not proptosis. Proptosis occurs in orbital pathologies like cellulitis, thyroid ophthalmopathy, or orbital tumors.

## Q2 (marked page p.234)
In adults, the most common cause of unilateral (and bilateral) proptosis is thyroid eye disease due to orbital tissue inflammation and expansion.

## Q3 (marked page p.236)
Preseptal cellulitis affects tissues anterior to the orbital septum, so eye movements and vision remain normal. In contrast, orbital cellulitis involves the orbit and causes proptosis and restricted motility.

## Q4 (marked page p.238)
A blow-out fracture of the orbital floor damages the infraorbital nerve, leading to numbness over the cheek, upper lip, and gums. Enophthalmos and restricted eye movement may also occur.

## Q5 (marked page p.240)
The normal exophthalmometric reading (corneal apex to lateral orbital rim) ranges between 12–22 mm. Values above this suggest proptosis.

## Q6 (marked page p.242)
In preseptal cellulitis, infection is confined anterior to the orbital septum, so eye movements remain normal. Proptosis, pain on movement, and chemosis occur only in orbital cellulitis.

## Q7 (marked page p.244)
A blow-out fracture of the orbital floor damages the infraorbital nerve, causing numbness over the cheek, upper lip, and upper teeth. Enophthalmos and restricted eye movement may also occur.

## Q9 (marked page p.248)
Blow-out fracture most frequently involves the floor of the orbit along the thin bone covering the infraorbital canal. Sometimes, the medial orbital wall may also be fractured. Those thin walls of the orbit separate the orbital cavity from the surrounding nasal sinuses.

## Q10 (marked page p.250)
Exophthalmos (proptosis) → Anterior displacement (forward protrusion) of the globe (measured by a ruler or by Hertel exophthalmometer).
