## Q11 (p.251) key D
Orbital floor fractures are best assessed with CT using coronal sections. CT is the imaging method that resolves bone directly, and the orbital floor lies in a plane close to horizontal, so an axial CT slice runs almost parallel to it and can miss a fracture line or under-represent how much orbital soft tissue has herniated through it; a coronal reconstruction cuts across the floor at right angles and shows the bony defect, any trap-door fragment, and any prolapse of orbital fat or the inferior rectus muscle into the maxillary sinus below far more clearly. This is why coronal CT (or CT with coronal reformats from a helical acquisition) is the standard pre-operative study for a suspected blow-out fracture.

MRI is excellent for soft tissue — orbital apex lesions, orbital tumours, inflammatory disease — but bone gives almost no MRI signal, so it does not show a fracture line well and is not the modality of choice here. Plain X-ray has a limited role, useful mainly as an initial screen for traumatic bony injury, but it lacks the spatial detail to define a floor fracture, herniated contents, or entrapment, all of which matter for surgical planning. Ultrasound has a place in orbital imaging generally but cannot penetrate or characterise bone and orbital fat behind it, so it cannot assess a fracture of the orbital floor.

The point that CT depicts bony structures, MRI serves orbital-apex and soft-tissue disease, and plain X-ray is little used beyond an initial look at traumatic bony injury is stated in the source material for orbital imaging in general (Opthalmology endpoint.pdf p.251, cross-referenced against L10) orbit.txt slide 14). The refinement that coronal sections specifically are what is needed for the orbital floor is not spelled out in that material and is added here from general medical knowledge — tagged accordingly as not taken from the course material.

## Q12 (p.253) key B
Proptosis — forward protrusion of the globe — is assessed with the Hertel exophthalmometer, an instrument that measures the distance from the lateral orbital rim to the front of the cornea for each eye. A reading greater than 20 mm is taken as indicating proptosis, and a difference of 2–3 mm or more between the two eyes is treated as suspicious regardless of the absolute value, because it is the asymmetry, not just the raw number, that flags unilateral orbital disease.

Slit-lamp biomicroscopy examines the anterior segment — cornea, anterior chamber, iris, lens — under magnification, and has no way to quantify how far forward the globe itself sits relative to the orbital rim, so it cannot substitute for exophthalmometry. Ophthalmoscopy looks at the fundus (retina, optic disc, choroidal vessels) and likewise gives no measurement of globe position. "None of the above" is wrong because the Hertel exophthalmometer is a real, standard instrument for exactly this measurement.

Because the Hertel reading is reproducible between visits (the same base setting should be used each time), it is also the practical tool for following proptosis over time in a condition such as thyroid eye disease — confirming whether it is stable, worsening, or responding to treatment — rather than a one-off diagnostic test used in isolation.

## Q13 (p.255) key C
A 38-year-old man with unilateral pulsating exophthalmos after head trauma has the classic presentation of a carotid–cavernous fistula (CCF): an abnormal arteriovenous communication between the carotid artery and the cavernous sinus, raising venous pressure in the sinus and in the structures that drain into it. Direct fistulae are high-flow shunts caused by trauma in roughly 75% of cases, where arterial blood passes straight into the cavernous sinus through a defect in the wall of the intracavernous internal carotid artery — the mechanism that fits a post-traumatic, pulsatile presentation. The classic triad is pulsatile proptosis, conjunctival chemosis, and a whooshing noise in the head; the pulsation and an associated bruit or thrill can be reduced or abolished by gently compressing the ipsilateral carotid artery in the neck, a bedside manoeuvre that supports the diagnosis.

Grave's disease (thyroid eye disease) produces a chronic, autoimmune, gradually progressive proptosis unrelated to trauma, and it is not pulsatile, so it does not fit either the trigger (head injury) or the specific sign described here. Retrobulbar haemorrhage is also a recognised, trauma-related cause of proptosis, but the bleeding itself does not produce a pulsating globe — that specific word is what separates it from a fistula, and is the discriminating feature that keeps this question distinct from a very similarly worded vignette elsewhere in this section describing a progressively expanding, non-pulsatile proptosis after trauma. Cavernous sinus thrombosis also causes proptosis (often bilateral) with reduced vision and ocular-motility signs, but its usual trigger is infection — sinusitis, orbital or preseptal cellulitis, otitis — rather than trauma, and pulsation is not one of its features.

## Q14 (p.257) key D
A fist blow during a quarrel is a classic mechanism for a blow-out fracture of the orbit, and the floor of the orbit is the most likely site to fracture. A sudden rise in intraorbital pressure from a blunt object that is larger than the orbital rim (a fist, unlike a smaller projectile) is transmitted hydraulically to the walls of the orbit, and it is the thin, unsupported floor — a shell of bone separating the orbit from the maxillary sinus below — that gives way first, allowing orbital fat and sometimes the inferior rectus muscle to prolapse or herniate into the sinus. This is also the reason the source material identifies fracture of the orbital floor with orbital fat prolapse as the leading cause of enophthalmos after trauma.

The medial wall (over the ethmoid sinus) is the next most commonly involved site, thin like the floor but generally less exposed to a direct anterior blow. The lateral wall is formed by the thick zygomatic and sphenoid bone and is the most structurally robust orbital wall, so it is rarely fractured by blunt trauma of this kind. The roof of the orbit is also comparatively strong and separates the orbit from the anterior cranial fossa; a roof fracture is uncommon and, when present, usually reflects more severe frontal trauma rather than an isolated fist blow.

This site-of-fracture detail is not spelled out at this level in the source material beyond the passing mention of orbital-floor fracture as a cause of enophthalmos, so the specific ranking of floor > medial wall > lateral wall > roof by vulnerability to blunt trauma is added here from general medical knowledge — tagged as not taken from the course material.

## Q15 (p.259) key B
